01 — The defect nobody photographs

The lead that survives wave soldering is the failure you ship

Wave soldering does its job: it fills the barrel and forms the fillet. What it cannot do is decide how much lead should remain above the joint. That decision is left to whoever is holding the cutters — and that is where the variability, the rework and the EMC failures start.

01

Long leads are not cosmetic

A lead tail that stands proud of the joint becomes an antenna, a short-circuit path against a neighbouring pad, and a mechanical lever that loads the solder fillet every time the board flexes. In a high-voltage or high-frequency assembly the tail length is a design parameter, not a finishing detail.

02

Hand cutting does not repeat

Two operators with the same flush cutter on the same board will produce two different protrusion distributions, and the same operator will drift across a shift. Without a repeatable cut there is no protrusion specification you can hold, no tolerance you can put in a process sheet, and no way to defend an inspection result.

03

Crushing is not cutting

A cutter that pinches the lead before it shears transfers that force into the solder joint. The joint that looked perfect at AOI now carries a hairline crack that opens under thermal cycling. Shear geometry — two edges passing each other — is what separates a clean cut from a damaged joint.

04

The swarf has to go somewhere

Thousands of small metal offcuts per shift land on the fixture, in the conveyor, and inside the product if nobody removes them. Loose lead trimmings are a foreign-object defect and a short-circuit risk in their own right.

05

Labour is the smallest part of the cost

The operator's time is easy to count. What is harder to count is the rework loop behind every inconsistent cut, the inspection time spent checking protrusion by eye, and the customer return that traces back to a joint nobody thought was at risk.

06

Automation only pays if it is set up once

A cutting machine that needs a programmer at the line for every product change is not automation, it is a different bottleneck. The value comes from a stored program, a fixture that adjusts, and an alignment routine that absorbs the position error of the board in front of it.

02 — The cut

Shear, not pinch: why the cutter geometry decides whether the joint survives

The customer presentation that started this page makes the point in one line: the cutting edges are deliberately offset, so the lead is sliced rather than squeezed. That single design decision is what separates a lead cutter from a pair of pliers.

Concept General teaching illustration of the two cutting modes. It is not a drawing of the S-430AC cutter head. The customer presentation states the design intent — offset cutting edges, reducing twisting and pull damage on the solder joint — and shows a reference photo of the shear-flush cutter.

Costs you money The pinch cut

Force has to go somewhere. If the edges compress the lead before it separates, part of that force arrives at the solder fillet as a peel load. The joint may still pass a visual inspection and still fail later. The burr left on the lead end also catches on test fixtures and handling trays.

What you want The by-pass shear cut

Two sharp edges pass in offset planes and slice the metal in tension-shear. The lead separates with a small, flat, repeatable face and almost no load reaches the joint. This is the geometry the customer presentation identifies, and the reason the design note says the offset matters.

03 — The machine

What is actually inside an automatic lead cutter

Strip away the enclosure and the S-430AC is four axes, a fixture, a teach pendant and a vacuum. Understanding those five things tells you whether the machine will fit your product — long before you discuss price.

X

Left and right

Carries the cutter head across the board. The manual states the X axis controls the scissors moving left and right.

Y

Forward and back

Drives the precision fixture, so the PCBA travels under the head. Fixture width is adjusted to suit the product.

Z

Up and down

Controls the cutter head height. Each instruction carries its own back-cutting height and scissor open/close delays.

R

Shear angle

Sets the shearing angle of the scissors and the centring position of the knife edge — the setting that decides cut quality.

Why we do not publish a boards-per-hour number

A lead cutter's output is dominated by two product-specific variables: how many leads are on the board, and how far apart they are. A board with 90 well-spaced leads and a board with 900 dense leads use the same machine and produce outputs that differ by an order of magnitude. Any single figure would be true for one customer and misleading for everyone else.

Send us a board file or even a photograph of the solder side. We will count the leads, estimate the path, and give you a range — with the assumptions written down so you can check them.

ARTICLE 01The 1.3 Millimetres That Decide Whether Your Board Passes EMC

A lead tail that stands proud of the joint is an antenna, a lever and a short-circuit path at the same time. Here is the measurement nobody puts in the process sheet — and the method that puts it there.

Articles are marketing commentary. Machine facts quoted inside them come from the S-430AC User Instruction Manual, the customer presentation or the key-components list.

Pick up a board that has just come off the wave. Turn it over and look at the solder side, not the component side. The joints look right — bright fillets, filled barrels. Now look at the tails. Every one of them is a different length. The one under the transformer is longer than the one beside the connector. The one the operator reached last is shorter than the one they reached first.

That board will pass AOI. It may pass ICT. And in eighteen months, in a cabinet in a humid warehouse, one of those tails will be the reason a unit comes back.

The tail is not a finishing detail. It is a design parameter that nobody specified.

Three things scale with tail length, and all three are things your customer measures. First, coupling: a tail standing off the board couples to whatever runs beside it, and at high frequency a few millimetres of stub is enough to shift an impedance and put an emission over the limit. Second, mechanical load: the tail is a lever, and every flex of the board puts a peel stress on the fillet that the joint was not designed to carry. Third, clearance: a tail that grows past the coating line, the test fixture or the housing becomes an interference and an assembly defect.

None of this shows up as a soldering defect, because the soldering was fine. That is why the problem survives every inspection gate you have.

So run the audit. It takes one board and one gauge.

Take a soldered board. Measure the tail length at twenty leads spread across the board — corners, centre, under the tall parts, next to the connectors. Write the twenty numbers down. You now have a distribution instead of an impression, and the distribution tells you which of three situations you are in.

If the twenty numbers are tightly clustered and inside the tolerance your customer's drawing already specifies, you have a process, and you should document it and stop worrying. If they are scattered but all short, you have a process that is holding by luck, and the first new operator will break it. If they are scattered and some are long, you do not have a process at all — you have a habit.

The second and third cases are the ones worth money. Scatter is the signal, and scatter has a cause: hand cutting. Two people with the same flush cutter, or one person across a shift, cannot produce a tight distribution, because the cut is a judgement rather than a setting.

What replaces the judgement is a number you can dial.

The S-430AC is built around exactly that idea. Each action instruction in the program carries its own scissor closing delay, scissor opening delay and back-cutting height. That means the cut is not one global setting — it is a parameter per lead position, stored in the program and repeated identically on every board. The R axis sets the shearing angle and the centring position of the knife edge, which is the setting that decides whether the lead separates cleanly or gets torn. And because the cut timing precision is 1 ms, the delay you dialled is the delay you get.

The reference figure in the customer's own presentation — cut lead protrusion under 1.3 mm, on a 152 mm panel — is the target they were working to. We quote it as a reference, not as a guaranteed tolerance, because the manual does not publish a protrusion spec and the number that matters is the one you measure on your board with your gauge.

Then do the audit again in three months.

This is the part people skip, and it is the part that keeps the benefit. A cut-off sample proves the machine can hit the number once. A quarterly re-audit proves the process still holds after the new flux, the new supplier, the new board revision and the new operator. The first audit finds the problem; the second finds the change that quietly reintroduced it. Factories that re-audit catch escapes while they are still a trend line on a chart, not a warranty claim in an inbox.

Keep the twenty numbers. Chart them. When a point moves, you know where to look, and you know it weeks before the customer does.

ARTICLE 02Your Operator's Wrist Is the Most Expensive Tool on the THT Line

Hand trimming looks free because the cost is already in the payroll. Count the hours properly and the bench is usually the largest single labour item after insertion.

Articles are marketing commentary. Machine facts quoted inside them come from the S-430AC User Instruction Manual, the customer presentation or the key-components list.

Walk onto a THT line and find the person with the cutters. Ask them how many boards they finished today. Then watch them for ten minutes without saying anything. That ten minutes will tell you more about your real lead-trimming cost than any quote you have received.

What you will see is a rhythm with a lot of gaps in it. Reach, position, close, release, drop the offcut, reach again. The cutters are only closing for a fraction of the time. The rest is the hand travelling, the eye locating, and the operator deciding — every single lead — how much to take off.

The bench does not look expensive because nobody invoices you for it.

Labour is the most visible cost and the least examined. It is already in the payroll, so it feels like a constant rather than a decision. But a trim bench is not one cost. It is the operator's hours, the second operator you added when volume grew, the inspection time spent checking protrusion by eye, and the supervisor's time on the days when a batch goes out wrong.

Then there is the cost that never appears in any account: the product you did not take. Every hour of manual trimming is an hour of capacity that cannot be sold. If your trim bench is the constraint, your revenue is capped by a pair of cutters.

Build the number in four lines, not four pages.

One: how many people spend the majority of their shift trimming leads? Two: what does one of them cost you per month, fully loaded? Three: what share of their trimming hours would a machine actually remove — remembering that loading, unloading and inspection stay with the operator? Four: how many months of that released cost equals the machine's delivered price?

That fourth number is the one that decides the project, and it is arithmetic you can do on the back of a drawing. The calculator on this page does exactly this, with your figures in your currency, and it runs in your browser so nothing is sent anywhere.

Two warnings on the method. First, do not use the gross wage — use the loaded cost, because on-costs are real. Second, do not claim 100 % of the hours. A machine that removes 70 % of trimming labour and keeps the operator employed on loading, inspection and changeover is a realistic outcome; a machine that removes all of it is a slide from a supplier who has never stood at your bench.

The machine's contribution is a repeatable cut, and the labour saving is a side effect.

It is worth being clear about the order of benefits, because it changes how you justify the purchase. The S-430AC's core output is a cut that is the same on board one and board ten thousand: a program per product, per-instruction delays and heights, an R axis that fixes the shear geometry, and double mark point alignment that absorbs the position error of the board in front of it. It stores hundreds of programs of up to 8,000 instructions each, so the product you ran last quarter comes back without reprogramming.

The labour released is what falls out of that. And it is genuinely released — the operator is not standing at the machine watching it cut, because there is nothing to watch. That is the difference between automating a task and automating a job.

Revisit the number every time your volume changes.

The payback calculation is not a one-off exercise. It moves with your wage rates, your order book and your product mix. The factory that ran the numbers two years ago and decided the bench was cheaper should run them again — because the bench got more expensive and the machine did not. Put the four-line model in a spreadsheet, update it annually, and let the answer change when it changes.

ARTICLE 03Flush Cutters Twist — And That Is Why Your Solder Joints Crack Later

The joint you are damaging does not fail at the bench. It fails after thermal cycling, in the field, and the failure analysis starts with a cutter nobody suspected.

Articles are marketing commentary. Machine facts quoted inside them come from the S-430AC User Instruction Manual, the customer presentation or the key-components list.

Take a pair of flush cutters and cut a lead on a scrap board. Now watch the joint under magnification while someone else cuts the next one. You will see the lead move before it separates — a small rotation, a small pull. The cutters are not slicing the lead. They are holding it still while the edges close on it, and the difference between those two things is where your cracked joints come from.

Nothing looks wrong afterwards. That is the whole problem.

The force has to go somewhere, and the solder is the softest thing in the chain.

A cutter is two wedges. When the edges meet in the same plane, the lead is compressed before it shears, and the compression travels down the lead into the fillet. Solder is the weakest element in that path, so it deforms. What you get is a joint that is metallurgically sound — it passed the wave, the fillet is the right shape — but mechanically pre-loaded. It has already spent part of its fatigue life before the board is even tested.

Then the product goes into service. Every power cycle expands the board and the lead at different rates. Every thermal cycle works that pre-loaded fillet. The crack starts at the toe of the fillet, propagates under the intermetallic, and the unit fails months later in a way that no incoming inspection could have predicted.

And the reason it is hard to chase is that the evidence was destroyed at the bench. There is no alarm, no scrap bin, no defect code. There is just a board that left the line looking perfect.

The framework is simple: separate in shear, never in compression.

Cutting edges should pass each other in offset planes, so the metal separates under shear stress rather than compression. The lead is sliced, not squeezed. The load path into the joint is almost eliminated, the cut face is small and flat, and the burr that would catch on handling trays is not there.

This is exactly the point the customer's own presentation makes. It shows a shear-flush cutter and explains that the cutting edges are deliberately offset — the design note in the source reads "offset cutting edges, reduce twisting, pull damage on the solder joint." Three settings decide whether you get that behaviour in production: how sharp the edges are, whether the two edges are correctly offset, and whether the lead is fully supported at the moment of the cut.

That third one is the setting people forget. If the board moves while the cutter closes, the geometry is irrelevant — you get a torn cut and a loaded joint. The workpiece has to be held.

In a machine, those three settings become machine parameters instead of operator habits.

The S-430AC addresses each one directly. The cutter group's R axis adjusts the shearing angle and the centring position of the knife edge — that is the offset geometry, dialled rather than assumed. The precision fixture clamps the PCBA and holds it through the cut, and the pressing cylinder is one of the mapped output channels in the I/O table. The board-in-place sensor on input channel 1 confirms the workpiece is presented before anything closes.

Around those mechanics sits the control layer: per-instruction scissor closing delay, opening delay and back-cutting height, so the dwell and the approach are tuned per lead position rather than guessed. Cut timing precision is 1 ms. The cutter body and head are listed as consumables, which matters more than it sounds — a dull edge is a compression cutter no matter how good its geometry was when it was new.

The customer's presentation labels its cutter photograph "for reference only". We keep that label. What is confirmed is the design intent and the fact that the machine exposes the parameters to control it; the specific cutter geometry on your build is confirmed at order.

Make cutter condition a schedule, not a symptom.

The failure mode of a cutting edge is gradual, which is why it catches people out. The cut is fine, then it is slightly less fine, then the joints start to show tearing and everybody blames the board. Put the cutter in the maintenance plan as a wear item with a check interval you set from your own cut samples. The manual's maintenance section already puts a cutter check in the operator's daily scope, alongside the needle, the air pressure and the pneumatic sealing.

Keep a reference sample: one board cut on a fresh cutter, photographed at magnification. Compare against it monthly. When the cut face stops matching the reference, change the cutter before the joints tell you.

ARTICLE 04The Lead Cutter Is Not the Bottleneck. The Fixture Is.

Most lead-cutting projects fail at changeover, not at cutting. A machine that cuts beautifully but takes an hour to set up will be bypassed by the second week.

Articles are marketing commentary. Machine facts quoted inside them come from the S-430AC User Instruction Manual, the customer presentation or the key-components list.

Imagine the machine has arrived. It is installed, levelled and running. The first product goes through, the cuts are clean, the protrusion is consistent, everybody is pleased. Now change to the next product.

That is where you find out whether you bought automation or a very expensive single-product fixture.

The cut takes seconds. The setup takes whatever you let it take.

Think about the sequence a product change actually requires. The board is a different size, so the fixture has to be adjusted. The lead positions are different, so a different program has to be recalled. The board may sit at a different height or a different angle, so the mark points have to be re-aligned. Then you run one board, measure the protrusion, and adjust.

Only one of those steps is the machine's cutting cycle. The others are setup, and setup is where the hours go. A line running high-mix, low-volume work can spend more time changing over than cutting, and at that point the machine's cut quality is irrelevant — it will be sitting idle while the operator uses the hand cutters to keep the line moving.

This is why we ask about your product mix before we talk about anything else.

The framework: score a machine on changeover, then on cutting.

Ask four questions about any lead-cutting machine, in this order. How is the fixture adjusted, and does it need tools? How many programs can be stored, and how are they recalled? How is the board aligned to the program? And what happens when the next board in the batch is slightly out of position?

The answers tell you the real changeover time far more accurately than any cycle-time figure. A machine that stores hundreds of programs and adjusts its fixture by hand in a minute will beat a faster machine that needs an engineer for every product change.

On the S-430AC, three of the four answers are published.

The fixture: the manual states the precision fixture clamps different PCBA products and that the width can be easily adjusted — deliberately described as easy, because that is the design goal. The programs: hundreds of processing files can be stored on the flash chip, each supporting 8,000 instructions, recalled from the handheld box when the product comes back. That is the difference between a product you set up once and a product you set up every time.

The alignment is the interesting one. The machine uses double mark point alignment: two mark points are added to the instruction list, chosen far apart on the fixture, and the operator moves the needle to each in turn. The manual explains the purpose plainly — double mark point positioning corrects the error caused by the angle and position deviation of the workpiece. In practice that is what makes a stored program reusable: the board can be a little crooked or a little off-position and the machine finds it instead of cutting in the wrong place.

And programming does not need an engineer at a PC. The handheld box has a 3.2-inch colour screen and an all-English interface for program design, parameter modification and machine monitoring, and it can be moved between machines of the same type — with the important restriction, stated in the manual, that it must only be plugged or unplugged with the power off. For complex products, the PC-side software imports DXF and PLT files and runs automatic path optimisation, then downloads over the serial port. The controller then runs standalone.

Then re-measure changeover every quarter.

Changeover time drifts. Someone finds a shortcut, someone else documents it, and the shortcut becomes the method. Six months later the "easy" fixture adjustment has become a two-person job with a mallet. Put a stopwatch on one product change per quarter and write the number down. If it has grown, find out why before it becomes the reason the machine is idle.

Keep a one-page setup sheet per product: fixture setting, program name, mark point method, the protrusion you measured, and any quirk. That sheet is what makes a new operator productive on a product they have never run.

ARTICLE 05After Wave Soldering, Every Board Has the Same Problem

The wave does its job perfectly and leaves you a defect anyway. Understanding what the process can and cannot control tells you exactly where the lead cutter belongs.

Articles are marketing commentary. Machine facts quoted inside them come from the S-430AC User Instruction Manual, the customer presentation or the key-components list.

Watch a board come out of the wave. It crosses the solder, the barrels fill, the fillets form, and it leaves the machine looking finished. In one sense it is finished — the electrical connection is made and the mechanical bond is formed.

In another sense the board is only half done, and the half that is missing is the half nobody in the soldering department owns.

The wave can fill a barrel. It cannot decide how much lead should remain above it.

This is a genuine boundary of the process, not a criticism of it. The wave's job is to deliver molten solder to the joint and let surface tension form the fillet. The length of the lead above the fillet was decided much earlier — when the component was inserted, and when the lead was formed. The wave inherits whatever the insertion station gave it.

So the tail length is set upstream and left unresolved downstream. The insertion station is measured on placement accuracy and speed. The wave is measured on fill and defects. Nobody's metric includes tail length, which is precisely why it drifts.

The consequence is that the trimming operation gets orphaned. It is not insertion, so the insertion team does not own it. It is not soldering, so the wave team does not own it. It becomes a bench at the end of the line with a person at it, and a person at a bench is not a process.

The framework: name the station, then place it in the sequence.

If you accept that trimming is a process, it needs a position. The correct position is after the solder has solidified and the board has cooled, and before electrical test and before any conformal coating. After soldering, because the lead is then held rigidly and will not shift while being cut. Before test, because the fixture clearance in ICT depends on the tail. Before coating, because a tail that pierces the coating film is a coating defect as well as a mechanical one.

In many lines that means: wave, cooling conveyor, lead cutter, then test. In others the cutter is loaded and unloaded manually, and in others again it is shared between two lines. All three are valid — what matters is that the position is written down and the operation has an owner.

Giving the station a machine gives it an owner.

The S-430AC is designed for exactly that slot. It is a 3-axis platform — X carrying the cutter across the board, Y moving the fixture and PCBA under the head, Z setting the cutter height — with an R axis that adjusts the shearing angle and knife-edge centring. The precision fixture clamps the board; the board-in-place sensor on input channel 1 confirms it is presented; the pressing cylinder holds it during the cut. The cut lead is drawn into the pin collecting cup by vacuum in real time, and the cup rotates out for emptying — which is what stops the trimming operation from becoming a foreign-object problem.

Because the machine is programmed per product and stores hundreds of programs, the trimming station stops being a person's judgement and becomes a named process step with a stored recipe. That is the change that makes it auditable.

For higher volumes there is an inline variant in the same product family, the SF320AT inline auto selective lead cutter, documented on the same server. Which one suits you depends on how the board arrives at the station — manual load, magazine, or conveyor.

Then keep the station honest with a periodic check.

A named process step needs evidence that it is still performing. Once a quarter, pull a board from the line, measure twenty tails, and compare the distribution to the one you recorded at commissioning. The comparison is the whole point: it catches a worn cutter, a drifting fixture, or a program that someone edited, long before any of them reach a customer. The manual's own fault table already gives you the first checks for the symptoms that matter — irregular shearing points at cutter sharpness, air pressure and the shear close/open times.

ARTICLE 06Why Hand-Cut Leads Can Never Hold a Tolerance

You cannot write a tolerance for a judgement. Here is the statistical reason the hand bench cannot be specified, and what has to change before it can be.

Articles are marketing commentary. Machine facts quoted inside them come from the S-430AC User Instruction Manual, the customer presentation or the key-components list.

Ask a quality engineer to write a tolerance for a hand-cut lead and watch what happens. They will write something — usually a generous number — and then they will tell you, honestly, that they cannot guarantee it. They are right. The problem is not the operator's skill. It is that the operation has no setting.

A tolerance is a promise about a distribution. To make that promise you need a process with a dial, not a person with a judgement.

Hand cutting has no controlled input.

Think about what actually determines where a hand cut lands. The operator's eye judges the tail against the joint. Their hand positions the cutter. Their grip decides how far the edges close before the lead separates. Their attention decides whether they are consistent on lead four hundred as they were on lead four. Every one of those is a variable, and none of them is a number you can put on a setup sheet.

So the output has a wide distribution, and a wide distribution is expensive in three separate ways. It forces you to set the tolerance wide, which weakens the specification you can offer a customer. It means you have to inspect to catch the outliers, which costs time and still misses some. And it means your process capability index is a fiction, because there is no controlled input to compute it from.

The operator is not the problem. The absence of a setting is the problem.

The framework: a tolerance needs a dial, a program and a feedback loop.

Three things have to exist before a tolerance means anything. A dial: some parameter that determines the outcome and can be set to a value. A program: a stored record of that value for this product, so the same result can be reproduced next quarter. And a feedback loop: a measurement that tells you whether the output still matches the setting.

Notice that none of these is "a faster cutter". Speed is not what makes a tolerance holdable. Repeatability is.

The S-430AC is built as a dial, a program and a loop.

The dial is the instruction parameters. Each action instruction carries its own scissor closing delay, scissor opening delay and back-cutting height, so the cut at each lead position is a set value rather than an estimate. The R axis sets the shearing angle and the centring position of the knife edge, which fixes the cut geometry. Cut timing precision is 1 ms, so the delay you dialled is the delay the machine executes.

The program is the storage layer. Programs are held on a flash chip, hundreds of files with up to 8,000 instructions each, and they can be copied between machines of the same type. That last point matters more than it looks: it means a proven program can be replicated across a fleet instead of re-taught on each machine.

The loop is the alignment and the measurement. Double mark point alignment corrects the angle and position deviation of the workpiece, so the program still lands correctly when the board is not perfectly placed. The parameters can be batch-modified by type, which is how you tune a whole product rather than editing eight hundred instructions by hand. And the pendant itself is the monitoring surface — the manual describes it as being for program design, parameter modification and monitoring equipment status.

The reference protrusion figure in the customer's presentation — under 1.3 mm on a 152 mm panel — is a target, not a published tolerance. Treat it as the starting point for your own capability study, not as a number you can print on a drawing without measuring.

Then run the capability study and re-run it on a schedule.

Cut thirty boards on the same program and measure the tail on the same twenty positions on each. That gives you a real distribution, a real mean and a real spread — and it gives you a capability index you can defend to a customer. Then repeat the study quarterly, and every time you change the cutter, the fixture or the program. The study is the feedback loop that turns a setting into a promise.

If the spread widens, you know before the customer does. That is the entire value of having a dial instead of a judgement.

ARTICLE 07Programming a Lead Cutter Without a PC on the Floor

If making a new program needs an engineer at a workstation, the machine will run out of programs and stop being used. Here is how offline programming actually works.

Articles are marketing commentary. Machine facts quoted inside them come from the S-430AC User Instruction Manual, the customer presentation or the key-components list.

Here is a failure mode nobody puts in the brochure. The machine is installed, the first three products are programmed, and everything works. Then a new board arrives. The person who built the first three programs has left, the programming workstation is in another building, and the machine sits idle for two weeks while somebody finds the time.

The machine was never the bottleneck. The programming route was.

Automation that needs a specialist is automation with a queue in front of it.

Every machine that requires a specialist to configure has the same structural weakness: its availability is limited by that specialist's calendar. This is true of lead cutters, dispensers, and every other programmable station on a line. The symptom is always the same — a capable machine running at a fraction of its potential, and an operator who has quietly gone back to the hand cutters because that is the only way to keep the line moving today.

The fix is not more training. The fix is a programming route that an operator can use for the simple cases and a specialist can use for the complex ones.

The framework: two routes, one destination.

You want a manual route for the boards you run occasionally and know well, and a data route for the complex boards with hundreds of leads. Both routes must end in the same place — a program stored on the machine's own controller, running offline, with no dependency on a PC being present on the floor.

Design the routes separately and the machine stays busy. Design only one and it will be idle half the time.

On the S-430AC, both routes are documented.

The manual route is the handheld box: a 3.2-inch 320 × 240 colour screen with an all-English interface, used for program design, parameter modification and machine monitoring. The manual's programming section walks through the sequence — new file, reset, name, save, then single points, path points, I/O on and off, and the per-point parameters such as opening time in advance, withdrawing height and output state. It is a teach-by-doing interface, which means an operator who knows the board can build the program by walking the machine around it.

The data route is the PC-side software. The manual states it supports importing DXF and PLT files with automatic path optimisation. So for a dense board, the path comes from the CAD data rather than from a person clicking hundreds of points. The result downloads to the controller over the serial port, and then the machine runs standalone and offline — which is the important part, because it means the machine does not need a computer next to it and does not stop when the network does.

There is one hardware rule worth knowing before you plan the workflow: the pendant can be moved between machines of the same type, but it must only be plugged or unplugged with the power off. The manual warns explicitly that doing otherwise damages the controller and the pendant. Plan the pendant's home position with that rule in mind.

Then treat the program library as an asset.

A program is not a setup artefact, it is intellectual property. The machine stores hundreds of files of up to 8,000 instructions on a flash chip, and programs can be copied between machines of the same type. That means you can build a library that outlives both the machine and the programmer.

Give each program a naming convention that ties it to the product revision, not just the product name. Keep a one-page setup sheet per product: program name, fixture setting, mark point method, the protrusion you measured, and the date. Review the library every quarter and delete the programs for products you no longer run — a library with stale entries is worse than a short one, because people stop trusting it.

ARTICLE 08When a Lead Cutter Actually Pays for Itself

Four conditions decide whether the project works. If two or more are missing, the machine will still cut beautifully and still be a mistake.

Articles are marketing commentary. Machine facts quoted inside them come from the S-430AC User Instruction Manual, the customer presentation or the key-components list.

We turn down lead-cutter projects. Not often, and never because the customer cannot pay — but because one of four conditions is missing, and a machine that arrives into the wrong situation does more damage to our reputation than a lost order ever would.

Here they are, so you can check them yourself before you talk to anyone.

Condition one: you actually have a separate trimming operation.

Some processes cut the lead as part of insertion — a clinch die trims and forms in the same stroke, and the board arrives at the wave with the leads already at length. If that is your process, you do not have a trimming operation to automate, and the project has no basis. Check before anything else: does a person or a station cut leads after soldering on your line, today?

Condition two: the scatter is costing you something measurable.

A hand bench with a tight, stable distribution and no customer complaints is not a problem worth solving. The case exists when the scatter shows up somewhere you can count: rework hours, re-inspection, field returns traced to joint damage, or a tolerance you cannot offer a customer because you cannot hold it. If you cannot name the cost, you are buying a machine to feel better about a process, and that is a weak reason.

Condition three: your product mix fits the changeover.

This is the one people underestimate. A machine is only as good as its setup time relative to how often you change products. Run three products a year and changeover barely matters. Run thirty products a month and a slow changeover will make the machine idle no matter how well it cuts. The S-430AC helps here — the precision fixture adjusts by hand, hundreds of programs are stored on the flash chip and recalled on demand, and double mark point alignment absorbs the position error of the board in front of it. But the answer depends on your mix, and you are the only one who knows it.

Condition four: the envelope fits your largest board.

Here we have to be straight with you. The operation manual does not publish a maximum board size, a stroke figure or a footprint. The customer's own presentation shows a 152 mm panel and a reference protrusion under 1.3 mm. The fixture width is described as easily adjustable. That is what the documents establish, and it is not enough to promise that the machine will take your largest board.

So the envelope is confirmed from the current general arrangement drawing against your board outline, before anything is ordered. If your largest board does not fit, none of the other three conditions matter.

What the machine does, when all four hold.

The cut becomes a program rather than a judgement: per-instruction scissor closing delay, opening delay and back-cutting height, an R axis fixing the shear angle and knife-edge centring, and 1 ms cut timing precision. The offcut goes into a vacuum collection cup instead of onto the fixture, which removes a foreign-object risk rather than adding one. Programs live on the machine, so the product you ran last quarter comes back without reprogramming. And the manual's I/O table, maintenance section and fault table give your technician a documented starting point for every routine task and every common symptom — six fault symptoms mapped to first checks, four daily maintenance checks, four consumables.

None of that is a promise about your board. It is a description of a machine, and the gap between the two is what the sample cut closes.

Re-run the four conditions annually.

Processes change. A clinch die gets added, a product mix shifts, a customer tightens a tolerance. The four conditions that failed last year may hold this year, and the ones that held may have gone. Put the four questions in your annual capital review and answer them fresh each time rather than inheriting last year's conclusion.
