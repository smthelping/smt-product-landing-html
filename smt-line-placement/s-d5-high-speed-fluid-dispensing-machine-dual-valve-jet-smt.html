01 — The real problem

Manual dispensing breaks down exactly where your yield does

Nobody loses money on the first hundred boards. The loss shows up at volume, in the four places a hand-held dispenser cannot hold a tolerance.

01

Dispensed volume drifts with the operator, not the program

A hand-held valve responds to how hard someone squeezes and how long they hold. Two operators, two glue weights, two reflow outcomes. A jet valve meters a fixed shot from a program instead.

02

Manual dots cannot follow an uneven surface

Where the substrate is not flat, a contact dispenser has to be re-taught for every board. Non-contact jetting fires the drop across a gap, so surface height stops being a variable you have to manage.

03

Programming is a stop-the-line job

When every new product means a technician standing at the bench with a teach pendant, changeover eats your capacity. On-line teaching and CAD import move that work off the line.

04

Nobody can tell you what the glue weighed

Without material-volume monitoring there is no data until the boards fail. Low-volume alarm and optional weight calibration turn an invisible process into a recorded one.

05

Air bubbles and satellite dots become scrap

A weak, long-stroke dispense scatters. A short, strong jet with a temperature-controlled flow path keeps the fluid consistent and the drop clean.

06

One lane is one board at a time

A single-lane conveyor caps throughput at the slowest station. A dual-lane conveyor with dual valves lets one machine carry two products or two operations at once.

07 — The valve assembly

Two valves, one head, synchronous or alternating

The dual-valve head carries a piezoelectric jet valve and a conductive silver paste valve side by side, with the vision camera and the laser height measurement on the same mounting plate. Synchronous mode fires both at once; alternating mode trades throughput for reach.

Non-contact, so no Z-axis travel

The valve fires across a gap rather than touching down. Removing the Z move is what lets the head hold speed on a surface that is not perfectly flat.

Temperature-controlled flow path

The nozzle and feed line are regulated together, so the fluid reaching the jet is the same viscosity it was when you calibrated the shot.

Short stroke, strong jet

A short, high-force stroke is what suppresses bubbles and satellite scatter compared with a slow, long-stroke dispense.

13 — Field notes

Eight notes from dispensing projects

Written for process engineers evaluating fluid dispensing. The articles are in English; the headings and summaries are translated with the rest of the page.

ARTICLE 01Your glue weight drifts between shifts, and it is not the operator

Dot weight moves because four physical things move. Find which one, and the fix is usually a setting rather than a person.

Article body in English. Headings and summaries follow the language you selected.

Two shifts, same program, same board, and the reflow result differs. The instinct is to blame the second operator. Most of the time the operator is the messenger, not the cause.

Start by separating the variables. Dot weight is set by four things: the fluid's viscosity at the moment it reaches the nozzle, the pressure behind it, the time the valve is open, and the gap between the nozzle and the board. Change any one and the shot weight changes.

Viscosity moves with temperature. A machine that has been running for four hours has a warmer valve body and a warmer feed line than one that started twenty minutes ago, so the same open-time produces a different weight. This is why a temperature-controlled flow path is not a luxury feature. If your dispenser has valve temperature regulators, the question is whether they are actually holding setpoint across the shift, not whether they exist.

Pressure moves with the shop's air supply. A compressor cycling on and off, or a second machine tapping the same line, shows up as weight scatter that looks random and is not. An air stabiliser on the machine removes the shop's air behaviour from your process. Log the line pressure at the machine for a week before you change anything else.

Open-time and gap are the two you control directly, and they interact. A longer open-time at a larger gap gives a similar weight to a shorter open-time at a smaller gap, but the shape of the dot is different: the long-shot version is more likely to leave a tail. Tune gap first, then time, and change only one at a time.

Here is the part that decides whether you can hold this: can you see the weight without weighing boards? Material volume monitoring watches the reservoir, which tells you when you are running out, not whether the last hundred shots were correct. A precision weight calibration system is the option that closes that gap, and it is the one to ask for if your product has a tolerance you cannot inspect downstream.

So before the next shift change, write down the four variables and check them in order: valve temperature at start and end of shift, air pressure at the machine, open-time, and gap. One of them is moving. The operator was never the problem.

ARTICLE 02How to size a dispensing envelope before you sign anything

Machine envelope, board envelope and dispense envelope are three different numbers. Mixing them up is how lines end up with a machine that cannot run the product that pays for it.

Article body in English. Headings and summaries follow the language you selected.

The most expensive mistake in buying a dispenser is sizing it on the biggest panel in the catalogue instead of the biggest panel you actually run. The second most expensive is sizing it on the biggest panel you run instead of the biggest dispense pattern on that panel.

There are three envelopes and they are not the same. The machine envelope is the floor space and the height. The board envelope is the largest panel the conveyor will carry. The dispense envelope is the area the valve can actually reach and dispense within, which is smaller than the board envelope because the head needs travel and the pattern has to stay inside the working area.

The specification sheet gives you numbers for each, and they are written in a shorthand that is easy to misread. On the S-D Series sheet, the single-lane dispensing range is quoted as several pairs of dimensions, because a single valve, two valves firing together, and two valves alternating each reach a different area. Four numbers, one row.

Work it backwards. Take your current product mix and write down the largest panel, the largest dispense pattern on that panel, and where that pattern sits. Then check whether the pattern fits the dispense envelope for the valve configuration you are buying, not just whether the panel fits the conveyor.

Then add the growth case, but only the real one. If you have a product in tooling that is 15 percent larger, size for it. If you have a customer who might send something larger one day, do not pay for that today. A machine sized 40 percent larger than your work runs slower on your work, because the gantry has more mass to move.

One more number people forget: the pattern has to fit twice if you are running two lanes. A dual-lane conveyor halves the usable width per lane. On the S-D5 the dual-lane simultaneous range is narrower than the single-lane range in the width direction, which is the whole point and also the whole constraint.

Before you sign, send the supplier your actual largest panel and your actual largest pattern, and ask them to confirm both fit the configuration quoted. A supplier who can answer that in writing is a supplier who has done the arithmetic. If the answer is 'it will be fine', ask again.

ARTICLE 03Non-contact jetting and needle dispensing are not the same tool

One touches down for every dot, the other fires across a gap. That single difference decides your speed, your maintenance and which products you can run at all.

Article body in English. Headings and summaries follow the language you selected.

A needle dispenser and a jet valve both put a controlled volume of fluid on a board. Everything else about them is different, and the difference starts with whether the nozzle touches the board.

A needle dispenser has to come down to the gap you set, dispense, and lift. The Z axis travels for every dot. That travel is time, and it is also a moving target: if the board is warped, the gap changes and so does the dot. To compensate you re-teach the height, or you add a height sensor, or you accept the variation.

A jet valve fires the drop across a gap without touching down. The Z axis does not move during dispensing, so the head keeps its speed and the gap can absorb surface variation. This is why jetting is described as non-contact and why the specification sheet lists it as eliminating Z-axis movement rather than as a nozzle type.

The second difference is maintenance. A needle has an orifice that clogs, a tip that wears, and a wetted path that needs cleaning on a schedule that depends on your fluid. A jet valve has a much smaller wetted path and an auto nozzle vacuum cleaning function on the machine. Fewer wear items, and the cleaning is a program rather than a shift task.

The third difference is the fluid window. Jet valves handle a wide viscosity range because the shot is formed by a mechanical impulse rather than by flow through an orifice. The S-D5 is rated from 0 to 250,000 CPS. At the thin end a needle drips; at the thick end a needle will not flow at all without pressure that also causes stringing.

Where a needle still wins: very large deposits, high-viscosity pastes that need a positive displacement screw, and applications where you need a continuous bead rather than discrete dots. If your process is one of those, say so early, because it changes which machine you should be looking at.

The practical test: take your fluid, your pattern and your cycle time, and ask what fraction of the cycle the Z axis would spend travelling if you used a needle. If it is more than a small percentage, the jet pays for itself in cycle time alone, before you count the scrap you stop producing.

ARTICLE 04Changing over to a new product without stopping the line

Every minute a dispenser is being programmed is a minute the line behind it is not producing. There are two ways to get that minute back, and they suit different shops.

Article body in English. Headings and summaries follow the language you selected.

Changeover is where a dispensing station earns or loses its place in the line. If a new product means a technician at the machine with a teach pendant for half a shift, the machine is not a line asset, it is a bench tool with a conveyor.

There are two routes to a new dispense program and they are not interchangeable. On-line teaching means you jog the head to each point and record it at the machine. CAD import means you bring the coordinates in from the product data. The S-D5 supports both, and the standard feature list names them together for that reason.

On-line teaching is faster when the dispense pattern is simple, when the fiducial positions are already known, or when you do not have usable CAD for the panel. It is also the only option when the pattern is defined by what the operator can see rather than by a drawing.

CAD import is faster when the panel data already exists and the pattern has many points. The work moves off the machine to an engineer with the drawing, and the machine only has to verify the fiducials and run. For a product with hundreds of dispense points, this is not a small difference.

Whichever route you use, the thing that decides your real changeover time is what happens after the first article. If the program is stored per product and can be recalled, the second order is a recall. If it is not, every order is a re-teach. Check that the control stores the program against a product identifier, and check how many you can hold.

Then close the loop on quality. The first article off a new program is a guess until something measures it. Vision locates the fiducials, but the dot itself needs a check: either an operator with a microscope, or the optional precision weight calibration, or a downstream inspection step that can reject. Choose one before you run production, not after.

A workable target for most shops: the first article verified and the program stored within one shift for a simple panel, and within a day for a dense pattern with CAD data available. If your changeover is longer than that, the bottleneck is the data, not the machine.

ARTICLE 05Bubbles, satellites and tails: reading a dispense defect

Three defects, three different causes, and only one of them is a machine problem. Here is how to tell them apart before you call anyone.

Article body in English. Headings and summaries follow the language you selected.

A bad dispense looks like a bad dispense. But bubbles, satellite dots and tails come from three different places, and treating them as one problem means changing three things at once and learning nothing.

Bubbles in the deposit usually come from air in the fluid path, not from the valve. They appear after a reservoir change, after the machine has been idle, or when the fluid was agitated before loading. Check the order of operations first: purge after every reservoir change, and let the fluid settle before you run. If bubbles persist with a freshly purged, settled reservoir, then look at the feed line for a leak on the suction side.

Satellite dots, the small strays around the main deposit, are a jetting problem. They happen when the drop breaks up in flight instead of arriving as one shot. The causes are a long stroke, a weak impulse, or a gap that is too large for the fluid's viscosity. The S-D5 deck describes the design intent directly: a short stroke and a strong jet force, which is what suppresses bubbles and scatter. If you are seeing satellites, reduce the gap first, then check the stroke setting.

Tails are a stringing problem, and they are almost always fluid-related. A fluid that is too viscous to break cleanly, or a nozzle that is too cold, will draw a thread from the nozzle to the board. This is the defect that a temperature-controlled flow path exists to prevent. Check the valve temperature against the fluid datasheet, not against what the last product needed.

The fourth thing to check is the nozzle, and it is the most common single cause of a dot that drifts without any of the above. A partially blocked nozzle produces a dot that is the right weight and the wrong shape. The S-D5 has auto nozzle vacuum cleaning as standard and an optional wiping device. Whatever your machine, put nozzle condition on a schedule rather than on a symptom.

Here is the discipline that makes this fast: change one variable, run ten boards, measure, then change the next. If you change gap, stroke and temperature together you will fix the defect and never know which one did it, which means you will meet it again on the next product.

Write down what you changed and what happened. A dispense defect log with five entries is worth more than a year of remembering, because the next engineer will meet the same three defects and will not have your memory.

ARTICLE 06When a second lane pays for itself, and when it does not

Dual lane doubles the board count per pass but halves the usable width. Run two numbers before you specify it.

Article body in English. Headings and summaries follow the language you selected.

A dual-lane conveyor sounds like free capacity. It is not free, and on some products it is not capacity at all. Two numbers decide it, and both come from your own panels.

The first number is width. A dual-lane machine splits the working width into two lanes, so the dispense envelope per lane is narrower than the single-lane envelope. On the S-D5 the dual-lane simultaneous range is roughly half the width of the single-lane range. If your panels are wider than a lane, dual lane is not an option for that product, regardless of what it would do for throughput.

The second number is dispense time against handling time. If a board spends most of its cycle being dispensed, two lanes with two valves genuinely doubles output, because both lanes dispense at once. If a board spends most of its cycle being loaded, clamped and unloaded, the second lane only helps if the handling overlaps with the dispensing. Check which of those describes your product before you assume.

There is a third case worth naming: two different products, one machine. Two lanes can run two part numbers at the same time, which is useful for small batches where the changeover cost is the real problem rather than the cycle time. If your shop runs many short orders, this is the strongest argument for dual lane.

Against that, dual lane costs more, takes more floor space, and adds a lane that has to be maintained and calibrated. It also changes the fixture design, because a narrower lane means a different edge support arrangement.

The test to run: take your top three products by volume. For each, write the panel width and the split between dispense time and handling time. If at least two of the three fit a lane width and are dispense-dominated, dual lane is worth quoting. If only one does, buy a single lane and spend the difference on the valve configuration instead.

One practical note. Dual-valve and dual-lane are separate options on the S-D5, and they can be specified independently. Two valves on one lane and one valve on two lanes solve different problems. Make sure the quotation says which one you are getting.

ARTICLE 07What traceability actually requires from a dispense station

MES integration is an option, not a solution. Decide what has to be recorded before you decide what to buy.

Article body in English. Headings and summaries follow the language you selected.

Traceability requests usually arrive as one sentence: we need the dispenser to talk to our MES. That sentence hides four decisions, and the machine specification only answers one of them.

The first decision is what identifies the board. A 2D code on the panel, a code on the carrier, or a serial number applied downstream. The S-D5 offers a camera 2D code scanner, an internal or external scanner, and a handheld barcode scanner, and the code sizes each supports are listed. Pick the reader after you pick the identifier, not before.

The second decision is what gets recorded. The minimum useful record is board identifier, timestamp, product program, and pass or fail. Anything less is a log that cannot answer a customer complaint. Anything more needs to be justified, because every extra field is something that can be wrong.

The third decision is where the record lives. If it stays on the machine, you have local history and no line-level view. If it goes to MES, you need the interface and someone on your side who owns it. The S-D5 lists MES (Industry 4.0) software as an option, which is the right place for it: it is a project, not a checkbox.

The fourth decision is what happens on a fail. A record that logs a bad board and lets it continue is worse than no record, because it proves you knew. Decide the reject action before you go live: mark, divert, or stop the line.

There is a version of this that does not need MES at all. If your requirement is internal process control rather than customer traceability, material volume monitoring plus a stored program per product plus a first-article check may be enough. Write down the actual requirement before you buy software you will not use.

Whichever route you take, get the interface agreed in writing before the order. The machine can supply the dispense record; your MES has to be able to receive it. That is a conversation between two engineers, and it is much cheaper before the machine ships than after.

ARTICLE 08The acceptance test to run before you sign for a dispenser

An acceptance test is not a demo. A demo shows the machine working; an acceptance test shows it working on your product, at your tolerance, with the numbers written down.

Article body in English. Headings and summaries follow the language you selected.

Most dispenser purchases go wrong not because the machine is bad but because nobody agreed in writing what good looks like. An acceptance test fixes that in one page, and it costs you a day.

Start with the sample. Send your actual panel, or a representative one, with the dispense pattern marked and the fluid you intend to use. A demo run on the supplier's standard board proves the machine works; it does not prove it works on yours, and those are different claims.

Then agree the parameters that will be measured. For a dispensing station the useful set is small: dot weight and its spread across a run of boards, dot position relative to the fiducial, cycle time for the pattern, and first-pass yield on the deposit. Four numbers, each with a tolerance you state.

Be explicit about the accuracy figures you are testing against, because the documents may not agree. On this machine the specification sheet quotes repeat position accuracy of ±15 µm at 4σ with CPK at or above 1.33, while the introduction deck and product sheet quote ±10 µm. Both are on the same page of this website for exactly that reason. Ask which figure applies to your configuration and write that one into the test.

Run the test long enough to see the process settle. A dispenser that is accurate for the first fifty shots and drifts over four hours has not passed, and a short demo will not reveal it. Ask for a run that spans a warm-up, and ask to see the weight data rather than the conclusion.

Record the environment alongside the result: air pressure at the machine, ambient temperature, valve temperature setpoint. If the machine passes in the supplier's air-conditioned bay and fails in your shop, those three numbers are how you find out why.

Finally, agree what happens if a number misses. A test with no failure clause is not a test. Either the machine is adjusted and re-run, or the tolerance is renegotiated with both parties agreeing the reason. What you are buying with all of this is not a guarantee that nothing will go wrong; it is a shared definition of correct, written down while everyone is still friendly.

16 — Questions we get asked

Before you request a quote

The answers below are drawn from the S-D5 documents. Where a question needs your product to answer properly, it says so instead of guessing.

Which fluids can the S-D5 dispense?
The S-D5 deck lists conductive silver paste, washable flux paste, die bonding solder paste, laser solder paste, stainless steel solder paste, and other special solder pastes and silver pastes for dispensing. Viscosity is rated from 0 to 250,000 CPS. Send us the datasheet for your fluid and we will confirm the valve and nozzle before you order.
Do I need one valve or two?
One valve if your product needs a single fluid at a single pattern. Two valves if you dispense two fluids, or one fluid at a rate the single head cannot reach. The dual-valve head can run both valves together or alternate between them. On the S-D5 the dual-valve configuration is an option, not the base machine.
Why is non-contact jetting better than a needle dispenser?
A needle dispenser has to touch down, so the Z axis travels for every dot and the result depends on the gap. A jet valve fires the drop across a gap with no Z movement, which holds speed and keeps the dot consistent on surfaces that are not perfectly flat. It also removes the needle from the maintenance list.
What board size can it actually handle?
The S-D Series specification sheet states a maximum of L510 × W650 mm for a single lane, and a minimum of L50 × W50 mm. The S-D5 deck states a support range of 350 × 550 × 40 mm. Those two do not agree, and section 10 says so. Send us your largest and smallest panel and we will confirm both ends against a real fixture.
Does it fit into an existing line?
Yes. The conveyor runs at 900 ± 30 mm transportation height with SMEMA in and out, and the direction can be set left-to-right or right-to-left at build. Edge distance is adjustable from 0 to 5 mm. If your line height differs, tell us and we will confirm the conveyor option.
How long does programming a new product take?
Two routes: on-line teaching at the machine, or importing a CAD file. Which is faster depends on whether your product data is already in CAD and how many dispense points it has. The industrial PC runs Windows 10 and the dispense program is stored per product, so a repeat order is a recall rather than a re-teach.
What does it need from my factory?
AC 220 V single phase at 10 A and 2.5 kW, and clean compressed air at 0.4 to 0.6 kgf/cm². Air preparation is included on the machine. Nothing else needs to be brought in.
Can it talk to our MES?
MES integration is an Industry 4.0 software option, and 2D code scanning is available as a camera scanner or a handheld. If you need the dispense record tied to a board serial number, tell us which MES and which identifier you use, and we will scope it before quoting rather than after delivery.
