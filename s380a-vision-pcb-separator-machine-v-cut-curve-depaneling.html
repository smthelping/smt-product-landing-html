01 — Why it matters

A V-score blade cuts one straight line. Most boards need more than that.

Separation is the last operation before the board ships, and it is usually the one nobody designed for. If the panel outline is anything other than a set of parallel straight scores — a curved LED module, a circular sensor board, an L-shaped cut-out for a connector block, a breakaway stamp-hole tab — the blade machine cannot do it. That is when the operation migrates back to the bench, to a hand tool and an operator, and stays there.

01

The shape decides the method, not the volume

A blade separator is the right answer for panels that are genuinely straight-line only — it is fast, cheap to run and simple to maintain. The moment the outline includes an arc, a circle or a closed cut-out, the blade stops being an option and the operation becomes a routing job. Choosing the machine before looking at the outline is how lines end up with both.

02

Hand-breaking is a yield loss you already accept

Breaking a scored panel by hand sends a shock wave along the board. Whether that damages a nearby MLCC or a solder joint depends on where the components sit and how hard the operator pulled — which means the defect rate moves with the operator, the shift and the day. A milled contour removes the shock entirely, and it does the same thing on the first board and the ten-thousandth.

03

Dust is the defect that shows up next week

Milling produces fibre-reinforced dust. If it lands on the panel it becomes a conductive contaminant, a solderability problem or a field failure that appears months after shipping. The S380A is supplied with a matched dust collector and bottom extraction as standard, because extraction is part of the cutting process, not an accessory added afterwards.

05 — Programming

From a photograph of the board to a cutting program

Traditional router programming means reading coordinates off a drawing and typing them in, then chasing the errors at the machine. The S380A inverts that: the camera images the real panel, and the programmer draws on the picture. The 2023 manual sets out the sequence below — this is the documented workflow, not a simplified retelling of it.

Step 01

Image the panel

The CCD system scans the whole board and builds the panorama you will program against. Everything that follows is drawn in this view, which is why a new product is programmed against the actual board rather than against a CAD file.

Step 02

Draw the cut on the picture

Straight lines and arcs are placed directly on the panorama. The cutter diameter has to match the line width used when drawing, otherwise the finished board will not measure what you intended — the manual calls this out explicitly.

Step 03

Teach the Mark points

Mark1 and Mark2 are set by coordinate and then learned from a template. The test value turning green is the confirmation that the learn succeeded; a red or out-of-range value means the pattern is not reliably recognisable and must be re-taught.

Step 04

Build sub-programs and array them

Each product gets a sub-template with its own Mark and cutting track. Sub-boards are then placed and arrayed by matrix — rows and columns — so a panel of many identical boards is programmed once and multiplied. Different products can run on the left and right tables.

Step 05

Set the speed and the depth

Cutting speed is set on the track line within the published 5–100 mm/s range. The manual's own rule for first-off runs: start slow, and slow down further as the cutter gets smaller, because an aggressive feed is what breaks a thin bit. Cutting depth is taught by lowering the head onto the board outside the fixture.

Step 06

Run it, watch it, correct it

The program runs under visual monitoring so the track can be watched on screen before and during the cut. If a line drifts, Mark matching realigns the program to the board, and individual tracks can be dragged, stretched or shortened rather than reprogrammed from scratch.

01

Why a V-score blade cannot cut a curved panel — and what to do instead

The RFQ arrives with a circular LED module on it. The blade machine in the corner has been separating panels for six years. This is the guide that explains why it is the wrong tool, and what the right one looks like.

+

Start with the failure

It usually shows up as a rejected panel rather than a stopped line. The operator scores a straight line, then a curved edge, and the acrylic LED module sitting 4 mm from the cut cracks at the corner of the cut-out. Or the panel comes apart, but the edge is a jagged line of delaminated glass fibre that will not fit the housing. Nobody calls that a machine problem. It gets logged as an operator problem, and the operator gets retrained. Then it happens again on the next shift.

What is actually happening

A V-cut separator works by wedging a straight blade into a V-shaped score until the remaining material fractures along the score. Two things follow from that geometry. First, the cut must be straight — a blade cannot follow an arc, so a curved outline simply cannot be produced. Second, the fracture releases stored energy into the panel as a shock wave, and the shock travels outward from the cut. Components and joints near the score absorb that energy.

Neither of those is a defect in the blade machine. It is what blade separation is. The problem is choosing it for a board whose outline it was never able to produce.

A method you can reuse

Before you choose a separation method, answer three questions about the panel, in this order. One: is every cut line straight and parallel? Two: is every cut open at the edge of the panel, or is any cut a closed contour inside the board? Three: how close is the nearest component to the cut? If the answer to question one or two is no, blade separation is off the table and the operation is a routing job. If the answer to question three is under roughly 5 mm, the shock argument applies even when the lines are straight.

Where the method becomes hardware

The S380A is the answer to the routing case. It mills the contour with a router bit between 0.8 and 3.0 mm, guided by a camera that images the whole panel, so an arc, a circle, an L-shaped recess or a closed cut-out is drawn on screen and then followed. Cutting stress is low because material is removed rather than fractured. The published contour list covers straight lines, arcs, circles, L-shaped and U-shaped panels, stamp-hole panels and V-slot panels — which means one machine can handle a mixed panel portfolio that used to need two.

How the gain compounds

The first benefit is the panel you could not make before. The second, less obvious one arrives about six months later: because programs are stored per product and arrayed by matrix, adding the next curved product to the line costs a fixture and an hour of programming rather than a new machine and a new supplier. The capability you bought for one board keeps paying out on the ones that follow it.

02

The true cost of hand-breaking PCB panels

Manual separation never appears on a cost sheet as a line item. It appears as labour, as scrap and as warranty returns — three places, none of which are added together.

+

Start with the failure

Two people at a bench, a bin of panels, a hand tool. It looks cheap because the tool cost nothing and the operators were already on the payroll. Then you count the scrap: 1.5 per cent of panels scrapped at separation, and half of those have no visible defect until the board reaches functional test. The bench has been quietly producing field failures for as long as it has existed.

What is actually happening

Hand separation is a variable process wearing the costume of a fixed one. The force applied depends on the operator's grip, the depth of the score, the temperature of the board and how much of the shift has passed. Micro-cracks in ceramic capacitors and hairline fractures in solder joints do not announce themselves at the bench — they fail at temperature cycling, in the field, months later. The cost lands in warranty and in the customer relationship, not in the separation cost centre.

A method you can reuse

Cost the bench in three layers, then add them. Layer one is direct labour: operators × fully loaded annual cost. Layer two is scrap at separation: scrap rate × annual panel volume × panel value. Layer three is the returns you can trace back to separation damage — the hardest to measure, and usually the largest. Once the three are on one page, the bench stops looking cheap and starts looking like a recurring cost with no asset behind it.

Where the method becomes hardware

A machine replaces layers one and two immediately and layer three over the following quarters. The S380A removes the manual force entirely: the contour is milled under program control at a feed you set between 5 and 100 mm/s, with the published repeat positioning accuracy of ±0.01 mm. The same program produces the same cut on the first board and the ten-thousandth, on the day shift and the night shift.

How the gain compounds

The labour saving is a one-time step change. The quality saving is not — it accumulates, because every month that passes without separation-induced field failures is a month of avoided returns, avoided freight and avoided credibility loss with the customer. That is the part of the payback that never appears in a spreadsheet prepared at the time of purchase.

03

Vision programming: from a board photograph to a cutting program in minutes

Coordinate programming is where router depaneling earned its reputation for being slow to set up. Drawing the cut on a picture of the actual board is what removed that objection.

+

Start with the failure

A new product arrives. An engineer takes the CAD file, reads coordinates, types them into the machine, runs a dry cycle, watches the head cut through the fixture instead of the board, goes back to the drawing, finds a sign error, and repeats. Six hours later the first good panel exists. On a line running forty products a year, that is a quarter of an engineer's time spent re-entering data that already existed in a picture.

What is actually happening

The bottleneck is not the machine, it is the translation step. A drawing is an abstraction; the panel in the fixture is the physical truth. Every error introduced when moving from one to the other has to be discovered at the machine, and discovery costs machine time and scrap. Removing the translation — programming directly against an image of the real panel — removes the entire class of error.

A method you can reuse

Program in six steps and do not skip the third. One: image the panel and build the panorama. Two: draw the cutting path as lines and arcs directly on that image, with the line width set to match the cutter diameter — the manual is explicit that a mismatch means the finished board will not measure what you intended. Three: teach Mark1 and Mark2 and confirm the test value turns green. Four: create a sub-template per product and array it by matrix rows and columns. Five: set the cutting feed and teach the depth outside the fixture. Six: run under visual monitoring and correct with Mark matching.

Where the method becomes hardware

This is what the S380A's CCD whole-board visual programming system does. The camera scans the board, the programmer draws on the result, and the Mark points are learned from a template rather than calculated. Because both Y tables carry independent programs, the same workflow covers running two different products simultaneously — left and right — which is how a depaneling machine keeps up with a mixed-model line.

How the gain compounds

Programs persist. The second time a product runs, the setup is a fixture change and a recall, not a programming session. Over a year the saved engineering hours are real but the more valuable effect is responsiveness: when a customer pulls an urgent order forward, the line can answer yes, because bringing a product back online takes an hour rather than a day.

04

Dust: the depaneling defect nobody measures

Milling fibre-reinforced board produces a conductive contaminant. Where it goes decides whether it becomes a defect now, later, or never.

+

Start with the failure

The line passes AOI, passes functional test, ships. Eleven weeks later a board comes back from the field with intermittent behaviour that nobody can reproduce on the bench. It gets logged as an unexplained failure and written off. What actually happened is that a few milligrams of glass-fibre dust settled on a connector or bridged two pads during separation, and it took a temperature cycle and a humidity cycle for it to matter.

What is actually happening

Routing removes material, and that material has to go somewhere. FR-4 dust is abrasive, mildly conductive when it holds moisture, and small enough to travel. Two failure modes follow: it lands on the panel as contamination, or it accumulates inside the machine and degrades the extraction that was supposed to be removing it. The second is the more common one, because the first symptom of a saturated filter bag is not a visible defect — it is a slow decline in how much dust the machine captures at all.

A method you can reuse

Treat extraction as a cutting parameter, not as housekeeping. Specify the extraction at the cutting point rather than at the cabinet. Put the filter bag, the collection box and the drawer on a schedule with named owners and record it — the manual's intervals are weekly for the collection box, monthly for the bag, quarterly for the electrical box filter. Then verify: if extraction performance has fallen, check the duct from the cutting port to the inlet for a leak before you blame the fan.

Where the method becomes hardware

The S380A is supplied with a matched dust collector rather than leaving you to source one. Published dust collector size is 665 × 635 × 1610 mm at 155 kg, powered at AC380V 3HP against the separator's AC220V 50/60Hz 1HP. Dust collection is from the bottom as standard, with top collection available as an option. The manual documents the interlock between the two machines, so a collector that is not running is a fault the machine reports rather than a condition it ignores.

How the gain compounds

Clean separation means fewer unexplained field returns, and unexplained returns are the most expensive kind because they consume engineering time as well as freight and goodwill. It also means the machine itself stays accurate for longer: the same dust that contaminates a panel abrades a guide rail, and rail lubrication is on a quarterly schedule for a reason.

What to do next

Ask your current separation supplier what happens to the dust, and where it is captured. If the answer is a vacuum hose attached to a shop vacuum, ask how much of it is captured at the cutting point. Then compare that with the extraction arrangement in the maintenance section of this page.

05

Tool breakage is a scheduling problem, not an accident

A snapped router bit stops a line and costs a cutter. The reason it snapped is almost always a parameter somebody set once and never revisited.

+

Start with the failure

The head is part way through a panel when the spindle note changes and the cut stops. The bit is gone. The panel is scrap. The operator changes the tool, reloads, and runs the rest of the batch at a feed rate set slow enough to feel safe. Output drops for a week and nobody connects the two events, because the broken bit was treated as the event.

What is actually happening

Cutting feed and cutter diameter are coupled, and the 2023 manual states the rule directly: the smaller the milling cutter, the lower the speed should be set, because a feed that is too fast is what causes breakage. A 3.0 mm cutter and a 0.8 mm cutter cannot share a feed rate and both survive. Add tool wear and the picture gets worse — a worn bit cuts hotter and loads the spindle harder, so the last ten per cent of a tool's life is the part most likely to break it.

A method you can reuse

Build a feed table instead of a feed setting. One row per cutter diameter in your inventory, one column per material and thickness, and a starting value taken from the low end of the published 5–100 mm/s range for the smallest cutter you run. Raise the feed for a product only after a full batch runs clean. Write the table down and keep it at the machine; a parameter that lives only in an operator's memory dies when that operator changes shift.

Where the method becomes hardware

The S380A supports the method in two ways. First, tool monitoring covers automatic tool sinking, tool breaking and sliding tool detection, so a broken or slipping tool is detected as a condition rather than discovered as a scrapped panel. Second, automatic Z-axis tool-height correction after a tool change means a new bit does not need a manual height re-teach — which matters, because a height error is the other common way a fresh cutter dies young. Automatic tool change is documented as the Q380A configuration, so confirm it for your build.

How the gain compounds

Two effects. Tool cost falls, which is minor. Uptime rises, which is not — a line that stops twice a month for twenty minutes has lost less time than it has lost rhythm, and rhythm is what keeps a small batch schedule honest. The third effect is that your operators stop setting parameters defensively, and defensive parameters are the main reason a capable machine underperforms its specification.

Dual-table depaneling: the throughput you get for free

Most of the cycle time on a single-table router is the operator loading and unloading. The second table is what removes it.

+

Start with the failure

Watch a single-table machine for ten minutes. The spindle cuts for maybe four of them. The rest is the operator lifting a finished panel out, placing a new one, closing the door and pressing start. The machine's rated capability is not the constraint on your output — the door is.

What is actually happening

Loading and cutting are serialised when they share one table. Each operation waits for the other, so the machine's cutting time and the operator's handling time add together instead of overlapping. If cutting takes 40 seconds and handling takes 25, the cycle is 65 seconds — and 38 per cent of your machine's capacity is spent doing nothing while a human moves a board.

A method you can reuse

When you specify a depaneling machine, ask for the cycle as two separate numbers: cutting time and handling time. Then ask what fraction of handling time can be overlapped with cutting. Any machine that cannot answer the second question is a single-table machine with extra steps, whatever the brochure says. The design you want is the one where the operator's job is to keep a table supplied, not to wait for one.

Where the method becomes hardware

The S380A is a dual-table machine on independent Y1 and Y2 axes. The documented behaviour is that cutting and board placement proceed at the same time, so while one table is under the spindle the other is being unloaded and reloaded. The published specification lists the work table simply as "dual table", and the manual adds the detail that matters commercially: the left and right tables can cut two different products at the same time.

How the gain compounds

Running two products simultaneously is the larger effect for a high-mix plant. It means a small urgent order does not have to wait for a large batch to finish — it runs on the other table. That changes how you schedule, not just how fast you cut, and scheduling flexibility is what lets a small plant accept orders a larger competitor has to decline.

07

Router or blade? A decision rule for a mixed board portfolio

Most plants do not have one kind of panel. They have a mix, and the mix is what makes the machine decision hard. Here is a rule that survives contact with a real product list.

+

Start with the failure

The plant buys a blade separator because most panels are straight-line. Two years later, four of the twenty products on the line have curved outlines, and those four are hand-separated at a bench with a rotary tool. The machine that was bought to remove manual separation created a manual separation island, because it was chosen against the average panel rather than against the whole portfolio.

What is actually happening

Blade separation optimises for one thing: speed on straight, open, parallel cuts. Router separation optimises for a different thing: any outline at all, at a slower feed. Those are not two points on one scale — they are two different capabilities. A portfolio containing even a small share of non-straight panels needs both capabilities present, and the question is whether you buy them as two machines or one.

A method you can reuse

Sort your product list into three buckets. Bucket A: every cut straight and open, no component within 5 mm of a cut, high volume. Bucket B: any curved, circular, L-shaped, U-shaped or closed cut-out. Bucket C: straight cuts but components close to them, or a low-volume mix that changes often. Bucket A belongs on a blade machine. Buckets B and C belong on a router. Then count the volume in each bucket — if B plus C is more than roughly a fifth of your panels, one router that also handles V-slots is usually the simpler answer than two machines and a hand bench.

Where the method becomes hardware

The S380A sits on the router side of that split and reaches into bucket A as well, because V-slot panels are on its published cutting capacity list. So the machine covers the entire list rather than part of it. The honest counter-argument is cycle time: for a genuinely straight-line high-volume panel, a blade machine will be faster per panel, and if that is all you make, buy the blade machine.

How the gain compounds

One machine that covers the portfolio means one fixture library, one program library, one maintenance schedule and one operator skill. That is a smaller gain than the capital saved, and a more durable one — every additional capability you add to a single platform costs a fixture, while every additional capability spread across a second platform costs a second set of everything.

What to ask a depaneling supplier before you sign

Ten questions, and the answers that should worry you. Written so you can use it as a checklist in the meeting.

+

Start with the failure

A machine is delivered, installed, and commissioned. It cuts well for eight months. Then a guide rail needs grease, a filter bag needs cleaning, a cutter needs a feed change and nobody knows the numbers. The supplier is responsive but distant, the spare part takes three weeks, and the machine that was going to remove a bench slowly becomes a bench with a spindle attached.

What is actually happening

Depaneling machines fail on service, not on specification. The specification is a printed page and it is easy to compare. What is hard to compare is spare-part lead time, whether the supplier manufactures the parts or resells them, whether a maintenance schedule comes with the machine, and whether the person who answers the phone has ever stood in front of one. Those are the things that decide year three.

A method you can reuse

Ask these ten, in writing, and keep the answers. One: what is the spare-part lead time, and which parts do you manufacture yourself? Two: does the machine ship with a maintenance schedule including intervals and lubricants? Three: what is the published repeat positioning accuracy, and separately, the cutting accuracy? Four: which tool diameters are supported, and what feed do you recommend for each? Five: is dust extraction supplied and interlocked, or sourced separately? Six: how is a new product programmed, and how long does it take? Seven: what happens to a program when a board revision shifts the Mark points? Eight: is automatic tool change included, or is it an option? Nine: what is the training, and who delivers it? Ten: give me two customers running a similar outline.

12 — FAQ

Answers to the questions this machine raises

These are the six questions that come up in almost every S380A enquiry. Where the two source documents disagree, the answer says so rather than picking one.

What PCB thickness and panel size can the S380A handle?
The published cutting thickness range is 0.3 to 3 mm and the published effective cutting size is 350 × 550 mm. The 2023 operation manual quotes a working size of L350 × W330 mm, which appears to describe one table rather than the whole envelope. Confirm which figure applies to your configuration before laying out the fixture.
What shapes can the S380A cut?
Straight lines, arcs, circles, L-shaped panels, U-shaped panels, stamp-hole panels and V-slot panels. The published machinable working area covers V groove, stamp hole, bridge and circle. Because the cut is milled with a router bit rather than pressed with a blade, the contour is not limited to a straight line.
How accurate is the S380A?
Published repeat positioning accuracy is ±0.01 mm. The 2023 operation manual additionally quotes a cutting accuracy of ±0.02 mm. Repeat positioning accuracy describes how consistently the machine returns to a commanded position; cutting accuracy describes the result on the board, which also depends on fixture rigidity, cutter condition and board support.
What is the difference between the S380A and a V-cut blade separator?
A V-cut blade separator presses a straight blade through a scored line, so it can only separate panels along straight, parallel V-scores. The S380A mills the contour with a 0.8 to 3.0 mm router bit under vision guidance, so it handles curved, circular, L-shaped, U-shaped and stamp-hole panels as well as V-slot panels. The trade-off is cycle time: blade separation is faster on boards that are genuinely straight-line only.
Does the S380A need a dust collector?
Yes. The machine is supplied with a matched dust collector. The published dust collector size is 665 × 635 × 1610 mm, weight 155 kg, powered at AC380V 3HP, with the separator at AC220V 50/60Hz 1HP. Dust collection is from the bottom as standard, with top collection available as an option.
What support and spare parts come with the machine?
Free installation and training, one-day lead time on spare parts, five days for a customised design, and 7 × 24 worldwide support. Southern Machinery designs and manufactures its own feeders, nozzles and grippers, which is what makes the one-day spare-part lead time possible.
