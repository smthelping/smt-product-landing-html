Insights

Eight things worth knowing before you quote a line

Written for the person who has to defend the capital request: what the problem actually costs, how to size it, and which machine answers it.

THT Auto Insertion

Your THT Line Is Paying a Tax Nobody Put on the Invoice

Hand insertion never appears as a line item. It appears as headcount, rework and a delivery date you quietly pad.

Where it starts hurting

Walk your through-hole bench at 10am. Count the hands. Now count them again at 10pm on the last day of the month. The second number is the one your cost model never sees, and it is the number that decides whether you win the next order or lose it on price.

What is actually going wrong

Manual insertion does not fail loudly. It drifts. A tired operator seats a radial 0.4 mm proud, wave solder bridges it, test catches it, rework fixes it, and nobody logs the four minutes. Multiply by a thousand boards and the defect rate you report is honest while the cost you carry is not. The problem is not the people. It is that a human is being asked to hold a tolerance that only a machine can hold shift after shift.

A method you can reuse

Audit it in three passes. First, count components by type — radial, axial, odd-form — not by board, because machines are bought per component family. Second, measure the real seconds per insertion at the bench, including pick and reposition, not the ideal. Third, price the rework loop separately: touch time plus the test re-run. You now have a cost per component that a machine quote can actually be compared against.

The equipment that carries it

That audit maps onto three machines. Taped radials go to the S3000 at up to 25,000 CPH. Axials go to the S4000 with 5 to 60 modular feeder stations. Everything that fits neither — connectors, relays, transformers, shielding cans — goes to the S7900 with two, four or six servo heads at 0.6 to 0.8 s per part. Loose LEDs skip taping entirely on the S3300 at 15,000 CPH.

Why it keeps paying

The machine pays once on labour and keeps paying on consistency. A fixed insertion depth means wave solder stops seeing variable standoff, which means the rework bench empties, which means your test yield stops arguing with your delivery date. The second line you build reuses the same programs, the same feeders and the same operator training, so the second payback is always faster than the first.

Your next step

Send us one board and its component list. We will tell you which of the four machines it needs, how many feeder stations, and what stays manual — including the honest answer that some boards do not justify automation yet.

Feeders & Feeding Systems

Odd-Form Parts Are Not the Problem. Your Matching Process Is.

Most odd-form automation projects do not fail at the machine. They fail at the moment somebody guesses which feeder a part needs.

Where it starts hurting

A customer sends a photograph of a relay and asks whether it can be automated. Three emails later nobody has mentioned lead pitch, and a quotation is already being written. That is how an odd-form project starts going wrong — not at installation, but in the first week.

What is actually going wrong

Odd-form parts resist automation because they arrive in every possible packaging and no standard geometry. Bulk in a bag, tube, tray, carrier tape, reel of terminals. The machine can handle almost all of it; what it cannot handle is ambiguity. If the body is 18 x 12 mm but the lead span is 7.5 mm and nobody wrote that down, the feeder specified will be wrong and the first anybody hears of it is at commissioning.

A method you can reuse

Replace the guess with five measurements taken once, in this order: packaging as received, body L x W x H, lead pitch, lead count and lead diameter. Those five numbers select the feeder. Then the pick face selects the nozzle. Then a free sample test confirms both before money moves. Measure once, decide twice, test before you buy — that sequence removes nearly every odd-form surprise.

The equipment that carries it

We built that sequence into a page. The odd-form feeder and nozzle matcher holds 76 real component lines from anonymised customer evaluations, each mapped to one of ten feeder models — SAF1001, SRF1001, STF1003, SBF1001, SBF2002, SLF1001, SJF1001, STP1001, SSPC101, SRT7001 — filterable by packaging, pitch, body size, lead count and diameter. Type your part's dimensions and it tells you what fits and what is out of envelope.

Why it keeps paying

The value compounds because the table is reusable. The next new product introduction starts from a mapping you already trust, your engineers stop re-litigating the same connector every quarter, and the feeders you already own get reused across programmes instead of being bought twice.

Your next step

Open the matcher, filter to your component class, and send the pre-filled feasibility request. If your part is not in the 76 lines, send the drawing and we will add the measurement and run a free sample test.

Nozzles & Grippers

The Nozzle Is 0.3% of the Machine and 60% of Your Throw Rate

You bought a placement head accurate to tens of microns and bolted a worn consumable onto the end of it.

Where it starts hurting

Your mounter says 0.05 mm. Your first-pass yield says otherwise. Before you call the machine supplier, look at the twelve centimetres of steel between the head and the component, because that is almost always where the argument ends.

What is actually going wrong

A nozzle fails in three quiet ways. The tip wears and the vacuum seal leaks, so the part rotates in flight. The internal bore clogs with flux residue and the pick becomes intermittent. Or the nozzle was never right for the part in the first place — a flat vacuum tip asked to hold a domed LED lens, or a standard tip asked to grip a connector that needs jaws. None of these throw an alarm. All of them show up as throw rate, and throw rate is read as a machine problem.

A method you can reuse

Treat nozzles as tooling, not as spares. Keep a nozzle register per programme: which part number, which nozzle type, how many picks since fitting. Clean on a cycle count rather than on complaint. Replace on measured wear rather than on failure. And whenever a part fights the standard range — odd geometry, delicate lens, no flat pick face — specify a custom tip instead of making the operator compensate.

The equipment that carries it

We machine nozzles for JUKI KE and FX series, FUJI NXT H01 to H24, Yamaha, Panasonic NPM and CM, Siemens and ASM SIPLACE, and Samsung heads — vacuum, slot, pipe and gripper types. Where the catalogue has nothing suitable, the custom nozzle and gripper service starts from your component drawing and produces a tip designed around the part rather than around the catalogue.

Why it keeps paying

Nozzle discipline compounds quietly. Lower throw rate means less component cost burned per shift, fewer unexplained placement defects reaching AOI, and a cleaner signal when something genuinely is wrong with the machine. Once a programme has a known-good nozzle set, every rebuild of that product starts at yield instead of climbing to it.

Your next step

Send the component drawing or the head model you run. We will tell you whether a catalogue nozzle fits or whether it needs machining — and send the 3D sample drawings so you can check clearance before ordering.

Feeders & Feeding Systems

Stop Buying Feeders. Start Buying Feeding Decisions.

A feeder is cheap. Choosing the wrong one for three years is not.

Where it starts hurting

There is a shelf in almost every EMS plant with four feeders on it that nobody uses. They were bought for a programme that changed, or for a part that turned out to be 2 mm too wide. That shelf is the real cost of feeder procurement.

What is actually going wrong

Feeders get bought as hardware when they should be bought as a decision about how a part arrives. Bulk in a bag is a different decision from tape on a reel, which is different again from a tube or a tray. Buy the hardware first and you lock the packaging decision by accident — which is why plants end up hand-loading parts into a feeder that was supposed to save hand-loading.

A method you can reuse

Decide the packaging before the hardware. Ask three questions in order. Can the part arrive taped? If yes, a tape feeder is almost always cheapest per part. If no, can it be oriented reliably from bulk? If yes, a bowl feeder removes the taping step entirely. If neither, it is a tray or tube part and you should be designing the pick, not the feed. Only then specify a model.

The equipment that carries it

That maps onto real machines. The SBF1001 vibratory bowl feeder takes loose parts from 3 x 3 mm to 50 x 50 mm at 10 to 300 g, orients them on a helical sort track at 0.8 s per feed and ±0.5 mm, with optional polarity screening and lead cutting. The S-RF1001 radial taped feeder lets an SMT mounter pick and insert radial through-hole parts directly. And when a feeder's own accuracy is suspect, the FUJI NXT calibration jig verifies pick position against a ±0.25 mm limit before it goes back on the line.

Why it keeps paying

Decisions outlast programmes. A packaging rule written once gets applied to every new part your NPI team sees, the bowl you bought for one connector family feeds the next three, and the calibration jig keeps the feeders you already own inside spec instead of quietly retiring them.

Your next step

Tell us how your part arrives today — bag, tube, tray, tape — and what it weighs. We will tell you which feeding decision is cheapest over three years, and run a free sample feeding test to prove it.

Smart Storage & ESD

Your Reel Room Is a Warehouse Pretending to Be a Line

Nobody budgets for the twenty minutes an operator spends looking for a reel. It is still the most expensive twenty minutes in the building.

Where it starts hurting

Time one changeover properly, from the moment the line stops to the moment it runs again. Then ask how much of that was placement, and how much was somebody walking a rack with a torch.

What is actually going wrong

A reel room degrades in a way that is invisible on paper. Stock is accurate, the count is right, and yet the line waits. The reason is that storage was designed for density and retrieval was never designed at all. Add mixed 7-inch and 13-inch reels, partial returns that nobody re-labels, and moisture-sensitive parts that have to be tracked by exposure time, and the search becomes the bottleneck instead of the machine.

A method you can reuse

Separate three jobs that get bundled together: store it, find it, prove it. Storage is a density question — how many reels per square metre. Finding is a guidance question — can the operator be led to the exact slot without reading labels. Proving is a data question — can the system tell your MES what left the rack and when. Solve them in that order; a rack that stores well but cannot guide is just a tidier version of the problem.

The equipment that carries it

That is what pick-to-light racks are for. The SIS7200A holds 1,400 seven-inch reels on a 2130 x 400 mm footprint with LED pick-to-light guidance, so the operator is pointed at the slot rather than searching for it. The SIS5210A does the same job on casters — five layers, mixed 7-inch and 13-inch, 600 or 300 reels — for plants that need the stock to move to the line rather than the line to walk to the stock. Both integrate through the Southern Machinery storage API.

Why it keeps paying

Guidance keeps paying because it removes a skill requirement. A new operator finds a reel as fast as a veteran on their first shift, changeover time stops depending on who is rostered, and the exposure and traceability data accumulates without anyone keeping a spreadsheet.

Your next step

Send us your reel mix — how many 7-inch, how many 13-inch, how many changeovers a shift. We will size the rack, and send the API document so your MES team can see the integration before you commit.

Board Handling

Every Board You Touch by Hand Is a Board You Pay for Twice

Once for the labour, and again for the handling damage you will not trace back to this moment.

Where it starts hurting

Count how many times a single PCB is picked up by a human between bare board and final test. In most plants the honest number surprises the person who runs the plant.

What is actually going wrong

Manual board handling costs in two currencies. The visible one is labour: an operator tied to the head of a line, loading magazines one board at a time, unable to do anything else. The invisible one is damage — flex cracks in ceramic parts, ESD events that never register, fingerprints that become solder defects two stations downstream. The second currency is the expensive one precisely because the failure is recorded somewhere else, so the handling step never gets blamed.

A method you can reuse

Map the line by transitions, not by stations. Every place a board changes owner — storage to line, line to buffer, buffer to the next process — is a handling decision with three options: a person, a conveyor, or a loader. Mark each transition with its board size, its cycle time and whether boards arrive in magazines or stacks. The transitions where a person is only moving material, not judging it, are the ones to automate first.

The equipment that carries it

The loader family covers those transitions: SLD magazine loaders for standard magazine flow, the S-MLD250 mini loader where floor space is short, L-type loaders where the line has to turn a corner, destackers for bare boards arriving in stacks, vacuum loaders for thin or flexible boards, and combo units where two of those jobs meet in one frame. Between processes, ESD trays with grids — 457 x 289 x 45 mm inside, 20 cells — carry part-built boards without letting them touch each other.

Why it keeps paying

Automated handling earns its keep after the first week because it makes everything downstream measurable. A board that always arrives in the same orientation at the same interval lets you tune print and placement to a stable input, and the defects that remain are real process defects rather than handling noise.

Your next step

Send us your board dimensions, your magazine type and your line takt. We will tell you which loader fits the transition, and whether a conveyor would do the job for less.

Materials & Consumables

The Cheapest Part on the Line Decides When the Line Stops

A reel of splice tape costs almost nothing. Running out of it costs an hour of a fully staffed line.

Where it starts hurting

Ask your line leader what stopped production last month. Then ask your buyer what they spent the most time sourcing. The two answers are almost never the same component, and that gap is where availability planning quietly fails.

What is actually going wrong

Consumables fail as a category because nobody owns them. They are too cheap to interest procurement and too operational to interest engineering, so they get reordered when someone notices the box is empty. Meanwhile the splice that holds one reel to the next is the single point where a running insertion machine becomes a stopped one, and the lead-forming step that nobody audits is what decides whether a radial part seats at the right height.

A method you can reuse

Give consumables the same treatment as tooling: define the spec, define the reorder point, and define who checks. Spec means the actual dimension — 3-hole or 5-hole splice, 12.7 mm or 15.0 mm tape — not 'the usual'. Reorder point means a shelf count tied to run rate, not to memory. And ownership means one named person per line, because a consumable with no owner is a stoppage with no warning.

The equipment that carries it

On the material side that means AI radial splicing tape in 3-hole and 5-hole formats, masking or kraft paper, die-cut on both faces with locating dots, plus the hand splice cutter sized for 12.7 mm and 15.0 mm radial tape. On the preparation side it means the S-300B, which takes loose radial parts from a bulk bowl, forms the leads to the pitch your inserter expects, checks polarity and value, ejects the failures and delivers a sealed taped pack at 180 ± 20 components per minute.

Why it keeps paying

Getting this layer right pays every single shift, which is why it compounds faster than any machine purchase. A line that never waits for a splice runs the utilisation you costed it at, and a lead pitch that is formed rather than hoped for removes a whole class of insertion rejects before they reach the machine.

Your next step

Tell us your tape widths and monthly volume. We will quote the splice tape and cutter, and if you are still taping by hand, show you what the S-300B changes.

SMT Lines & Turnkey

One Product, One Line: How LED Tube Makers Got Their Cost per Board Down

General-purpose lines are flexible and expensive. If you make one thing a million times, flexibility is something you are paying for and not using.

Where it starts hurting

If 80% of your output is one product, every hour your general-purpose line spends being general is an hour you are paying for an option you never exercise.

What is actually going wrong

A flexible line is priced for the worst case. Wide conveyors for boards you rarely run, a placement head configured for a component mix you no longer have, changeover capability you use twice a month. Meanwhile the one product that actually pays the rent is handled with carriers that were an afterthought, and the line's real constraint turns out to be the 1170 mm strip that does not fit anything neatly.

A method you can reuse

Before specifying anything, write down four numbers for the product that dominates: board or strip dimensions, components per board by type, target boards per hour, and the longest process step. The fourth number is the one that sets the line, because everything else has to be balanced around it. Then ask what you would remove if this were the only product you ever built. Most of the answer is cost you can delete.

The equipment that carries it

The LED T8 tube line is what that looks like built. For 1170 mm strips: a vacuum loader sized to the strip, a 1200 mm stencil printer, a 20-head placement section, 16-strip carriers so the board handling stops being the constraint, and reflow matched to the actual thermal mass. The page carries an ROI calculator that takes your own labour rate, volume and current yield — no figures of ours baked in.

Why it keeps paying

A dedicated line keeps paying because its programs stop changing. Operators learn one setup instead of twelve, carriers wear predictably, and the yield curve flattens at a higher point than a flexible line reaches between changeovers. You buy a narrower machine once and collect the difference every shift.

Your next step

Run the ROI calculator on the LED line page with your own numbers. If your dominant product is not LED tubes, send its four numbers and we will configure the equivalent line around it.
