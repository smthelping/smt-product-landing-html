Where the loss actually happens

Components are rarely killed by a wrist strap

Static damage is a floor-level problem, not an operator-level one. It happens in the gap between the incoming inspection bench and the insertion line - in the container, on the trolley, at the kitting station. That is the part of the process a tray owns.

01

Charge needs somewhere to go

An insulating bin holds the charge on your components until something touches them. Conductive polypropylene gives that charge a path to ground before it can arc, and because the carbon is compounded into the material there is no coating to wear off.

02

Mixed parts cost more than lost parts

When two part numbers share one bin, a wrong value reaches the board, passes in-circuit test, and fails at final assembly. A grid keeps part numbers apart by construction rather than by discipline, so the separation survives a busy shift.

03

Auditors ask for evidence, not intent

An ESD control plan asks you to show the container, its resistance range and its audit record. A tray with a stated resistivity range and a repeatable test point is evidence. A generic tote is a gap that gets written down.

How the tray is built up

Six parts, one tray, no tools

The tray is assembled from a moulded base, a base pad, two lengths of fluted divider and a cover. Every part is replaceable on its own, which is what keeps a grid reconfiguration a five-minute job instead of a purchase order.

01

Moulded conductive base

The shell that carries everything else: conductive polypropylene, moulded with corner posts that locate the cover and keep stacked trays from sliding.

02

Anti-static EVA base pad

A 457 x 289 x 5 mm pad sits flat in the base. It cushions lead ends, stops small parts from working their way under a divider, and gives the tray a soft floor.

03

Three long dividers

Fluted anti-static board cut to 457 mm, running the full internal length. Three of them set four rows across the 289 mm width.

04

Four short dividers

The same board cut to 289 mm, crossing the long dividers. Four of them set five columns along the 457 mm length.

05

The grid they make

Four rows by five columns gives 20 cells at about 91 x 72 mm. That pitch suits most through-hole components, connectors and small sub-assemblies.

06

Reconfigure without reordering

Lift a divider out and the cells either side become one. Move it and the pitch changes. The tray follows the BOM instead of the BOM waiting for a tray.

07

Cover, seated not screwed

A 457 x 289 x 5 mm fluted board lid drops onto the corner posts. Nothing to lose, nothing to thread, and the tray is closed in one movement.

08

Stack and nest

Closed trays stack on their posts and nest when empty. Storage grows upward instead of across the floor, which is usually the cheapest capacity a factory can add.

09

Verify, then put it into service

Meter the surface on receipt and on a fixed cycle, and keep the reading. A tray is a calibrated item in an ESD programme, not a consumable to be replaced when it looks tired.

From delivery to a working programme

Putting the trays into an ESD control plan

A tray only does its job inside a grounded programme. These are the six steps that decide whether the trays you buy change anything on the floor.

STEP 1

Confirm the ground path first

Check the workstation earth points before the trays arrive. A conductive tray that never touches a grounded surface is a plastic box with an unusual colour.

STEP 2

Set the grid to the BOM

Insert the dividers for the part mix on the line and label each cell with its part number or lot code. Agree the layout with the operators, not for them.

STEP 3

Meter on receipt

Measure surface resistance when the trays land and record the reading against the delivery. That first number is the baseline every later audit is compared with.

STEP 4

Train the two rules that matter

Parts go into the tray before they move, and trays are never dragged across a bench. Those two habits cover most of the ways a tray stops working.

STEP 5

Set an audit interval and keep it

Re-meter on a fixed cycle and retire trays that have drifted out of range. The interval matters less than the record - an audit nobody writes down is not an audit.

STEP 6

Reconfigure between runs

When the BOM changes, change the dividers rather than the trays. This is the step that makes a grid tray cheaper than a rack of single-purpose bins.

Eight articles on ESD handling and kitting

Written for the person who has to make the change on the floor: what the loss looks like, how to find your own numbers, and how to convert a line without stopping it.

ARTICLE 01The 40-Second Delay That Costs You a Shift

Nobody times the walk between the store and the bench. It is the cheapest thing on the floor to fix and the last thing anyone measures.

Full text in English. Summary translated.

Ask a production manager for the cycle time of a THT line and you will get a competent answer about insertion, wave soldering and test. Ask how long it takes to get the right components to the right bench and the answer turns into a shrug. The number was never measured, because it does not appear on any machine's counter.

It is not the walking that costs you. It is what happens at the end of the walk. A kit arrives as a bag of mixed parts, the operator tips it into a bin, and from that moment the separation between part numbers exists only in the operator's head. Two part numbers in one bin means somebody, at some point, reaches for the wrong one.

The fix is to make the separation physical rather than mental. A tray with cells is a promise that part A and part B cannot touch, and a promise does not need to be remembered on the third hour of a double shift. The same logic applies to counting: a full cell is a count you do not have to do.

A grid tray turns a bin into a set of containers that happen to share a shell. Twenty cells at about 91 by 72 mm, a divider you can lift out and move, a cover that lets the whole thing travel closed. The part that matters is not the tray - it is that reconfiguring the tray for a new BOM takes minutes and no purchase order.

This is why the trays keep earning after the first job. Every new product on the line is a new kitting problem, and a tray whose dividers move is a tray that has already solved it. The cost per use falls every time the grid changes, which is the opposite of how a single-purpose bin behaves.

Before you buy anything, time one kit from the store to the bench and back. Write the number down. Then decide whether the delay is worth a tray - and if it is, send the component list with the timing and we will size the grid to it.

ARTICLE 02Why Your ESD Audit Keeps Finding the Same Thing

The finding is almost never the wrist strap. It is the container the parts are sitting in when nobody is looking at them.

Full text in English. Summary translated.

Every ESD audit ends with a list, and the same three or four items keep appearing on it. The operators have been trained. The benches are grounded. The straps are tested. And the tray the components travel in is a grey tote with no stated resistance, no test record, and no reason to be there other than that it was free.

The reason it survives audit after audit is that it is invisible. Nobody owns it. It is not a machine, so it is not on the maintenance schedule. It is not a consumable, so it is not on a reorder list. It simply exists, and it carries every component that moves through the building.

The method that fixes this is to treat the container as a calibrated item. That means three things: a stated resistance range from the supplier, a measurement on receipt, and a fixed re-measurement interval with the readings kept. The interval matters less than the record. An audit nobody writes down is not an audit, it is a conversation.

A tray with a printed resistivity range and a flat surface you can meter gives you all three. You can put a probe on it, write the number in a log, and hand the log to an auditor. The conversation changes from what we intend to do to here is what we measured.

And the record compounds. Once the trays are metered on a cycle, you start seeing which ones drift, which handling habits damage them, and where the audit interval should be tightened. That data is only available if the container was measurable in the first place.

Pick the five containers that carry the most components in your factory and ask what their resistance range is. If the answer is that nobody knows, that is the finding. Send us the list and we will tell you what a metered replacement would look like.

ARTICLE 03Five Rules for Separating Components You Cannot See

A grid is a rule you can hold in your hand. These are the rules that make it work.

Full text in English. Summary translated.

Components are small, similar, and often identical in colour. Resistors in two values look the same at arm's length. A grid tray does not make them different - it makes them physically unable to meet, which is a much stronger guarantee than a label.

The first rule is one part number per cell, and never two. The moment a cell holds two values, the tray is a bin with decoration. If the cell is too small for the component, the answer is a coarser grid, not a shared cell.

The second rule is that the grid follows the BOM, not the other way round. Dividers are separate parts precisely so that a new product means a new divider layout. If changing the layout requires a new tray, the layout will not get changed.

The third rule is that a full cell is a count. Counting components out of a bin is the most error-prone and most repeated task in kitting. Counting cells is a glance. Design the cell size around the quantity you kit per board and the counting problem largely disappears.

The fourth rule is that the tray closes before it moves. An open tray on a trolley is a tray that will be dropped, and a dropped tray redistributes everything inside it. A cover that seats on the corner posts takes one movement and removes the whole failure mode.

The fifth rule is that the tray is metered, not trusted. Conductive material degrades with abrasion and time. A tray that read 10⁶ ohm last year may not read 10⁶ ohm today, and the only way to know is to measure it. Build the measurement into the kitting cycle rather than into an annual event.

ARTICLE 04What a Grid Tray Actually Changes on the Bench

Not the components. The behaviour of the person loading them.

Full text in English. Summary translated.

A tray looks like a passive object, and the assumption is that buying one changes nothing except the storage. In practice the change is behavioural, and it shows up in the first week.

The first change is that kitting becomes a placement task instead of a sorting task. The operator stops deciding where things go and starts filling cells. Decisions are where errors live, and a grid removes most of them from the moment of loading.

The second change is that partial kits stop disappearing. When a job is interrupted, a tray with a cover can be closed and put aside with its contents intact and its count still readable. A bag of loose parts cannot, so it gets tipped back and re-counted later.

The third change is that the tray travels. Because the grid holds the parts and the cover holds the grid, a loaded tray can go from the store to the line to the rework station without being decanted at any point. Every decant is a chance to lose a part or mix two values.

The fourth change is the one that shows up in audit. A tray with cells and labels is self-documenting: an auditor can see the separation without asking anyone. That is the difference between a finding that gets closed and a finding that gets repeated.

None of this requires the tray to be expensive. It requires the grid to match the components and the tray to be reconfigurable when the product changes. Tell us the component envelope and the quantity per board and we will propose the grid that does the most work for the least tray.

ARTICLE 05The Tray You Buy Once and Reconfigure Forever

If a storage decision has to be repeated every time the product changes, it is not a solution. It is a subscription.

Full text in English. Summary translated.

Most component storage fails the second time. The first job fits the bin perfectly. The second job needs different quantities, or a different component, or a different count per board, and the bin that was perfect becomes a compromise that nobody gets around to fixing.

The reason is that most storage is bought as a shape. A bin is a shape. A rack slot is a shape. When the component changes, the shape is wrong and the only remedy is another purchase. The cost of the storage then scales with the number of products you build, which is the wrong direction.

The alternative is to buy the shell once and the separation separately. A tray with a fixed internal size and separate dividers has one purchased part that never changes and a cheap part that changes constantly. That inversion is what makes the cost per product fall instead of rise.

In practice this means ordering spare dividers in both lengths at the same time as the trays. Three long and four short gives the standard grid; a second set of each gives you a different pitch on the same tray. The incremental cost of that flexibility is a fraction of a second tray.

It also changes how the storage behaves over years rather than months. Dividers wear, get lost, and get cut down for a special job. If they are a stocked spare, the tray is repaired in a day. If they are part of a sealed unit, the unit is replaced.

Ask what happens to your storage when the next product comes. If the honest answer is that you buy more storage, ask us for a tray and a spare divider set instead. The second product is usually the one that decides whether the first purchase was a good idea.

ARTICLE 06Measure Your Own Handling Time Before You Buy Anything

Ten minutes with a stopwatch is worth more than any payback figure a supplier gives you.

Full text in English. Summary translated.

Every supplier, including us, has a payback number that says their product pays for itself. Those numbers are built on assumptions about your line, and your line is not the line the assumption came from. The only figure that matters is the one you measure yourself.

The measurement is simple. Pick one board, follow the kit from the store to the point where it is ready to insert, and time the whole thing. Do it three times on different shifts. What you are looking for is not the average, it is the variation: the shift where it takes twice as long is where the money is.

Then do the same thing with a tray. Fill cells from the kit, close the tray, walk it, open it at the bench. The tray time is usually dominated by the loading, which is a fixed cost per kit rather than a variable one, and that is why the comparison improves as quantities go up.

Put both numbers into the calculator on this page along with your tray price and your wage. If the payback comes out longer than you are comfortable with, do not buy the trays - the page will tell you so rather than printing a flattering figure, and the same ten minutes has saved you a purchase.

If the payback is short, you now have a number you can defend in a budget meeting, because you measured it. That is worth more than any specification sheet, and it takes an afternoon.

Send us the two timings and the component list when you write. We will size the grid against your actual numbers instead of a generic pitch, and tell you honestly if a tray is not the answer.

ARTICLE 07Latent ESD Damage: The Defect That Passes Test

A component that fails in the field usually passed every test you ran on it. That is the whole problem.

Full text in English. Summary translated.

Electrostatic discharge does not always destroy a component. More often it wounds it - a gate oxide thinned, a junction degraded, a bond weakened. The part passes in-circuit test, passes functional test, ships, and fails three months later in a customer's hands where the failure is expensive and the cause is unknowable.

This is why ESD control is a process problem rather than an inspection problem. You cannot screen for latent damage, because the part tests good. You can only prevent it, and prevention happens at every point where a component is touched, moved, or stored.

The storage point is the one most often left out. Benches are grounded, operators are strapped, and then the components spend four hours in an ungrounded tote on a trolley. The control programme is intact everywhere except the place the parts spend most of their time.

The method that closes this gap is to make the container part of the ground path. A conductive tray sitting on a grounded surface gives accumulated charge somewhere to go continuously, not only at the moment of handling. The tray does not need to be touched by an operator to work.

That is also why the material matters more than the shape. A conductive compound carries the charge through the body of the tray; a coating carries it only until the coating is scratched, which on a working tray is a matter of weeks. Ask which one you are buying.

If your failure analysis keeps finding field returns with no identified root cause, the storage path is worth auditing before anything else. Send us the component types and how they are currently stored, and we will tell you where the gap is likely to be.

ARTICLE 08From Bin to Grid: A 30-Day Conversion Plan

Converting a line does not need a project. It needs an order of operations.

Full text in English. Summary translated.

The reason storage upgrades stall is that they get planned as a change programme. Somebody writes a scope, somebody else asks about the budget, and six months later the totes are still there. A conversion does not need a programme; it needs one bench, one product, and a month.

Week one is measurement, not purchase. Pick the bench with the worst kitting problem, time three kits, and record the component types and quantities per board. You now know the cell size and the count, which are the only two facts that decide the tray.

Week two is the pilot. One tray, configured for that one product, with the grid set to the measured quantities. Do not convert the whole line. The point of the pilot is to find out what you got wrong about cell size and cell count, and it is much cheaper to be wrong about one tray.

Week three is the correction. Almost every pilot needs one change: a divider moved, a cell made wider, a second tray added for the components that did not fit the first. Because the dividers are separate parts, that correction costs a divider and not a tray.

Week four is the rollout decision, and it should be made on the measured numbers from weeks one and two rather than on the pilot's novelty. If the timing improved and the miscounts stopped, extend it. If it did not, you have spent one tray finding out.

Thirty days, one bench, one product. Send us the component list from week one and we will quote the tray and the spare divider set so that the correction in week three is already on the shelf.

Before you send an enquiry

Questions buyers ask first

What is the internal size of the tray in the drawing?
457 mm long, 289 mm wide and 45 mm deep inside, with the cover and the EVA pad at 5 mm each. Overall height is drawn as about 50 mm and is marked indicative on the drawing, so treat it as a nominal figure until the final drawing is issued.
How many cells does it make?
Twenty: four rows across the 289 mm width from three long dividers, five columns along the 457 mm length from four short dividers. The pitch works out at about 91 x 72 mm. Changing the divider count changes the number of cells without changing the tray.
Is the tray conductive or dissipative?
The product sheet states a surface resistivity of 10⁴ to 10⁸ ohm for the range, tested to IEC 61340-5-1. The reference datasheet supplied with the enquiry states 1 x 10⁶ to less than 1 x 10⁹ ohm surface resistance to ANSI/ESD STM11.11, which is a different measurement on a different tray. The two figures are printed side by side in the open points table rather than merged, because surface resistivity and surface resistance are not interchangeable.
Can I change the grid later?
Yes, and that is the point of the construction. The dividers are separate fluted board parts, so a different BOM means a different divider set, not a different tray. Order spare dividers in both lengths and the same tray covers several products.
Does it come with a cover?
The drawing lists a 457 x 289 x 5 mm cover plate as one of the four components, and the published range lists a box cover as an accessory. Confirm on the quotation whether the cover is included in the unit price or listed separately, because the two documents describe it in different places.
What temperature can it take?
The product sheet lists a standard grade plus an optional 120 °C and 150 °C grade. It does not state the maximum for the standard grade, so if the trays will sit near a reflow oven, a wave solder pot or a selective solder cell, specify the high-temperature grade and confirm the number in writing.
Can it go into a cleanroom?
The published range lists smooth-surface grades for ISO Class 5 to 8 environments, which are the models without deep grid crevices. A fluted divider tray has edges by design, so if your cleanroom classification is the binding constraint, ask for the smooth-surface model instead of this one.
How do I check the trays after delivery?
Meter the surface on receipt, record the reading against the delivery, and re-meter on a fixed cycle. The reference datasheet states its test method as ANSI/ESD STM11.11 and cites ANSI/ESD S20.20, S541 and S4.1; no test report for this tray was supplied with the enquiry, so agree the method and the acceptance range with us before the first shipment.
