01 - Why it matters

Radial parts are where a mixed-technology line quietly loses its afternoon

Every EMS plant we visit has the same shape of problem: the SMT side runs at machine speed, and the through-hole side runs at the speed of a wrist. The gap is almost never the placement head. It is the material presentation in front of it.

01

The picking bench is the real bottleneck

Electrolytic capacitors, Y-caps and terminals do not arrive in reels. They arrive in taped stacks that somebody has to cut, straighten and feed by hand. That bench does not scale with your mounter, it scales with your headcount - and it is the first place quality drifts when a shift gets tired.

02

Lead geometry is a process, not a step

A radial component is only insertable when the lead length matches the board, the pitch matches the hole spacing and the body sits square to the nozzle. Hand operators compensate for all three every cycle. A feeder that trims to 3-6 mm, forms to the material pitch and bends at 90 degrees makes those three settings a setup, not a judgement call.

03

Tape pitch is not a detail you can average

Radial tape comes on 12.7 mm and 15 mm hole pitch, and the two are not interchangeable. A feeder specified for one will not run the other. Getting this wrong is the single most common reason a radial feeder underperforms on a factory floor - and it is decided before the purchase order, not after.

02 - How it works

One motor, two feed wheels, and a cutting module you can move

The SRF1001 is deliberately simple on the outside and precisely arranged on the inside. Tape enters vertically, the feed wheels index it forward by the hole pitch, the front-end optical fibre confirms a component is present, and the cutting module - whose position is adjustable - trims and forms the leads before the head takes the part.

  1. 01

    Open the safety plug

    Before the first use, remove the safety plug from the safety hole. It locks the mechanism for transport; while the feeder runs, the plug stays out.

  2. 02

    Lock the feeder to the bank

    Hold the fixed handle, drop the positioning pin into the positioning hole, then release the handle. The feeder is fixed, square and repeatable - no shimming, no re-datum per shift.

  3. 03

    Connect power and air

    Plug the power lead into the power interface - the machine-side interface usually makes the second lead redundant - then connect the air interface. Working voltage is 24 V DC; working air pressure is 0.4-0.6 MPa.

  4. 04

    Load the tape on the feed gear

    Guide the tape in through the components input channel and sit the tape holes over the bumps on the feed gear. If the holes miss the bumps, the feeding position is wrong - that is the whole alignment check. Keep the smooth side of the paper tape against the wheel face.

  5. 05

    Set the cut, form and bend

    Set the cutting module to the lead length your board needs, anywhere from 3 to 6 mm. Fit the shaping mould that matches the material pitch. If the odd-form head needs a 90-degree body, enable the bending option.

  6. 06

    Index, sense, cut, present

    The feed motor drives both feed wheels, the front-end optical fibre watches for material, the cutter trims and returns, and the part presents at the pick point. If three consecutive cycles pass without material, the feeder alarms instead of feeding air.

06 - Set-up

Commissioning is a same-shift job

There is no software licence, no external compressor beyond the plant air line, and no calibration rig. Six steps take the feeder from the crate to producing parts, and the parameter set is small enough to write on the inside of a cabinet door.

  1. 01

    Unbox and inspect

    Check the feeder, the power lead, the air line and the spare safety plug against the packing list. The safety plug should be in the safety hole for transport - remove it before first use.

  2. 02

    Mount and lock

    Hold the fixed handle, insert the positioning pin into the positioning hole, release the handle. Confirm the feeder is seated square on the bank and the latch is locked before power is applied.

  3. 03

    Power, air and ground

    Connect 24 V DC and 0 V, and bring the air line in at 0.4-0.6 MPa. When the host communicates with the feeder, 0 V must be shared. Never short 24 V to 0 V - that damages the control board.

  4. 04

    Load a tape and set the position

    Thread the tape through the components input channel, seat the tape holes over the feed gear bumps, then set Y with the dividing plates and X with the limit block. Keep the smooth side of the paper tape to the wheel.

  5. 05

    Set cut, form and parameters

    Set the cutting module to the required lead length, fit the shaping mould for the material pitch, then set the feed speed, the feed alarm length and the refeed interval on the panel. Parameters save automatically after a few seconds of inactivity.

  6. 06

    Validate before you run production

    Run the documented validation: a continuous feed test and a powered ageing run. Then clean the fibre head, lock the settings, and log them per part number so the next changeover is a recall rather than a re-tune.

INSIGHT 01The picking bench is not a headcount problem

Every mixed-technology line has a bench where somebody trims, straightens and feeds radial parts by hand. It is usually treated as a staffing issue. It is a material-presentation issue wearing a staffing costume.

Walk onto most EMS floors and the SMT side is quiet, fast and measured. Then you reach the through-hole section and there is a bench: two or three people, a tray of electrolytics, a pair of cutters, and a rhythm that has not changed in a decade.

The instinct is to fix it with people. Add a shift. Move the strongest operator there. Pay overtime. None of that touches the cause, because the cause is not the operator - it is that radial components arrive as taped stacks that nothing in the line can present to a nozzle. The bench exists to convert packaging into presentation. That conversion is a machine job.

Here is a way to see it in ten minutes. Take the parts that pass over that bench and write down three numbers for each: the tape hole pitch, the lead length the board needs, and the lead pitch the holes are drilled to. You will almost always find that the bench is doing the same three mechanical operations on every part, all day, with human hands and human judgement.

Once you have written it down, the bench stops looking like a staffing decision. It becomes a specification: a feeder that indexes tape by the hole pitch, trims the lead to a set length, forms it to a set pitch, and presents the part at a repeatable height. That is what a radial tape feeder is - the SRF1001 trims to 3-6 mm with an adjustable cutting module, forms with moulds matched to material pitch, and indexes with two motor-driven feed wheels at +/-0.2 mm.

The habit that keeps it working is boring and it is the whole game: log the settings per part number. Feed speed, feed alarm length, refeed interval, cutting module position, shaping mould. A changeover then becomes a recall instead of a re-tune, and the bench does not quietly reappear on the third shift.

INSIGHT 02Your radial line is not slow. Its changeover is.

When a radial insertion cell misses its output target, the head gets blamed. Most of the time the head is fine and the feeder is being re-set from scratch every time the part number changes.

A radial insertion cell that runs one product all week will look excellent on any benchmark. The same cell running four product families a day can look broken - same head, same boards, same operators, half the output.

That gap is almost never cycle time. It is the re-set. Somebody has to change the tape, re-align the feed position in Y and X, re-check the lead length, swap the shaping mould if the pitch changed, and then run a few scrap boards to prove it. Do that four times a day and you have lost more hours to set-up than the head ever cost you in speed.

The method is to make every setting a reference rather than an adjustment. On a radial feeder there are exactly four mechanical references worth writing down: the Y position set by the dividing plates, the X position set by the limit block, the cutting module position that sets the 3-6 mm lead length, and the shaping mould that matches the material pitch. Add the panel parameters - feed speed, feed alarm length, refeed interval - and you have the complete recipe for a part number.

The point of a feeder built this way is not that it is fast. It is that it is returnable. The SRF1001 fixes the tape with dividing plates and limit blocks rather than by feel, and the panel parameters save themselves after a few seconds of inactivity, so a changeover is a lookup and a re-seat rather than an experiment.

Then build the loop that keeps it: a laminated card per part number, kept at the machine, with the four mechanical references and the parameter codes written on it. Review the card whenever a new part is added. A line that can change over in minutes stops being a line that is judged on its best day.

INSIGHT 03The five-minute radial audit that decides a feeder purchase

Before you price a feeder, price the problem. Five measurements taken on your own floor will tell you whether a radial tape feeder pays for itself - and whether you should buy one at all.

Most feeder decisions are made backwards: a supplier quotes, the plant manager asks for a discount, and somewhere in the middle nobody has measured the thing the feeder is supposed to fix.

The result is a purchase that cannot be evaluated. Six months later the argument is about impressions instead of numbers, and the feeder either gets quietly under-used or gets credit it did not earn.

Here is the audit. Five measurements, taken at the bench, in five minutes. One: how many radial components go onto a typical board. Two: how many boards the line runs per day, averaged across the products you actually build. Three: how many seconds an operator spends picking, trimming, forming and inserting one radial part today - time it with a phone, do not estimate. Four: the tape hole pitch, measured on the tape rather than read off a datasheet, because 12.7 mm and 15 mm are not interchangeable and the feeder must be specified for one. Five: the loaded hourly cost of the operator doing that work, including benefits and supervision.

Those five numbers are the entire business case. Components times boards times seconds divided by 3600 gives you hours per day; hours per day times the loaded wage gives you money per year; money per year divided into the feeder price gives you a payback in months. Nothing in that chain depends on a supplier's claim.

The discipline that makes the audit worth doing is repeating it. Run the same five measurements a quarter and keep them in one place. A feeder that was marginal at 40,000 components a month becomes obviously correct at 90,000 - and you will see that coming instead of arguing about it afterwards.

INSIGHT 04Tape pitch, lead length, span: three settings that decide your radial yield

Radial insertion problems are usually described as quality problems. They are almost always three mechanical settings that were never specified together.

A radial component that goes in crooked, sits proud of the board or clinches badly is a symptom. The cause is normally upstream of the head, in three numbers that nobody wrote down when the part was chosen.

The first is tape hole pitch. Radial tape comes on 12.7 mm and 15 mm pitch and the two are explicitly not interchangeable. Specify the wrong one and the feeder will mis-index by a fraction of a pitch, which shows up as intermittent misses rather than a clean failure - the worst kind of fault to chase.

The second is lead length. A radial feeder trims the lead to a set length; on the SRF1001 that is 3-6 mm, adjustable by moving the cutting module. If the trimmed length does not match the board thickness and hole depth, the part either will not seat or clinches short. The third is lead span - the distance between the two leads - which has to match the drilled hole pitch, and which is set by the shaping mould rather than by the feeder.

Treat those three as a specification, not a setting. For every radial part, write down pitch, trimmed length and required span before the part is released to production. Then the feeder's job is unambiguous: index by the pitch, trim to the length, form to the span. A 90-degree body bend, where the head needs it, is a fourth line on the same card and a published option on the feeder.

Keep the card alive. Every new radial part gets a line; every line gets verified with a first-article check. Within a quarter you will have a radial insertion specification that outlives the people who wrote it - which is the only version that actually reduces yield loss.

INSIGHT 05Stop buying insertion machines. Start buying feeding.

Plants that struggle with through-hole automation usually already own the machine. What they do not own is a reliable way to present the part to it.

Ask a plant manager what limits through-hole automation and the answer is usually the inserter. Ask what actually stops the cell and the answer is much more specific: the tape ran out, the part was presented at the wrong height, the leads were the wrong length, somebody had to stop and re-thread the feeder.

That is a feeding problem, not a placement problem. A modern odd-form or radial inserter is a capable machine surrounded by a weak material interface. Buying a second, faster inserter to solve a feeding problem buys you a second machine waiting on the same tape.

The method is to specify the feed first. Take the radial parts you actually run and build a feeding matrix: component type, outline size, tape pitch, lead wire diameter, trimmed lead length, required span, and whether the head wants the body flat. Then look for the smallest number of feeders that covers the matrix. Frequently it is one platform plus a couple of moulds - a radial tape feeder covering electrolytics, Y-caps, square capacitors, terminals, springs and bent resistors from 2 x 2 x 3 mm to 15 x 15 x 30 mm.

Specify that way and the machine you already own gets busier. Feeding accuracy of +/-0.2 mm, a one-second action cycle and a 1 s takt headroom mean the inserter stops being the constraint - and the front-end optical fibre that alarms after three empty cycles stops the quiet starvation that shows up as unexplained output loss.

Keep it honest by measuring the thing you bought. Track machine utilisation and unplanned stops before and after, and track them per part number rather than per month. Feeding improvements hide inside averages; they are obvious per part.

INSIGHT 06From three shifts of hand-insertion to one: the arithmetic

Labour reduction is the easiest claim in this industry to make and the easiest to disprove. So here is the arithmetic, laid out so you can attack it with your own numbers.

Somebody in the plant says the radial bench needs three shifts. Somebody else says automation is too expensive. Both statements feel true and neither is a number, which is why the argument never ends.

The reason it never ends is that nobody has separated the bench into what it actually does. A radial bench performs four operations: present the tape, trim the lead, form the lead to pitch, and insert. Automation rarely removes all four in one move. What it reliably removes is the first three - and those three are where the seconds are.

So run the split. Measure manual seconds per component and divide them into present, trim, form and insert. Then ask what a radial tape feeder takes over. Indexing by tape hole pitch removes present. An adjustable cutting module trimming to 3-6 mm removes trim. Replaceable shaping moulds matched to material pitch remove form. What remains for a human is loading tape and clearing the occasional alarm.

Now the arithmetic is simple and yours. Components per board times boards per day times the seconds removed, divided by 3600, gives hours per day. Multiply by working days and the loaded wage for the annual value. A feeder with a one-second action cycle and 1,000 validated consecutive feeds does not take holidays, and it does not slow down on the third shift.

Then protect the result. Keep a spare cutter on the shelf - the cutter is a consumable rated at 5 million cycles - and rely on the documented 1-day spare-parts lead time rather than a stockroom. Log parameters per part number so a new operator can reproduce a shift's settings in a minute.

INSIGHT 07Electrolytics, Y-caps and terminals on one feeder: planning the mix

The parts on a radial bench are rarely one family. They are electrolytics, Y-capacitors, square capacitors, terminals, springs and bent resistors - and they do not all want the same treatment.

A feeder bought for one part is easy to justify and easy to outgrow. A feeder bought for a mixed radial programme is harder to specify and much more valuable - but only if the mix was planned before the order.

The trap is treating the mix as one requirement. A 5 mm electrolytic and a wide terminal differ in outline, lead wire diameter, lead pitch and often in whether the head wants the body upright or flat. Specify the average and you will mis-feed both ends of the range.

Build the mix as a table. For each part: component type, outline in three dimensions, tape hole pitch, lead wire core diameter, trimmed lead length, required lead span, and body orientation. Then group the table by what changes mechanically. Parts that share a pitch and a span share a shaping mould. Parts that share a lead length share a cutting module position. Parts that differ only in outline usually need no change at all.

That grouping is what makes one platform cover a whole programme. The published SRF1001 window runs from 2 x 2 x 3 mm to 15 x 15 x 30 mm with a 0.3-1.0 mm wire core, over electrolytics, Y-capacitors, square capacitors, terminals, springs and bent resistors. Shaping moulds are ordered by pitch; 90-degree bending is specified once, at order, for the parts that need it.

The habit that keeps a mixed feeder efficient is grouping the schedule, not the parts. Run all the parts that share a mould and a cutting position together, and the changeover count drops without changing a single mechanical setting.

INSIGHT 08Eight questions to ask a feeder supplier before the purchase order

A radial feeder is a mechanical contract. These eight questions turn a vague quotation into a specification you can hold someone to.

Most feeder purchases go wrong in the same place: a quotation arrives with a price and a picture, and nobody asks the eight questions that decide whether the machine will work on your parts.

The cost of not asking shows up months later as a feeder that runs one product well and everything else badly, and by then the conversation is about blame rather than specification.

Ask these eight. One: what tape hole pitches does it accept, and is the unit I am buying specified for 12.7 mm or for 15 mm - because the two are not interchangeable. Two: what is the component window, in millimetres, in all three dimensions, and what wire diameter does it cover. Three: what lead length can it produce, and how is that length adjusted. Four: how is the lead span set, and are the moulds included or ordered per pitch. Five: is body bending available, is it an option, and can it be retrofitted later. Six: what are the utility requirements - voltage, average and peak current, air pressure. Seven: how does it talk to my machine, and can it run standalone. Eight: what is the spare-parts lead time and which parts are consumables.

Now check whether the supplier answers with numbers. 13 - FAQ

The questions we are actually asked

Straight answers, including the ones that end with us telling you not to buy.

What components can the SRF1001 feed?
Electrolytic capacitors, Y-capacitors, square capacitors, terminals, springs and bent resistors, with component outlines from 2 x 2 x 3 mm up to 15 x 15 x 30 mm and a lead wire core diameter of 0.3-1.0 mm. If your part is a taped radial component inside that window, it is very likely feedable - send a sample tape and we will confirm the presentation.
Can one feeder run both 12.7 mm and 15 mm tape?
No. The two hole pitches are explicitly not interchangeable in the specification. The feeder must be specified for one of them at order. This is the first question to settle, because it is decided before the purchase order and cannot be adjusted afterwards.
How fast is it, and what takt should I plan?
One action cycle takes one second, and feeding accuracy is +/-0.2 mm. In practice the insertion head sets the takt - the feeder's job is to never starve it. Plan the takt from your head and your board, and treat the feeder's one-second cycle as headroom.
What lead length and bending can it produce?
Lead cutting spans 3-6 mm and is adjustable by moving the cutting module. Shaping uses replaceable moulds matched to the material pitch. A 90-degree bend is available as an option for heads that need the body flat to the board. Cutting and shaping are part of the base feeder; bending is specified at order.
What does it need from the plant?
24 V DC at 4 A average and 6 A peak, working air at 0.4-0.6 MPa, and a connection to the host machine through an 8-core, 16 mm aviation plug if you want IO communication. There is no external compressor, no software licence and no separate controller cabinet.
What happens when a component jams or runs out?
The front-end optical fibre detects material. If three consecutive cycles pass with no material, the feeder raises an alarm rather than running the tape through - the manual's error code for this is ERR01, cleared with the reset control. Most feeding problems are set-up problems: a tape whose holes missed the feed gear bumps, or a feed speed and refeed interval that have not been tuned to the material.
What should I keep on the shelf?
The cutter is explicitly a consumable with a 5 million-cycle service life, so keep a cutter. Beyond that, keep the wear items your own log tells you about - and rely on the documented 1-day spare-parts lead time rather than building a warehouse. Log every parameter change per part number; the cheapest spare part is a setting you can find again.
