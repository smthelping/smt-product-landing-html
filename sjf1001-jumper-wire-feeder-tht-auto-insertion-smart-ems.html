Coiled tinned copper wire in - cut and formed jumpers out - one slot on any SMT mounter or odd-form inserter

You do not buy jumper wires.
You buy wire by the kilo and let the machine form them.

The SJF1001 is a jumper wire forming feeder. It takes a coil of tinned copper wire up to 2 kg, unwinds it, straightens it through a set of adjusting and fixed wheels, feeds it with a motor-driven ratchet and photoelectric sensor, then cuts and forms each jumper in one cam-driven stroke and presents it at the pick point. Wire diameter runs 0.5-1.2 mm, pin span 6-40 mm, pin length 3-5 mm - all set by changing the forming mould, about five minutes of work. There is no compressed air to connect, no paper tape to dispose of and no offcut waste: the feeder integrates feeding, cutting and forming in a single 580 mm body that occupies one feeder slot.

01 - Why it matters

A jumper wire is the cheapest part on the board and the most expensive to make by hand

Most EMS plants we walk through are not short of insertion capacity. They are short of the small, repetitive, high-variety work that sits in front of it. Jumper wires are the clearest example: a piece of tinned copper wire that costs almost nothing, turned into an expensive part by the minutes a person spends cutting, bending and inserting it.

01

Pre-formed jumpers are a part number you keep buying

Buy jumpers pre-formed and you buy the wire, the forming labour, the packaging, the freight and the shelf space - and you re-order all of it every month. Feed coiled wire instead and the part number disappears. The material cost of a jumper wire is the material cost of the wire, plus a forming stroke the machine was already making.

02

Every jumper geometry is a mould, not a new purchase order

Jumper requirements change with the board: a 6 mm span here, a 40 mm span there, a longer lead on the thick board and a shorter one on the thin one. A forming feeder absorbs that variation through the mould. The manual states the disassembly and assembly takes about five minutes - which is the difference between a changeover and a stock-out.

03

The insertion step is where the money actually goes

A jumper wire has two ends and both of them have to land in the right hole, at the right height, with the right clinch, every board. That is a tolerance problem, not a dexterity problem - and it is exactly the kind of problem a machine solves and a tired third shift does not.

02 - How it works

One strand, four modules, one cam-driven stroke

The feeder is mechanically simple on purpose. A coil of tinned copper wire sits on the unwinding disc. The wire is pulled through a ring buckle into the straightening module, where one set of adjusting wheels and one set of fixed wheels take the curl out of it. The feeding module then indexes it with a motor, a ratchet and a photoelectric sensor, and the moving knife mechanism cuts and forms the jumper in a single stroke before presenting it at the pick point.

  1. 01

    Load the coil on the unwinding disc

    Place the coiled material on the rotating disc. Press the handle to open the pressing wheel, pass the wire through the bottom hole, then through the feeding wheel and the buckle. That is the whole threading job - there is no tape to align and no sprocket hole to seat.

  2. 02

    Straighten the wire

    The straightening module is a group of adjusting wheels and a group of fixed wheels held in a fixing seat and an adjusting seat. Loosen the adjustment seat and turn the adjustment nut until the wire leaves the module straight rather than coiled. This setting is what makes the cut length repeatable.

  3. 03

    Thread the feeding module

    Press handle 1 to open pressing wheel 1, insert the wire, release. Press handle 2 to open pressing wheel 2, then press the forward button on the panel until the wire passes the wheel. Release handle 2 and keep feeding forward until the wire is more than halfway into the forming module.

  4. 04

    Fit the forming mould for your jumper

    The forming mould is a front mould, a guide block and a rear mould. Span and pin length are properties of the mould, so a new jumper geometry means a new mould rather than a new feeder. The manual puts the disassembly and assembly at about five minutes.

  5. 05

    Set the feeding length on the coded switch

    Feeding length is set with the digital coded switch on the operation panel, and the working mode is switched with the mode button. The panel carries five buttons - power, mode switching, reset and alarm, cutter and feeding - so every motor action can be commanded by hand during set-up.

  6. 06

    Let the cam do the cut and the form

    In automatic mode the cycle runs in three moves: the unwinding module replenishes the reserve length when the buckle trips its photoelectric switch; the feeding module indexes one pitch after the moving knife mechanism moves; and after the inserter takes a part, the moving knife mechanism completes one cutting and forming stroke. Nothing is thrown away - the feeder does not produce waste.

No compressed air anywhere in the design

The specification sheet lists the air source requirement as none. That removes the airline, the filter-regulator, the lubricator and the whole class of faults that arrive with moisture in a pneumatic circuit. On a feeder that sits inches from a placement head, that matters.

Air: none required

Motor, ratchet and photoelectric sensor feeding

The feeding module combines a motor with a ratchet and a photoelectric sensor. The ratchet gives a positive mechanical index rather than a friction grip, and the sensor confirms the wire actually moved - which is what keeps a 1.5-second cycle honest over a long shift.

Positive index + sensing

A straightening module, not just a guide

Wire arrives on a coil and holds its curl. One set of adjusting wheels and one set of fixed wheels take that curl out before the wire is measured, because a curled wire measures short. The adjustment is a nut, not a shim kit.

Adjusting + fixed wheels

Cut and form in one cam stroke

The moving knife mechanism carries the cutting cutter, the forming cutter, a cam slot plate and a rocker. The forming knife and floating plate travel together, then the cam slot plate converts that motion so the cutting knife moves the other way. One stroke cuts and forms.

One stroke, two tools

A forming mould, not a feeder, per geometry

Span and pin length are functions of the mould: front mould, guide block and rear mould. Changing a jumper geometry is a five-minute mechanical change, so one feeder covers a family of jumpers rather than one.

About 5 minutes per change

No waste in the cycle

The manual states plainly that the feeder does not produce waste, because cutting, forming and automatic feeding are integrated. There is no paper tape to dispose of and no offcut bin to empty - which is a material saving as well as a housekeeping one.

Zero offcut waste

Panel controls, from the operation manual

The feeder runs from five front-panel buttons plus a digital coded switch. The same panel drives the machine during set-up and during production, so an operator can jog the wire or fire the cutter without opening a laptop.

  • Power - switches the feeder on and off
  • Mode switching - moves the feeder between automatic and jog operation
  • Reset and alarm prompt - clears the alarm state after a fault is corrected
  • Cutter - fires one cutting and forming stroke by hand, for set-up and for checking the mould
  • Feeding - indexes the wire forward by hand while threading the feeding module
  • Digital coded switch - sets the feeding length, pressed to step the value

06 - Set-up

Commissioning is a same-shift job, and the set-up is mechanical

There is no air line to bring in, no software licence, no tape to thread and no calibration rig. Six steps take the feeder from the crate to producing jumpers, and the settings that matter are a nut, a coded switch and a mould.

  1. 01

    Unbox and inspect

    Check the feeder, the power lead, the forming mould and the spare moulds against the packing list. Confirm the mould fitted is the one for your span and pin length before you power anything.

  2. 02

    Mount in the feeder slot

    The feeder occupies one station slot. Seat it in the bank and confirm the interface is fully engaged, because the same interface carries both communication and power - a partial engagement is an intermittent fault that looks like a control problem.

  3. 03

    Power up on 24 V DC

    Connect 24 V DC at 150 W. There is no air connection to make. Confirm the panel indicator light behaves as expected before loading wire, so a wiring fault is not mistaken for a feeding fault.

  4. 04

    Load the coil and thread the wire

    Place the coil on the rotating disc, press the handle to open the pressing wheel, and pass the wire through the bottom hole, the feeding wheel and the buckle. Then thread the feeding module: handle 1 open, wire in, release; handle 2 open, feed forward with the panel button, release, and keep feeding until the wire is more than halfway into the forming module.

  5. 05

    Straighten, then set the length

    Loosen the adjustment seat and turn the adjustment nut until the wire runs straight. Then set the feeding length with the digital coded switch. Log both settings per jumper part number - the straightener nut is the setting most likely to drift between operators.

  6. 06

    Validate before you run production

    Run a batch and inspect the span, the pin length and the cut face. Check the first part after every mould change, because the mould is the only thing that defines the geometry. Then lock the settings and record them, so the next changeover is a recall rather than a re-tune.

Eight notes on jumper wire feeding, written for the person who has to make the number

Each note follows the same path: the moment the problem becomes visible, why the obvious explanation is usually wrong, a method you can run this week, what to put in front of the machine, how to keep it working after the first month, and what to do next. Read them in order, or open the one that matches your shift.

INSIGHT 01The cheapest part on the board is the one you spend the most on

A jumper wire costs almost nothing as wire and a great deal as minutes. Here is how to see which one you are actually paying for.

Walk the through-hole area of almost any EMS plant and you will find the same bench. A coil of tinned copper wire, a pair of cutters, a small forming jig, a tray of finished jumpers, and one person who has done it so many times that the motion looks effortless. It is the quietest station in the building, and it is usually the one with the worst cost per placement on the whole line.

The obvious explanation is that hand-forming is cheap because the material is cheap. That is the wrong accounting. The material cost of a jumper wire is the cost of the wire, which is negligible. What you are actually buying at that bench is a person's minute, repeated for every jumper, on every board, in every shift - and that minute is the most expensive thing on the board. A jumper that looks like a fraction of a cent in the BOM can carry several cents of loaded labour, and it does so invisibly, because nobody puts the bench on a cost sheet.

Run the measurement before you run the argument. Pick the board with the most jumpers, sit with the operator for ten cycles with a stopwatch, and write down three numbers: seconds per jumper end to end, jumpers per board, and boards per day. Multiply seconds by jumpers by boards and divide by 3600. That is your daily hand-forming hours, and it is usually a shock. Then multiply by your loaded hourly wage and by twelve months of working days, and you have the annual figure that a feeder has to beat. Do not include quality, rework or scrap yet. This is the floor, not the ceiling.

Now put the same board in front of a wire feeder and watch what changes. The wire is loaded as a coil of up to 2 kg. The feeder unwinds it, straightens it through a set of adjusting and fixed wheels, indexes it with a motor and a ratchet, and cuts and forms each jumper in a single cam-driven stroke. The span and pin length come from the forming mould, and the feeding length comes from a coded switch. Nobody is measuring, nobody is bending, and nobody is deciding anything. The 1.5-second cycle is not the point - the point is that the cycle does not get tired at 3 a.m.

The first month decides whether the saving is real. Two settings will drift if you let them: the straightening nut, because a curled wire measures short, and the feeding length, because operators will adjust it to get a stubborn jumper through rather than stop the line. Write both settings on the inside of the machine cabinet, per jumper part number, and check the first part after every mould change. Log the change, not just the value - a setting with no author and no date is a setting you will have to rediscover.

Do the arithmetic on your own board before you talk to anyone about a price. If the annual hand-forming hours multiply out to something that pays a feeder back inside a year, you have a straightforward purchase. If they do not, you have a different problem - usually volume, or a board mix that changes too often for a fixed mould set - and that is worth knowing before the quotation arrives rather than after. INSIGHT 02Your jumper bench is a part number you keep re-ordering

Buying pre-formed jumpers hides the labour inside the price. Buying wire moves it back where you can see it.

Open your purchase orders and look for the jumper line. If you buy jumpers pre-formed, that line contains the wire, the forming labour of somebody else's factory, the packaging, the freight, the customs clearance and the shelf space - and then it repeats every month, forever, for a part that is a length of tinned copper wire with two bends in it.

The instinctive defence is that buying a finished part is simpler than making one. It is simpler to administer, and it is more expensive to own. The moment you accept a formed part into your building, you accept four things you cannot control: the supplier's forming tolerance, their lead time, their minimum order quantity, and their right to change the price. None of those appear as a line on a cost sheet, and all of them appear the first time a jumper geometry changes and you discover you are holding three months of the old one.

Compare the two cost structures honestly, on one page. On the buy side, put the unit price times annual quantity, plus the inventory carrying cost, plus the scrap of obsolete formed stock at each engineering change. On the make side, put the wire price per kilogram, plus the feeder, plus the moulds for the geometries you actually run, plus the amortised labour of the remaining supervision. The make side has a capital number and a very small running number. The buy side has no capital number and a running number that never stops. Which one wins depends entirely on how many months you keep running the same jumper - which is a fact you already know and your supplier does not.

What makes the make side practical rather than theoretical is the mould. On a wire feeder, span from 6 mm to 40 mm and pin length from 3 mm to 5 mm are properties of a replaceable forming mould - a front mould, a guide block and a rear mould - and the manual puts the disassembly and assembly at about five minutes. So a new jumper geometry is a mould and five minutes, not a new feeder and not a new purchase order. Order the moulds for the geometries you run, and keep them in the machine cabinet rather than in a store room.

The first month is where the make side either earns its place or does not. Track two things weekly: the weight of wire consumed against the count of jumpers produced, and the number of jumpers rejected for span or pin length. The first number should stay flat and predictable, because the feeder produces no waste - cutting, forming and feeding are integrated, so every millimetre of wire becomes a jumper. The second number should fall towards zero once the straightening setting is stable. If either drifts, you have a setting problem, not a machine problem.

Take one jumper part number - your highest runner - and cost it both ways on a single page. Most people find the crossover is well inside the first year, and that the real saving is not the unit price at all but the disappearance of the re-order. Then send us the part number and the drawing, and we will tell you what the make side actually costs for that geometry.

INSIGHT 03Six millimetres to forty: the span question that decides the feeder

Span is the first thing to settle and the easiest thing to get wrong. Measure it on the board, not on the drawing.

The first jumper you take off a board is almost never the span you assumed. It looks like a 10 mm jumper, the drawing says 10 mm, and then the feeder forms a 10 mm jumper and it will not sit flat because the holes are on a diagonal or the board is thicker than the sample. Span is the single most common reason a wire feeder underperforms in its first week, and it is decided before the purchase order rather than after.

The obvious explanation is that the feeder is inaccurate. It usually is not. Pin span on a wire feeder is a property of the forming mould, which means it is a hard mechanical dimension - a mould either makes a 6 mm jumper or it does not, and it does not drift by fractions of a millimetre the way a hand-formed jig does. What actually happens is that the span was specified from a drawing rather than from a board, and a drawing can be a nominal dimension, a reference dimension, or a dimension that was correct two revisions ago.

Here is the audit, and it takes an afternoon. Take three boards from three different lots of the same product. For each board, pull one jumper and measure the hole centre-to-centre distance with a caliper - not the outside width of the jumper, the hole centres. Do this for every distinct jumper on the board, and write the results in a table with a column for the drawing value. Any row where the measured value and the drawing value disagree is a row you were about to specify wrongly. Then repeat the exercise on the second product you run most, because the answer that matters is not one span, it is the range of spans across your mix.

Now map that range onto the platform. The published window is 6 mm to 40 mm, which is wide enough to cover the great majority of through-hole jumper work - but the number to bring to a supplier is not the window, it is your range and your distribution. If 90% of your jumpers sit between 8 mm and 14 mm, say so, because the mould set is ordered per geometry and you want the moulds you will actually use rather than a full catalogue. If you have one outlier at 38 mm that runs twice a year, say that too - it may be cheaper to keep hand-forming that one than to tool for it.

The first month after installation, keep the measurement discipline. Check the first part after every mould change with the same caliper you used in the audit, and record the span next to the mould number. Within a few weeks you will have a table that tells you which mould makes which jumper, and that table is worth more than any specification sheet, because it is about your boards. It also makes the next changeover a lookup rather than a measurement.

Before you go any further, do the caliper audit on your two highest-volume products and write down the span range. Then send us the range, the pin length and the wire diameter, and we will come back with the mould list rather than a generic quotation. It is a two-hour job that prevents the most expensive mistake in the whole project.

INSIGHT 04Wire diameter, span, pin length: three numbers before the purchase order

A wire feeder is a contract about a diameter, a span and a length. Get all three wrong and the machine is correct and useless.

There is a particular kind of expensive silence that happens three weeks after a feeder is installed, when the first engineer says out loud that the wire they bought is not the wire the feeder was set up for. It is rarely a dramatic error. It is a 1.3 mm wire against a published window of 0.5 to 1.2 mm, or a pin length of 2.8 mm against a documented 3 to 5 mm. Small numbers, complete stoppage.

The obvious explanation is that the machine is too narrow in its specification. That gets it backwards. A wire feeder is a forming tool, and a forming tool has to be matched to the material it forms - the same way a press tool is matched to a sheet thickness. The published band of 0.5 to 1.2 mm is not a limitation somebody forgot to widen; it is the band inside which the feeding module, the straightening wheels and the forming die all behave predictably. The failure mode is not that the feeder refuses to run a 1.3 mm wire. It is that it runs it, badly, and the badness shows up as span drift three shifts later.

Collect the three numbers properly, and collect them physically rather than from documents. Measure the wire diameter with a micrometer on a straight section, in three places, on the coil you actually intend to buy - not the coil in the sample kit. Measure the span as hole centre to hole centre on a real board, as described in the span audit. Measure the pin length as the length of lead that has to pass through the board plus the clinch allowance, and check it against the published relationship, where the pin length b equals c + d + 0.2. Write all three down with the product part number beside them.

Then present the three numbers as a table, one row per jumper, and treat the table as the specification. This is what the supplier is actually being asked to build, and it is the only artefact that lets them tell you yes or no honestly. A wire feeder that occupies one slot on a 24 V DC supply and needs no compressed air is easy to install; the difficulty was never installation, it was this table. If a row falls outside the published window, that row is a conversation about a customised variant, and it is a much cheaper conversation now than after the crate arrives.

The first month is about holding the three numbers still. Keep one coil of the specified wire as a reference coil, sealed, and use it to check any new delivery with the micrometer before the coil goes on the disc. This takes two minutes and it catches the supplier substitution that would otherwise be diagnosed as a feeder fault. Log the diameter against the batch number. If the span starts drifting, check the wire before you touch the straightener.

Build the table this week - one row per jumper, three columns, measured not copied. Then send it to us with your machine model, and we will tell you which rows are standard, which are mould options and which need a customised build. Three numbers, measured with a caliper and a micrometer, are the entire basis of a correct order.

INSIGHT 05Stop buying pre-formed jumpers. Start buying wire.

The switch is not about the feeder. It is about what you stop buying, storing and writing off.

Ask a plant manager how many jumper part numbers they hold and the answer is usually a pause. Ask how many they hold that were specified for a product they no longer build, and the pause gets longer. That is the real shape of the problem: not the cost of any one jumper, but the accumulation of formed parts that exist because changing a formed part means waiting for somebody else's factory.

The obvious explanation is that this is an inventory problem, and inventory problems are solved with a min-max calculation. They are not, in this case. A formed jumper is a part whose geometry is frozen at the moment of ordering, and every engineering change creates a new part number while the old stock becomes a write-off. No min-max setting fixes that, because the waste is not caused by ordering too much - it is caused by the part being unchangeable after the fact. What you actually want is the ability to change the geometry inside your own building, in minutes, without a purchase order.

Run a simple two-column audit on your jumper part numbers. In the left column, list every formed jumper you buy. In the right column, write the span, the pin length and the wire diameter. Then mark the rows where you have more than one geometry that differs only in span or pin length. Those marked rows are the ones a forming mould collapses into a single purchase. It is common to find eight or ten part numbers that reduce to three moulds, and that reduction is the saving - not the unit price, the part number count.

What replaces the part number list is a wire specification and a mould set. You buy one material - tinned copper wire, 0.5 to 1.2 mm, on coils up to 2 kg - and you hold moulds instead of parts. Moulds do not expire, they do not go obsolete at an engineering change, and they take about five minutes to change. The feeder itself occupies one slot, runs on 24 V DC and needs no compressed air, so there is no plant modification hiding in the project. The entire switch is a material change and a shelf change.

The first month, watch the material consumption rather than the machine. Weigh the coils in and count the jumpers out, and confirm that the ratio is stable - it should be, because the feeder produces no cutting waste. Then look at your stores and count how many jumper part numbers you have stopped ordering. That number is the honest measure of whether the switch worked, and it is a number your finance team will recognise immediately, unlike a cycle-time improvement.

Pick your ten most-used jumper part numbers, run the two-column audit, and see how many collapse into a mould set. If the answer is most of them, you have a material decision rather than a machine decision, and it is worth pricing properly. Send us the audit and we will tell you which moulds it takes.

INSIGHT 06From three shifts of hand-formed jumpers to one: the arithmetic

The saving is not a percentage. It is a specific number of operator hours, and you can calculate it today.

The statement that a feeder saves labour is true and useless. What a plant needs is a number: how many operator hours leave the building, on which shifts, and what those hours are then used for. Without that number the project is a preference, and preferences lose to capital committees.

The obvious explanation is that the saving is proportional to the jumper count. It is not, quite. Hand-forming has a fixed component and a variable component. The fixed component is the bench itself - the setup, the tray handling, the counting, the movement of finished jumpers - and it does not shrink when the jumper count halves. So the saving is not linear, and it is largest exactly where the bench is currently busiest: high jumper counts on long shifts. That is why the arithmetic has to be done on your board mix rather than on an industry average.

Build the calculation in four lines. Line one: seconds per jumper, measured with a stopwatch across ten cycles, multiplied by jumpers per board. Line two: that product multiplied by boards per day, divided by 3600 - this is the daily hours. Line three: daily hours multiplied by working days and the loaded hourly wage, for the annual value. Line four: the same daily hours multiplied by the share of jumpers the feeder takes over, which is the number that actually moves. Be conservative on that share; 80% is a fair planning figure for a well-specified wire feeder, and the remaining 20% is the awkward geometry you will keep doing by hand.

What you put in front of the machine is not just the feeder. It is the feeder with the mould set for the geometries you run, the wire specified by diameter rather than by supplier part number, and one person trained on the straightening adjustment and the coded switch. The feeder itself is deliberately undemanding: one slot, 24 V DC, no compressed air, no software licence, program import through a 15-pin plug. The training is the part that decides whether the 80% is real, because a feeder that nobody trusts gets bypassed at the first difficult jumper.

Re-measure at thirty days, not at twelve months. Count the operator hours actually spent on jumper work and compare them with the baseline you took with the stopwatch. If the reduction is less than you projected, the cause is almost always one of three things: moulds missing for a geometry that therefore still runs by hand, a straightening setting that drifts and forces rework, or an operator who has reverted to the old method for the parts they find fiddly. All three are fixable, and all three are invisible unless you re-measure.

Do the four-line calculation with your own stopwatch numbers, and write the projected operator hours on one side of a page and the thirty-day actual on the other. If the gap is small, the business case is closed. If it is large, tell us which mould was missing and we will fix the tooling rather than the story. Send us your four lines and we will check the arithmetic with you.

INSIGHT 07Coil weight, changeover time and the cost of a mould you did not order

The hidden costs in a wire feeding project are not the machine. They are the coil you cannot use and the mould you forgot.

Two failures account for most of the disappointment in a wire feeding project, and neither of them is a machine failure. The first is a coil that is too heavy for the disc or too light to be economic, discovered on the day of installation. The second is a jumper geometry that nobody remembered when the mould set was ordered, so it continues to run by hand and quietly eats a third of the projected saving.

The obvious explanation is that both are supplier problems. They are specification problems, and both are entirely predictable from documents that already exist. The published figure for coil weight is up to 2 kg including the material, which tells you the disc is sized for a hand-liftable coil rather than a bulk spool - so buying a 5 kg spool to save on unit price is a false economy that ends with the wire on the floor. And the mould list is a direct function of the span and pin length audit, which is a document you can produce in an afternoon before the order rather than after the installation.

Do both audits on the same day, because they use the same board walk. Walk your two highest-volume products and list every distinct jumper with its span and pin length, measured with a caliper. Group the list by span family and pin length. That grouped list is your mould order, and the count of groups is the number you should compare against the moulds you were planning to buy. Then, separately, look at your wire purchasing: what coil weights are available, what the minimum order is, and whether the coil you would naturally buy fits inside the published 2 kg limit.

Specify the material by diameter and coil weight rather than by a supplier's part number, and put the coil limit in writing in the purchase order. On the machine side, order the moulds for the grouped geometries, plus one spare for your highest runner, and keep them in the machine cabinet rather than in the store. The change itself is about five minutes of disassembly and assembly, so a mould you own is a changeover and a mould you do not own is a stopped line. That asymmetry is the whole reason the mould list deserves the same attention as the machine.

In the first month, keep a one-line log of every mould change: date, mould number, jumper part number, first-part inspection result. Within a few weeks this tells you which moulds you actually use and which you ordered on a guess, and it gives you the evidence to order the two or three you missed. Revisit the log at ninety days and re-order the mould set against real usage rather than the original projection. It is the cheapest correction available in the whole project.

Produce the grouped mould list and the coil specification on the same page, and check both against the published limits before you order anything. Then send us that page with your machine model, and we will confirm the mould set and the coil specification in one reply. Two hours of measurement prevents the two failures that cause most of the disappointment.

INSIGHT 08Nine questions to ask a wire feeder supplier before the purchase order

The answers tell you whether you are buying a machine or a drawing. Ask them in writing and keep the replies.

By the time a wire feeder project reaches a purchase order, most of the risk is already locked in - not in the price, but in the answers that were never asked for in writing. The specification sheet is a starting point, and a starting point is exactly what it sounds like. What separates a straightforward installation from a difficult one is a short list of questions, and the value of the list is that it makes the gaps visible while they are still cheap to close.

The obvious explanation is that a published specification sheet settles everything. It does not, and the most useful thing a specification sheet can do is admit what it does not contain. Our own SJF1001 sheet, for example, publishes the model, the dimensions, the power requirement, the absence of an air requirement, the feeding speed, the wire diameter band, the span and pin length windows, the coil weight, the control architecture and the mechanism of each module - and it does not publish the weight of the current body, because that number was carried over on a legacy sheet for a different chassis. A supplier who tells you that is easier to work with than one whose sheet appears complete and is not.

Ask these nine, in writing, and keep the replies with the order. One: what is the exact wire diameter band, and what happens at the edges? Two: what is the span range, and is each span a mould or an adjustment? Three: what is the pin length range, and how is it set? Four: what is the maximum coil weight, and does that include the material? Five: how long does a geometry change take, measured not estimated? Six: is compressed air required, and if so at what pressure and consumption? Seven: what is the program import interface, and does it need a laptop? Eight: what are the consumables, and what is their service life? Nine: what is the documented spare-parts lead time, in writing?

The answers you are looking for are specific and mechanical. A span that is a mould is a span you can change in about five minutes; a span that is an adjustment is a span somebody will adjust wrongly. No air requirement means one less line, one less filter-regulator and one less class of fault. A program import through a 15-pin plug means no software licence and no dongle. And a documented one-day spare-parts lead time means the difference between a consumable and a stopped line. None of these are features you can see in a photograph of the machine, and all of them decide what year three looks like.

Keep the answers as an appendix to the machine file, and revisit them at the first annual review. Suppliers change, part numbers change, and a reply from two years ago is not a commitment today. Then compare the answers against what you have actually experienced: if the documented changeover time was five minutes and yours takes twenty, that is a training or tooling gap worth fixing rather than a supplier to replace. The document is most useful as a baseline for your own performance, not as a weapon.

Send us the nine answers you have collected, including the ones where the reply was that the value is not published, and we will fill in the gaps for our own feeder in writing. It is a short conversation, and it is the cheapest due diligence available before a purchase order - because a supplier who can answer all nine precisely is a supplier who has built the machine more than once.

13 - FAQ

The questions we are actually asked

Straight answers, including the ones that end with us telling you not to buy.

What exactly does the SJF1001 feed?
Coiled tinned copper wire, 0.5-1.2 mm in diameter, in rolls weighing up to 2 kg including the material. It unwinds, straightens, feeds, cuts and forms the wire into jumper wires with a pin span of 6-40 mm and a pin length of 3-5 mm. It does not feed pre-formed jumpers, taped components or bulk parts - it makes the jumper from wire.
Do I need compressed air?
No. The specification sheet lists the air source requirement as none, and the manual confirms it. The feeder runs on 24 V DC at 150 W. On a machine bank that is one less line to route, one less filter-regulator to service and one less class of fault to chase.
How fast is it, and what takt should I plan?
The published feeding speed is 1.5 seconds per piece or faster. In practice the insertion head sets the takt - the feeder's job is to never starve it. Plan the takt from your head and your board, and treat the feeding speed as headroom.
How do I change from one jumper to another?
You change the forming mould. Span and pin length are properties of the mould - a front mould, a guide block and a rear mould - and the manual puts the disassembly and assembly at about five minutes. The feeding length is then set on the digital coded switch. This is why one feeder covers a family of jumpers instead of one.
Is there any waste?
The manual states that the feeder does not produce waste, because cutting, forming and automatic feeding are integrated. You buy wire by weight and every millimetre of it becomes a jumper. There is no paper tape to dispose of, unlike a taped-component feeder.
What happens when the wire runs out or jams?
The buckle position is monitored by a photoelectric switch, which is what tells the unwinding motor to replenish the reserve length. A fault is annunciated by the indicator light on the panel and cleared with the reset button once the cause is removed. Most feeding problems are set-up problems: wire that was not straightened, or a feeding length that was not set for the geometry.
What should I keep on the shelf?
Keep the forming moulds for the geometries you actually run, and the wear items your own log identifies. Beyond that, rely on the documented 1-day spare-parts lead time rather than building a warehouse. Log the straightener setting and the feeding length per jumper part number; the cheapest spare part is a setting you can find again.
