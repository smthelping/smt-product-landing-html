01 — Why it matters

A hand tally is a wage bill you pay every month for a number you already own

Every SMT warehouse has the same scene: a reel, a counting tray, a person with a pen, and a stock figure nobody fully trusts. The count is not hard, it is just endless — and it repeats every month, on every reel, for the whole life of the plant. The S-902 turns that repeating labour into one pass over the tape.

01

Counting is labour that scales with inventory, not with orders

More stock means more reels means more counting hours. The work does not shrink when orders shrink, so the cost sits there through every slow quarter. The manual's own description of the alternative is blunt: automatic calculation of the component number, convenient for warehouse counting and material issuing.

02

Hand counts disagree with each other, and with the stock system

Two people counting the same reel by hand can end a hundred pieces apart, and neither is lying. An optical count follows the guide holes, so the same reel gives the same number twice. The manual states the design intent plainly: zero error.

03

The real damage is the wrong number, not the lost minutes

A short count stops the mounter mid-order. An over-count hides a shortage until the last panel. Either way the line pays for a figure that was already wrong in the warehouse. Counting at the source — before the reel is issued — is the only place the error is still cheap.

The S-902 document states the machine is designed to automatically count and calculate SMD components, and that it suits material management and warehouse management at an SMT factory. That is the scope of this page.

02 — How it counts

Six steps from a loaded reel to a printed label

The cycle is short by design: set the pitch, load the tape, start the motor, read the number. Nothing on this page is a claim about speed in components per hour — the manufacturer does not publish one, and we will not invent it.

01

Set the pitch

Press the pitch key and enter the tape pitch — 02, 04, 08 and so on. Pitch is the distance between two components, and it is the only number the counter needs to convert guide-hole pulses into a component count.

02

Choose the mode

Automatic (A) for issuing a set quantity — the motor stops itself at the target. Manual (M) for pure stock-taking, where you stop the machine yourself.

03

Load and align

Clip the tape between the blocking plate and the counting gear and line the first component up with the V-notch. The anti-strip-off design in the manual is there to keep the tape from being damaged on the way through.

04

Zero the display

Pull the tape by hand and the counting gear turns. Press stop/clear for two seconds and the display returns to zero, so the manual pull does not enter the count.

05

Count

Start clockwise or anticlockwise depending on which tray the reel sits on. The count runs positive in one direction and negative in the other, so you can wind a reel back without losing the total.

06

Read, print, close

The quantity on the display is the reel quantity. With the optional printer and scanner, press print and the label carries the quantity — and the scanned material barcode if you scanned one.

What the counter physically is

Optical fibre sensing

A fibre-optic head reads the guide holes in the carrier tape. The product document and the manual describe the same principle: components are counted from the correspondence between the tape's guide holes and the parts.

Tray axis for reel mounting

Two tray arms carry the reel. The document labels the tray axis for reel mount and a screw for arm adjustment, so the arms can be spread and locked to suit the reel.

Width adjustment by handle

A handle sets the tape path width, which is how one machine accepts tape from 4 mm up to 56 mm wide.

Leak-detection fibre head

On the leak-detection variant a second fibre pair sits across the tape. The manual's adjustment procedure is specific: bring the two heads level with the component pocket, then trim the amplifier until the indicator just turns from red to green over an empty pocket.

Blocking plate with V-notch

The first component is aligned to the V-notch in the blocking plate before counting starts. The manual treats this as the alignment reference for both stock-taking and issuing.

Counting gear and pressing block

The tape runs between the blocking plate and the counting gear, held down by a pressing block so it cannot bounce while the tray moves.

ARTICLE 01The number your warehouse argues about every month

Two people, one reel, two different answers. The problem is not the people.

Article body text is published in English. Titles and standfirsts are translated.

Two people count the same reel. One says 4,180. The other says 4,096. Neither is lying, neither is careless, and the difference is now sitting in a stock system that will be wrong for the next three months.

Sit with that for a second, because it is the real problem in SMT material control and almost nobody names it. The warehouse does not lose parts. It loses confidence. Once two counts of one reel disagree, every number downstream becomes a guess with a decimal point attached, and the planner starts adding buffer that the company pays for in working capital.

The audit is four lines, and you can run it on a single shift. One: pick ten reels at random and count each twice, by two different people, without telling either what the other found. Two: record the spread, not the average. Three: multiply the typical spread by your reel volume per month. Four: convert that into the value of stock that is being carried purely to cover the error. Most warehouses find the spread is small per reel and enormous per year, and that the buffer stock they blame on suppliers is actually covering their own counting.

The fix is to stop counting parts and start counting something the parts cannot argue about. Carrier tape is punched with guide holes at a fixed pitch. An optical counter like the S-902 reads those holes, converts them through the pitch setting, and reports the quantity — so the same reel gives the same answer on Monday and on Friday, to whoever is holding it. The manual states the intent without decoration: zero error.

The compounding effect is quieter than the saving. Once counts agree, the buffer stock you were holding against counting error can be reduced on purpose instead of by accident. Planners stop padding. Audits take an afternoon instead of a week. None of that shows up in a machine quotation, and all of it shows up in the year-end inventory number.

A counting machine is one of the few purchases in an SMT plant where the payback arithmetic fits on a napkin. That is exactly why it is worth doing properly — the number is easy to get wrong in the direction of optimism.

Start with the honest version of the cost. Not the minutes a person spends turning a reel, but the whole envelope: the counting itself, the walk to the counting station and back, the re-count when the first number is doubted, the reconciliation meeting when the count disagrees with the system, and the buffer stock that exists because the counts are not trusted. ARTICLE 03Why reading the guide holes beats reading the label

Reel labels are written by whoever filled the reel. Guide holes are not.

Article body text is published in English. Titles and standfirsts are translated.

There is a label on every reel in your warehouse, and there is a moment in the life of that reel when the label stops being evidence and becomes an assumption.

The label was written when the reel was filled. It might have been written by a machine that counts accurately, or by a person who estimated, or by a supplier who copied the previous label. It may say 5,000 because the reel was sold as a 5,000-piece reel, which is a packaging convention and not a measurement. The moment a partial reel comes back from the line, the label is simply wrong, and everyone in the building knows it.

The audit takes one afternoon. Pull twenty reels that have been issued and returned at least once. Count each one properly. Compare the result against the label. Then sort the differences into three buckets: reels where the label was never a measurement, reels where the label was right when it was written and is now stale, and reels where nobody can reconstruct what happened. Most warehouses find the third bucket is the one that hurts, because it means the reel's history is unrecoverable.

Guide holes are different in kind. The tape is punched at manufacture with holes at a fixed pitch, and the relationship between holes and components is set by the packaging standard, not by anyone's estimate. An optical counter reads the holes as the tape runs past the fibre head — the S-902 document describes the machine as counting from the correspondence between the components and the guide holes on the carrier tape. The label becomes a cross-check instead of the source of truth.

This changes how you handle returns. A reel that comes back from the line gets counted in the same minute it is received, and the new number is printed on a new label, which is stuck over the old one. The warehouse system then holds a measured quantity with a timestamp rather than an inherited assumption. After a few months the label you trust and the label you verify stop being the same object, and the reconciliation arguments stop.

Take five reels that have been returned at least twice and count them properly. If more than one disagrees with its label, that is your evidence, and it is better evidence than anything we could show you. Send us the five numbers and we will tell you what the pattern means.

ARTICLE 04The empty pocket that stops your mounter at midnight

A gap in the tape is cheap to find in the warehouse and expensive to find on the line.

Article body text is published in English. Titles and standfirsts are translated.

An empty pocket in a carrier tape costs about nothing to find in the warehouse and a great deal to find at three in the morning on a running line.

Here is how the expensive version happens. A reel is issued for a job that needs 4,000 pieces. Somewhere in the middle of the tape, three consecutive pockets are empty — a fill error at the component supplier, or a pocket that was emptied during a previous partial run. The counter counts through the gaps without noticing, because a guide-hole counter counts holes and the holes are all still there. The reel goes to the line. The mounter reaches the gap during the night shift, and the line stops while somebody works out whether the machine has a fault or the reel does.

The audit is blunt: take ten reels from your incoming inspection queue and inspect the tape for gaps over a metre or two at each end. Then ask a harder question — how would you have found a gap in the middle? Most plants answer that they would not, and that they rely on the mounter to discover it. That is a plan for finding the gap at its most expensive.

A second fibre pair across the tape solves this at the point of counting. The S-902's leak-detection variant compares the light reaching the receiver through an empty pocket against the light blocked by a seated component, and when it sees a gap it alarms and stops the machine. The manual's diagnostic rule is worth remembering: if the machine alarms over components, the amplifier signal is too strong; if it runs past empty pockets, the signal is too weak. It is a two-minute adjustment, and it is the difference between a useful function and a nuisance.

Once gaps are caught at the counter, the value compounds in a way that has nothing to do with the gap. Incoming verification becomes meaningful — you are checking the supplier, not just recording the delivery. Issued reels carry a verified quantity and a verified continuity. And the night shift stops being the place where material problems are discovered, which is worth more than the counting time you saved.

Count ten reels from your incoming queue and check the tape for gaps. If you find any, tell us how many and where they were. That single number decides whether empty-pocket detection belongs in your configuration, and we would rather you bought it for a reason than as a line item.

ARTICLE 05Closing the gap between the counter and the stock system

A counted number that has to be retyped is a counted number you will lose.

Article body text is published in English. Titles and standfirsts are translated.

A counted number that has to be written down and retyped is a counted number you are going to lose. Not always, and not immediately — but often enough that the whole exercise becomes hard to defend.

Picture the actual sequence in a warehouse that counts by hand. Somebody counts a reel and writes the figure on a slip. The slip goes to a desk. The figure is typed into the warehouse system, from a slip, by a person who did not do the counting and cannot see the reel. At each handover there is an opportunity for the number to change, and no point at which anyone can prove it did not. When the figure later disagrees with reality, there is no way to tell whether the count was wrong or the transcription was.

The audit is a single question asked about a single month: pick five reels where the recorded quantity turned out to be wrong, and trace each one backwards to find where the number entered the system and who typed it. If you cannot complete that trace for even one reel, your problem is not counting accuracy — it is that your counting process has no evidence trail.

The counter and the system have to meet at the point of counting. The S-902's optional function set is built exactly for this: a scanner reads the material barcode on the reel, the counter produces the quantity, and the label printer prints both onto a label that goes on the reel. The document notes that if you do not scan, the printed label carries the quantity barcode only — so the traceability is optional, and it is the option most worth having.

With that in place the number has a physical carrier. The label on the reel is the record. The warehouse system is a copy of the label rather than a separate claim about it. Audits become a matter of reading labels, and a discrepancy becomes an investigation with a starting point rather than an argument with no beginning. That change is worth more than the labour saving, and it is the one that survives staff turnover.

Ask your warehouse one question: if a reel's quantity turned out to be wrong last month, could you say who entered the figure and from what? If the answer is no, the barcode and printer option is not a nice-to-have. Tell us your reel volume and we will quote it as part of the machine rather than as an afterthought.

ARTICLE 06Choosing between optical and X-ray component counting

One unwinds the tape. One looks through it. The right answer depends on what you are protecting.

Article body text is published in English. Titles and standfirsts are translated.

Two machines, both called component counters, both promising an exact number, and a price difference large enough that the choice matters. The difference is not quality. It is what each one can see.

Start with what is actually being asked. A reel arrives and you need to know how many parts are on it. There are two physically different ways to answer. You can run the tape past a sensor and count the pockets as they go by. Or you can look through the reel without opening it and count the parts where they sit. One requires the tape to move. The other requires the tape to be transparent enough to see through, which carrier tape usually is.

Ask three questions about your own operation. First, do you need to count reels that are still sealed, or reels that have come back from the line? Sealed reels favour the see-through method; returned partial reels are easy either way. Second, what is the value of the components on a typical reel? On low-value passives a counting error is an annoyance; on an expensive IC it is a write-off. Third, how many reels per day — because the method that is slower per reel becomes the wrong answer at volume.

Optical counting is the simpler machine and it is priced accordingly. The S-902 runs the tape past a fibre head, converts guide holes into a count, and gives you a range of ±99,999 pieces, a 600 mm maximum reel, eighteen speed levels and an optional gap-detection head. It suits issuing, kitting and day-to-day stock-taking — the work that happens to every reel in the building. X-ray counting, as in the AX6000 and ADL100 documents on this page, images the reel and counts the parts inside, which suits incoming verification and high-value reels where disturbing the packaging is itself a cost.

Most warehouses do not need to choose. The reels that get counted every day are cheap and numerous, and the reels that need looking through are few and expensive. A plant that runs an optical counter for the routine volume and sends the high-value incoming reels for X-ray verification spends a fraction of what an all-X-ray policy costs, and gets a better answer on the reels that matter. That is a routing decision, not a purchasing decision, and it is the one worth making first.

ARTICLE 07Set the target, walk away, come back to a printed label

Issuing a set quantity is a different job from counting a whole reel.

Article body text is published in English. Titles and standfirsts are translated.

Counting a whole reel and issuing a set quantity are two different jobs, and the machine settings that make one pleasant make the other useless.

Watch the difference. Stock-taking asks: how many are on this reel? You do not care how long it takes, you care that the answer is right, and you might not know when the tape will end. Issuing asks: give me exactly 3,000 pieces for job 4471. You care about the number stopping at 3,000, and you care that nobody has to stand there watching it. One job wants accuracy at the end of an unknown length. The other wants a target and a hand free.

The audit question is how many times a day your warehouse issues a partial reel. If the answer is more than a handful, you are running an issuing operation with stock-taking tools, and the cost shows up as an operator standing next to a machine that is doing something they cannot influence. Time it once — the whole cycle, reel in to label printed — and multiply by the daily count.

Target quantity and auto-stop are the settings that split the two jobs. In automatic mode you preset the quantity and the motor stops itself when the count reaches it; the S-902 document lists this as a standard function, and auto stopping at the last counted component as an option. The manual adds the field detail that causes most of the confusion: in manual mode the preset value has no effect at all, which is why operators who forget to switch modes conclude the machine is broken.

Once issuing runs unattended, the shape of the warehouse day changes. The operator loads a reel, sets the target, presses start and goes to do something else — receiving, put-away, another reel. Counting stops being a task that occupies a person and becomes a background process that produces a printed label. The labour figure moves, but the more useful change is that issuing a partial reel stops being expensive enough to avoid, so the warehouse stops issuing whole reels to avoid the paperwork.

Ask these and the specification sheet stops being a brochure.

Article body text is published in English. Titles and standfirsts are translated.

A component counter is a small machine with a short specification sheet, which is exactly why buyers stop asking questions too early. The questions below are the ones that separate a counter that pays for itself from one that ends up in a corner with a dust sheet on it.

The reason the questions matter is structural. A counter sits between two systems that both believe they are authoritative: the warehouse stock record and the production plan. A machine that produces a number nobody records has added nothing. A machine that produces a number in the wrong unit, or for the wrong tape, or that stops the operator every ten minutes, has added a cost. The specification sheet will not tell you which one you are buying. These questions will.

Run them as a checklist with whoever will actually use the machine. First: what tape widths do we really run, including the odd ones — because a counter specified for 8 to 56 mm is a different machine from one specified for 4 to 56 mm. Second: what is our largest reel diameter, measured, not estimated. Third: do we need to count sealed reels or returned partial reels. Fourth: how many reels a day, across all shifts. Fifth: does our warehouse system accept a barcode, or will the number be retyped. Sixth: who will be blamed when a count is wrong, and can that person prove what they did. Seventh: what happens at a gap in the tape. Eighth: what is the machine's role on the day it is not counting — spare, or shared. Ninth: what is the payback, in our own wage numbers.

Compare the answers against what the machine actually offers. The S-902 covers pitch from 1 mm and tape width from 4 mm in the product document, up to 600 mm reels, with the range and both directions standard, and empty-pocket detection, barcode reading and label printing as options. Note where our own documents disagree — the operation manual lists a narrower tape range — and ask us about it directly rather than assuming. That is what the evidence section on this page is for.

Buying on the ninth answer rather than the first is what makes the difference. A counter bought because it is cheap and counts fast will be judged on whether it counts fast. A counter bought because it removes a measurable monthly labour cost and closes a traceability gap will be judged on that, and it will pass. The specification sheet is the same in both cases.

14 — Questions

The questions buyers actually ask

Which components can the S-902 count?
Any SMD component supplied on carrier tape. The product document lists capacitors, resistors, diodes, ICs, inductors, USB connectors and LED beads. The limit is the tape, not the part: pitch from 1 mm to 56 mm and tape width from 4 mm to 56 mm, both customizable. Note that the operation manual lists a narrower set — pitch from 2 mm and width from 8 mm. Both ranges appear in the evidence table above.
What is the counting range?
−99,999 to +99,999 pieces, and this is one of the few parameters on which every source agrees. Counting in the reverse direction displays as a negative number, which is how you wind a reel back without losing the running total.
What is the difference between automatic and manual mode?
Automatic (A) mode is for issuing a set quantity: you preset a number and the motor stops when the count reaches it. Manual (M) mode is for stock-taking, where you stop the machine yourself. The manual notes that in manual mode the preset value has no effect — a detail that causes most of the confusion in the field.
How does empty-pocket detection work?
On the leak-detection variant, a fibre-optic pair sits across the tape and compares the light reaching the receiver through an empty pocket against the light blocked by a seated component. When it sees a gap it alarms and stops. The manual gives an amplifier adjustment procedure, and also a diagnostic rule: if the machine alarms over components, the signal is too strong; if it runs past empty pockets, the signal is too weak.
Is the count really exact?
The optical count follows the guide holes, and the manual states the design intent as zero error. It also states an honest caveat: when the machine stops, inertia can leave the count about one piece either side of the preset target, and you can rotate the tray by hand to land exactly on the number. Treat the ±1 as a stopping effect, not as a counting error.
Can it print labels and read barcodes?
Yes, as an option. A scanner reads the material barcode on the reel and a label printer prints the counted quantity. If you do not scan, the printed label carries the quantity barcode only. This is the function that keeps a counted number from being retyped into the warehouse system.
What power and air does it need?
The S-902 needs no compressed air. On power, the three documents disagree in wording but agree in substance: the product document marks AC 220 V / AC 100 V at 50/60 Hz, the operation manual describes a universal AC 70–260 V input drawing 40 W, and the customer nameplate is marked 100–240 VAC. Confirm the marking on your own unit before installation.
Should I buy an optical counter or an X-ray counter?
Optical counting — the S-902 — reads the tape as it runs, so it is fast and inexpensive and it fits issuing and day-to-day stock-taking. X-ray counting reads through a sealed reel without unwinding it, which suits incoming verification and high-value reels where you do not want to disturb the packaging. Southern Machinery supplies both routes; tell us what you are protecting and we will recommend one rather than both.
