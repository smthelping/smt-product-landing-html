01 — Why it matters

The residue you cannot see is the failure you will pay for

After wave soldering or reflow, a board can look perfectly clean and still carry rosin films, no-clean flux residues, solder balls and hand-solder flux. Those residues drive corrosion, electromigration, poor probe contact in ICT and coating delamination after conformal coating. Cleaning is not cosmetic — it is a reliability step, and doing it in-line keeps it repeatable.

01

Residues cause failures weeks later

Rosin and no-clean flux residues are hygroscopic. Under humidity and bias they support electrochemical migration — dendrites that short pads apart long after the board left your line. The field failure costs the board, the assembly, the warranty claim and the customer.

02

Manual scrubbing is not a process

A bench brush and solvent removes what the operator had time to reach. There is no logged temperature, no controlled pressure, no repeatable rinse. Two shifts produce two different cleanliness levels on the same product — and ion contamination is invisible until it fails.

03

Batch cleaning breaks the flow

Offline basket machines stop boards between processes and add handling. An in-line cleaner sits on the conveyor itself: boards flow from solder to clean without queues, without re-staging and without a second touch that damages components.

02 — The process

Nine sections, one pass, zero re-handling

Boards enter on the SUS304 net conveyor and travel through nine functional sections in a single pass. Chemistry is applied where it works best — immersion and high-pressure spray for flux removal, staged DI-water rinses to recover ions and chemicals, air knives to stop carryover, and hot air to leave the board dry. Section lengths below are quoted from the 2021 manual.

01

Pre-wash · 380 mm

Water-based cleaning liquid is sprayed top and bottom through 2 + 2 SUS304 spray bars to immerse and soften flux and dust, lowering the work the main wash has to do. The pre-wash shares the 240 L wash tank and recirculates through a steel filter net plus a 1 µm filter barrel.

02

Wash · 560 mm

The main wash: 6 upper and 6 lower spray bars with 72 nozzles form a liquid curtain at 2–8 bar, peeled off by a 5.5 kW SUS304 pump. The 36 kW heater brings the liquid to up to 80 °C, which is what actually dissolves rosin and no-clean residue films.

03

Chemical isolation · 360 mm

Three SUS304 air knives on a 10 HP blower blow wash liquid off the board and back into the wash tank. This is the section that keeps chemistry out of the rinse stages — it protects rinse water quality and pulls the liquid carryover cost back down.

04

Pre-rinse · 400 mm

DI water in a 60 L tank, sprayed through 3 + 3 bars (36 nozzles) at 2–5 bar, takes the remaining liquid film off the board and keeps it out of the rinse stages. Pre-rinse water is continuously refreshed by overflow from the rinse tank.

05

Rinse · 240 mm

The main rinse: a 40 L heated DI-water tank (12 kW, up to 60 °C) and upper/lower spray bars wash ions and particles off the pad geometry. Rinse water recirculates through a steel filter net and is refreshed from the final-rinse overflow.

Section lengths, tank capacities, nozzle counts and temperatures are quoted from the SME-6300 manual (2021), chapter 3.

Why the build is stainless

SUS304 frame, argon-arc welded

No PP or PVC structure parts. The frame resists hot alkaline chemistry, hot water and the mechanical load of a full production line without warping or ageing.

SUS316 heaters

Immersion-type SUS316 heaters in the wash and rinse tanks bring liquid to temperature quickly with ±3 °C PID control, so chemistry works at the temperature that was validated.

Mismatched nozzle rows

Nozzles sit vertically on each bar and are offset between neighbouring bars, so the spray curtain covers component shadows instead of leaving dry patches behind tall parts.

Quick-clamp spray bars

Every spray bar lifts out without tools for cleaning or nozzle changes — maintenance time shows up as production hours, not weekend work.

Tilted tank floors

Water tanks are tilted to the drain so they empty fully at changeover. No standing water sitting in a corner between runs.

Observation glass everywhere

Inner and outer doors carry windows so operators watch the spray, rinse and dry stages live; good seals keep water and vapour inside the machine.

The control system protects the board and the operator

Recipes, not guesswork

Conveyor speed, spray pressures, liquid and rinse temperatures and hot-air temperature are set on the touch panel and can be saved per product.

Alarms with addresses

Three-colour stack, buzzer and on-screen fault text. Board-drop detection at input and output, low-water heater protection and DI resistivity alarms all name the cause.

Soft starts and UPS run-out

Electric devices start two seconds apart to cut inrush current; a UPS keeps the conveyor running so loaded boards exit safely if power drops.

MES-ready (option)

An optional MES interface lets the line log cleaning as a real process step — recipe used, temperatures held, alarms raised.

05 — Options

Configure the machine around your line

The SME-6300 ships as a complete in-line cleaner. These are the documented options and standard extras worth deciding on before you order — the first four are factory options; the last two come with the machine.

Options quoted from documents

DI water processor, 1000 L/h

Turns tap water into ≥ 10 MΩ rinse water on the spot, with conductivity monitored below 10 µS/cm. Sized so the final rinse never starves the machine.

BGA water knives

Air- and water-knife arrangements for underside cleaning of bottom-terminated components — the spray reaches the joint shadow where a flat curtain cannot.

MES interface

Logs every cleaned board as a process record: recipe, temperatures, alarms. For automotive and medical EMS customers who must prove the step happened.

English operator interface

The machine ships with a Chinese touch interface as standard; an English operator interface is an option for overseas lines and training.

Automatic liquid dosing

Standard: a diaphragm-pump dosing system adds concentrated cleaning liquid at set times and quantities, so bath strength never drifts between manual top-ups.

Double filtration

Standard: steel filter nets plus 1 µm filter barrels on the wash circuits keep solder balls and particles out of the spray, protecting nozzles and freshly cleaned boards.

06 — Installation

What your floor needs before the truck arrives

The installation layout document defines exactly what the machine expects. Plan these six items and the commissioning day is a formality; miss one and the machine waits.

STEP 1

Site and environment

Hard industrial floor, 0–30 °C, humidity below 85 % with no condensation, no direct sunlight, away from heat sources and vibration. Reserve L 8000 × W 4000 × H 2500 mm around the machine footprint, with at least 1.0 m access at front and sides for liquid change-out and service.

STEP 2

Power

AC 380 V / 50 Hz, three-phase five-wire with a forced yellow-green ground. Phase conductors 70 mm², neutral and ground 35 mm², and a 250 A distribution box beside the machine. Total connected load about 110 kW.

STEP 3

Compressed air

0.5–0.7 MPa of dry clean air, 200–400 L/min, brought in on a Φ12 mm main line. The air knives and blow dryers run on it continuously.

STEP 4

DI water inlet

One 1-inch female DI inlet at ≥ 0.3 MPa, 10–15 L/min, resistivity ≥ 10 MΩ. If you do not already make DI water, the optional 1000 L/h processor covers the machine and its margin.

STEP 5

Drainage

Two 2-inch drain connections: one for waste cleaning liquid, one for waste rinse water. Keep the cleaning-liquid line out of the general drain unless you have a waste-chemical collection pool.

STEP 6

Exhaust and hoisting

Three Φ250 mm top vents ducted to an extraction fan with a volume damper. The machine arrives on casters at about 2.5–3 t — move it with a lift platform and the floor casters, never by ropes or a single forklift.

ARTICLE 01

Pick up the last ten components your mounter rejected and lay them on the bench. Now pick up the nozzle that was active when they were rejected and look into its bore under a loupe. The reason this keeps happening is that a clogging nozzle fails politely. Vacuum does not disappear; it sags. Somewhere in your factory there is an ultrasonic bath, and someone sincerely believes it is handling the nozzles. It is not — and the proof is hiding in plain sight. Southern Machinery's own comparison tests show 110-type and 225-type nozzles that went through ultrasonic cleaning and came out with the bore still blocked, then went through a water-mist jet cycle and came out fully clear. Same dirty nozzle, same water, opposite result.

The physics explains it. Ultrasonic cleaning works by cavitation — micro-bubbles forming and collapsing against a surface. Cavitation is powerful on flat, exposed areas and weak in narrow channels, because the bubbles simply cannot sustain the pressure cycle inside a bore a few tenths of a millimeter wide. A placement nozzle is the worst possible geometry for it: a long, narrow tube with the dirt packed at the bottom. That is why the "after" photos from an ultrasonic bath always look clean at the tip and half-open in the throat.

The water-mist jet inverts the geometry. The SME24 fragments pure DI water into an ultra-fine mist and drives it at sonic speed — about 360 meters per second — straight through the bore, so the cleaning energy travels exactly where the dirt lives. Because each nozzle sits in its own independent cleaning device, the jet never strikes neighboring nozzles, and the coating layer and reflector panel come through untouched. Company testing after six minutes shows HITACHI, YAMAHA and Panasonic nozzles fully clear, inside and out.

There is a second, quieter advantage: what the process does not use. No solvent, no detergent, no ultrasonic bath water loaded with flux that finds its way back onto the next batch. The SME24 runs on DI or distilled water alone, discharges the used water automatically, and asks for nothing else — 150 cc per hour of water and clean compressed air.

So the method is simple: keep the ultrasonic bath for what it is good at, and give the nozzles a machine built for their geometry. Ask most line engineers how a nozzle cleaner works and you will get "it sprays water, I think." That vagueness is why cheap cleaners get bought and then quietly abandoned. The cycle is worth knowing precisely, because each of its five steps exists to protect the next one — and because when you understand it, you can tune it to your flux in minutes.

Step one: bottom wash in DI water. The jet enters the bore from below, where gravity is on your side, softening the flux film and pushing it upward and out. Step two: top wash — the spray field turns to the nozzle tops and clears the seat surfaces and the pickup face, where the film is thickest and where it does the most damage to vacuum seal. Company test data at about 0.5 MPa shows a 110-type nozzle clear in roughly one minute; a larger 225-type takes about two.

Step three is where the machine earns its keep: the bottom air blast. Pulsed compressed air — rhythm adjustable from one to ten seconds on the touch panel — shatters the softened residue mechanically and drives it out of the bore. This is momentum, not chemistry, which is exactly why the anti-reflective coating and the reflector panel survive thousands of cycles. Step four flips to the top air blast to dry the outer surfaces and push remaining droplets out of the smallest apertures, down to 01005 size. Step five is a final bottom pass and the automatic drain: the used DI water leaves the machine by itself, and the nozzles are dry — no wipe-down, no compressed-air hose at the bench.

The whole sequence runs 3–6 minutes of washing plus 1–3 minutes of drying on the SME24, for up to 24 nozzles per cycle. Notice what is missing: any step that depends on operator skill. The order is fixed, the pressures are set, the drain is automatic. That is what makes the result repeatable across shifts — the property no manual process has ever had.

Your mounter has been keeping a diary, and most factories never read it. The pickup-rate trend in the machine's own logs is the most honest account of nozzle health you own — more honest than any visual inspection, because it is measured on every single pickup cycle, thousands of times a shift.

Learn to read three signatures. Signature one: a slow weekly decay on one machine while identical machines hold steady — that is a nozzle aging in place, and the slope tells you how fast your flux is hardening in the bore. Signature two: a step change right after a nozzle change — the new nozzle restores performance, confirming the old one was the constraint, and the interval between steps tells you your true nozzle life. Signature three: a decay that clean-and-replace does not fix — that is not the nozzle anymore, and now you have earned the right to look at the feeder.

The discipline is to write the dates down. When you start cleaning nozzles on a schedule — one SME24 cycle weekly per machine — the trend line becomes an experiment: flat after cleaning means the routine captured the cause. 13 — FAQ

Answers to the questions this machine raises

What does the SME-6300 actually remove?
Rosin flux, no-clean flux residues, water-soluble flux, solder paste bleed, solder balls and dust from post-solder PCBA — the manual lists rosin flux, no-clean flux, water-soluble flux, solder paste ball and other organic and inorganic polluted materials on the board.
We use no-clean flux. Do we really need to clean?
Ask a different question: are the residues *activated*? No-clean flux leaves a film that is harmless only while it stays dry and un-biased. Under humidity, flux residues support corrosion and electrochemical migration, and conformal coating applied over ionic residue delaminates and traps the chemistry against the pad. If your product sees humidity, voltage or a coating, cleaning is still a reliability step.
How fast is it? What throughput should I plan?
The conveyor runs 0.1–1.5 m/min, recommended 0.2–0.4. Capacity is speed ÷ pitch: at 0.3 m/min with a 450 mm pitch the machine carries 40 boards per hour; at 0.5 m/min with 300 mm pitch, 100. The cleaner follows your solder line's pace — send us your board sizes and takt and we will size the speed for you.
What water does it need, and how much?
One 1-inch DI inlet at ≥ 0.3 MPa delivering 10–15 L/min of ≥ 10 MΩ water. The machine monitors incoming resistivity live (0–18 MΩ·cm meter) and alarms if quality drops. Staged overflow reuse means the rinse tanks are refreshed by the final rinse, not by raw consumption — if you do not make DI water yet, the optional 1000 L/h processor covers it.
What chemistry, and what does it cost to run?
Water-based cleaning liquid, dosed automatically at set intervals by the standard diaphragm-pump system. The wash liquid recirculates through steel filter nets and 1 µm barrels, and the chemical-isolation air knives return liquid from the board to the tank — both choices exist to stretch bath life. Your consumption depends on flux load and board throughput; we will model it with your numbers.
Will it fit my line?
The machine is L 5200 × W 1650 × H 1650 mm with a 900 ± 25 mm conveyor height, and boards travel left to right. Plan L 8000 × W 4000 × H 2500 mm of floor for service access. Conveyor height matches standard line height; tell us your upstream and downstream machines and we will confirm the interface.
What happens when power drops mid-board?
A UPS keeps the net conveyor running so loaded boards exit the heated sections safely. Pumps and heaters are protected against low water level, over-temperature and overload, and every fault names itself on the touch panel.
