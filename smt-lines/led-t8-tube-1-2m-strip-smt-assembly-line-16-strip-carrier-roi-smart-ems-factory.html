How it works

Six steps from bare strip to inspected LED board

Every step runs on the same 1195 x 240 mm carrier, so sixteen strips move as one panel and the long boards are never handled one by one inside the line.

1

Load sixteen strips

At an ESD workstation an operator seats sixteen bare 1170 mm strips in the carrier's 8 mm or 10 mm nests. The vacuum loader feeds full carriers into the line.

60 s manual loading per carrier at a separate workstation.
2

Print the whole carrier

One stencil covers all sixteen strips. The printer aligns by fiducials and cleans the stencil automatically.

Four framed long-board stencils included: 8 and 10 mm, 96 and 120 LEDs.
3

Place 1,536 to 1,920 LEDs

The 20-head placement machine fills every strip on the carrier, 96 or 120 SMD2835 LEDs each.

76 s or 86 s allocation, 82,000 CPH optimum.
4

Reflow on a controlled profile

The long-board oven solders FR-4 and aluminium MCPCB strips; profiles are qualified with at least nine measuring points across the carrier.

Air reflow; no nitrogen or chilled water required in the base offer.
5

Buffer, cool and inspect

The buffer gives carriers time to cool, then inline AOI checks every LED and solder joint on the carrier.

Golden-board and seeded-defect qualification before acceptance.
6

Unload and return

Finished carriers are stacked, strips are removed at the second workstation and empty carriers go back to loading.

55 s unloading per carrier; four people run the whole line.

Carrier design5 min read

Why a 1.2 m LED Strip Should Never Travel Alone

An 8 mm wide, 1170 mm long strip bends, twists and slips. The line that makes it profitably moves sixteen of them at once on a rigid carrier.

The board that behaves like a ruler

Hold a bare T8 strip by one end and it droops. Put it on a standard SMT conveyor and the rails grip a few millimetres of edge over more than a metre of length. Printing, placement and reflow all assume a flat, rigid board. A single LED strip is neither, which is why so many tube makers fight print smears, misplaced end LEDs and warped boards after the oven.

What single-strip handling costs

Run strips one at a time and every machine spends most of its cycle loading, clamping and releasing a board that carries only 96 or 120 LEDs. The printer prints one narrow strip per stroke, the placement head travels the full length for a few parts, and the oven runs mostly empty. Throughput falls, and every extra handling step is another chance to bend a board.

Turn sixteen strips into one panel

Seat the strips in a precise aluminium carrier and treat the carrier as the board. It needs locating nests for each strip width, a flat support every 150 mm or so, clear edges for the conveyor rails, fiducials the cameras can find, and a weight every machine in the line can carry. Done well, the long, floppy strip disappears from the process.

The 16-strip carrier in this line

Our carrier is 1195 x 240 mm and holds sixteen 1170 mm strips at 12 mm pitch, with interchangeable 8 and 10 mm nests. It has six sealed vacuum pickup pads for the loader, three frame fiducials, a loaded target of 2.5 kg and a flatness target of 0.2 mm cold. The line ships with forty of them: twenty for 8 mm strips and twenty for 10 mm.

Check your strips against the carrier

Measure your strip length, width, thickness and warp, and note where the first and last LEDs sit. Those numbers decide whether the standard nests fit or a variant is needed. Send them before you compare machines; the carrier sets the limits of the whole line.

Cycle time5 min read

Find the Bottleneck Before You Buy the Line

A line runs at the speed of its slowest station. In an LED strip line that is usually placement, and the margin is thinner than most brochures suggest.

Five fast machines, one slow minute

Line proposals love big numbers: printer speed, placement CPH, oven length. What decides output is the one station that needs the longest time per carrier. Everything upstream waits for it and everything downstream starves.

Why catalogue speeds mislead

An optimum CPH figure assumes ideal travel, ideal nozzles and no board handling. A 1.2 m carrier means long head travel, and every carrier still has to be transferred, clamped and recognised. Printer cleaning, AOI image capture and oven spacing add their own fixed times.

Allocate time to every station

Build a simple table: for each station, the complete cycle per carrier including transfer and cleaning. The largest number is your bottleneck. Divide 3,600 by it, multiply by strips per carrier, then apply realistic availability and yield. That is your planning output.

The numbers for this line

For 96 LEDs per strip, placement takes 76 s per carrier and sets the pace; for 120 LEDs it takes 86 s. The printer needs 55 or 60 s, AOI 65 or 75 s and the oven feeds one carrier every 74.7 s. At 75% availability and performance and 98% yield, that gives 557 or 492 good strips per hour against a design basis of 481.

Watch the margin at 120 LEDs

Pure placement time for 1,920 LEDs at 82,000 CPH is 84.29 s, leaving only 1.71 s inside the 86 s allocation. That is why the sample run with your real strips matters. If your products move to higher LED counts, plan for that margin now.

Reflow5 min read

Reflow for LED Strips: Heat the Joint, Not the LED

Aluminium MCPCB pulls heat away, white FR-4 soaks it up, and the LED itself dislikes both extremes. Long-board reflow needs a profile you can prove.

Good joints, shifted colour

An LED strip can pass a visual check and still fail the customer: colour shift, dim LEDs or cracked joints after a few thermal cycles. Most of these problems trace back to the oven profile rather than the placement.

Why long carriers make it harder

A 1195 mm carrier enters the oven over more than a minute. Its leading edge heats before its trailing edge, and the aluminium frame adds thermal mass. Aluminium MCPCB and FR-4 behave very differently. One profile tuned on a short test board rarely holds across the whole carrier.

Profile the worst case, not the average

Measure at the leading, middle and trailing end of the carrier, and on the left, centre and right of it. Profile each board material separately at full load. Check peak temperature, time above liquidus, ramp rates and cooling against your paste and LED specifications.

How this line handles it

The SRF-1000L has ten top and ten bottom heating zones over 3892 mm and three plus three cooling zones, takes carriers up to 1250 mm and 3.5 kg, and is planned at 1.0 m/min. Thermal qualification uses at least nine measuring points per carrier, separately for FR-4 and MCPCB. A vertical buffer after the oven gives carriers time to reach 45 °C or less before AOI.

Ask for the profile data

Whatever oven you buy, ask for profiles measured on a loaded carrier with your material, not a catalogue curve. Keep them as the reference for every recipe change.

Inspection4 min read

Inline AOI on Long Boards: What to Ask Before You Sign

An AOI that cries wolf slows the line; one that misses defects ships them. Agree the numbers before the machine arrives.

Inspection that nobody trusts

Many LED lines switch AOI to a lenient recipe within weeks, because operators are tired of checking false calls. Defects then slip through to lamp assembly, where they cost far more to fix.

Why LED strips trip up AOI

Bright reflective MCPCB, white solder mask and hundreds of identical LEDs make a difficult image. Long carriers mean many images per panel. Without a clear agreement on what counts as a defect, every recipe change becomes an argument.

Define defects and limits up front

List the defects AOI must catch: missing, offset, polarity, tombstone and visible solder faults. Build golden boards and boards with deliberately seeded defects. Agree an escape limit and a false-call limit, and test against both before acceptance.

The AOI plan for this line

The SM-VL-800 uses a 5 MP camera with automatic width adjustment and inspects a complete carrier in 65 or 75 s. The proposed qualification uses 100 known-good boards and 30 defect boards, with zero escapes of agreed seeded defects and 2% or fewer false-call boards. Recipes are set for reflective MCPCB. An inline 3D SPI is available as an option ahead of placement.

Keep the test boards

After acceptance, keep the golden and seeded boards on site and rerun them after every recipe change or software update. It takes minutes and keeps the inspection honest.

Labour4 min read

Four People, One Line: Staffing an LED Strip Line

Labour is often the biggest running cost after materials. A carrier-based line needs fewer hands, but each one has a clear job.

Where the people go

Walk a manual or semi-automatic LED strip line and you will find people at every machine: loading single boards, wiping stencils, checking parts, sorting finished strips. Labour scales with output.

Why automation alone does not fix it

Adding automatic machines without changing how boards move just relocates the work. Someone still has to load each strip, and someone still has to unload it. The real saving comes when sixteen strips are handled as one unit.

Design the jobs with the line

Plan each role from the carrier's point of view: who fills it, who empties it, who keeps the printer and feeders supplied, who owns quality. Give each role enough cycle time to be done well, with a person covering breaks.

The four roles in this line

The staffing plan has four people per shift: one loading carriers at an ESD workstation, one unloading and returning them, one on printing, material and line care, and one on AOI review, quality and relief. Loading takes about 60 s per carrier and unloading 55 s, both off the main line so they never stop it.

Count your current hours per thousand strips

Divide the labour hours you spend today by the number of good strips. Compare that with four people producing 3,848 strips in eight hours. The gap is the labour line in your ROI.

Site preparation5 min read

Before the Container Arrives: Preparing the Site

A line can be ready in China and idle on site for weeks because power, air or exhaust was not ready. Plan the room first.

The line is here, the power is not

It happens more often than anyone admits: seventeen cases arrive, the engineers fly in, and the oven cannot start because the supply is too small or the exhaust duct goes nowhere. Every day of delay pushes back payback.

Why utilities get underestimated

Connected power and running power are very different. A reflow oven may be rated at 80 kW to heat up but use a fraction of that once running. Buyers either oversize everything or miss the start-up load. Air and exhaust are often forgotten entirely.

Plan with the right numbers

Use connected load to size the supply and running load to estimate cost. Add a reserve for compressed air and duct losses. Check floor space, door sizes, lifting equipment and level floors against the packing list.

What this line needs

The base line has 89.65 kW connected and 18.05 kW running load; plan 150 kVA with a 25 kW operating budget. Air is 200 L/min base, so plan 300 L/min at 6 bar. The oven needs 380 V three-phase and two exhaust outlets of about 10 m³/min each. The layout is 24 x 5 m. It ships in 17 cases, about 11.2 t gross and 62.2 m³.

Walk the route from truck to line

Measure every door, corridor and turn between the unloading bay and the line position. The oven case is 6.5 m long. Book the crane and forklift before the ship sails.

FAT and SAT5 min read

Acceptance Tests That Protect Both Buyer and Supplier

A line is only as good as the test that accepts it. Agree how you will measure output, quality and stoppages before anything is built.

Two parties, two different numbers

Disputes over new lines usually come down to measurement. The supplier quotes a cycle time; the buyer counts boxes at the end of a shift. Both are right about different things, and neither agreed in advance which number counts.

Why vague acceptance hurts everyone

Without a protocol, acceptance becomes a negotiation. Breaks, changeovers, material shortages and operator learning all blur the result. The supplier waits for the final payment, and the buyer runs a line nobody has proved.

Test the whole process, on real boards

Start with your own samples and data. Prove the carriers run reliably, qualify the oven and the AOI separately, then run a full shift with real staffing, breaks and changeovers, and count only accepted boards.

The acceptance plan for this line

The plan starts with your 1170 mm strips and Gerber, BOM and XY data. It includes at least 100 fully loaded carrier transfers with no drops or collisions, 20 thermal cycles on first-article fixtures, a nine-point thermal profile per material, AOI qualification with golden and seeded boards, and an elapsed 8-hour FAT per LED version with at least 3,848 accepted boards and four operators, followed by SAT on site.

Write down what a good board is

Agree what makes a strip good: correct assembly and polarity, visible solder criteria and an electrical check. Optical bin, colour temperature and finished-lamp safety are usually separate tests. Put it in writing.

ROI5 min read

LED Tube Line ROI: How to Build a Number Your Bank Will Accept

Payback depends on three numbers: what you invest, how many good strips you make, and what each one is worth to you. Get those right and the rest is arithmetic.

A payback figure without a source

Many line proposals end with a payback figure and no working. When finance asks how it was calculated, nobody can say which output, which wage or which price was assumed. The project stalls.

Where ROI estimates go wrong

The common mistakes are using equipment price instead of total project cost, catalogue speed instead of accepted output, and forgetting the people, power and spares the line still needs. Each one makes payback look shorter than it will be.

Build it from four blocks

Investment: equipment plus freight, duties, installation, utilities and contingency. Output: accepted strips per hour, hours per shift, shifts and days. Value: your saving or margin per good strip. Running cost: operators, electricity and maintenance. Subtract, divide, and test the result at one, two and three shifts.

Applying it to this line

The calculator on this page fixes the investment at USD 392,750, the equipment total plus 25%. With placeholder values of USD 0.20 per strip, 481 strips per hour, 26 days, four operators at USD 350, USD 0.10 per kWh and 2% maintenance, one shift pays back in about 22 months, two shifts in about 11 and three in about 7. Your own numbers will differ.

Test the weakest assumption

Find the input you are least sure of, usually value per strip or realistic shifts, and see how payback changes when it drops by 20%. If the project still works, you have a robust case.

FAQ

Questions buyers ask before ordering

How many LED strips does the line produce?

The design basis is 481 good 1170 mm strips per hour and 3,848 per 8-hour shift with four operators. Calculated output is 557 strips per hour for 96 LEDs and 492 for 120 LEDs at 75% availability and performance and 98% first-pass yield.

Why carriers instead of single strips?

A 1170 mm strip only 8 or 10 mm wide is too flexible to run alone. One 1195 x 240 mm aluminium carrier holds sixteen strips flat, so the printer, placement machine, oven and AOI each handle one rigid panel instead of sixteen loose boards.

Can it run FR-4 and aluminium MCPCB?

Yes. The basis covers 8 and 10 mm strips on FR-4 and aluminium MCPCB, with 96 or 120 SMD2835 LEDs. Separate reflow profiles are qualified for each material, and AOI recipes are set for reflective MCPCB.

How long does a product change take?

A same-family program change is planned at 15 minutes. A width, material or stencil change is planned at 45 minutes including the first-piece check. Twenty carriers are dedicated to 8 mm strips and twenty to 10 mm strips.

What utilities does the site need?

Plan 150 kVA electrical capacity with about 25 kW operating budget, 300 L/min of clean dry air at 6 bar, two reflow exhaust outlets of about 10 m³/min each and a 24 x 5 m area. The oven needs 380 V three-phase; the other machines run on 220-230 V single phase.

How is the line accepted?

Through a sample run with your strips, a 100-carrier dry run, thermal qualification with at least nine measuring points, AOI qualification with golden and seeded-defect boards, and a full 8-hour FAT per LED version with at least 3,848 accepted boards, followed by SAT on site.

What does the ROI on this page include?
