The residue problem

Flux residue is a cost you pay three times

You pay when residue causes leakage currents and field returns, you pay when a customer audit flags your cleaning process, and you pay again when perfectly good assemblies become electronic waste. Manual wiping does not fix this — it hides it until the worst possible moment.

01

Invisible leakage paths

Rosin and activator residue under low-standoff components creates conductive films. Boards pass ICT today and fail in a humid warehouse or in the customer cabinet six months later.

02

Manual cleaning lottery

Hand brushing with solvent depends on who is on shift. Coverage, pressure and chemistry vary board to board, so quality becomes a lottery you cannot audit.

03

Coating that will not stick

Conformal coating over contaminated surfaces delaminates. The coating line gets blamed, but the root cause is an unwashed board arriving from the wave.

04

ESG and audit exposure

Open solvent buckets and inconsistent wiping are hard to defend in automotive, medical and aerospace audits — and every scrapped PCBA is reported as e-waste.

05

Rework that erodes margin

Every residue-related return triggers diagnosis, disassembly and re-cleaning. The rework loop consumes your best technicians and your thinnest margins.

06

A line that waits for a sink

When cleaning is an offline corner with a bench and a brush, boards queue, WIP grows, and your expensive placement and soldering capacity waits on a wash tub.

Inside the machine

From dirty board to dry board in one pass

The S-168 completes the full cycle automatically: board in, brush clean, dry, air dry, board out. Designed for surface-mount assemblies, short-pin through-hole components and mixed-technology PCB cleaning.

1

Board in-feed

The entrance access device accepts boards from an upstream conveyor or by hand loading, with 2.0 mm special wash-plate chain claws gripping the board edge.

2

Four-stage brush wash

Four cleaning stages with imported antistatic brown-hair brushes and alumina cover brushes scrub both sides, each stage on independent stepless frequency control.

3

Filtered circulation

A cyclic filtration system with primary sponge plus SUS filter screen and an internal circulation pump keeps detergent clean, with liquid-level control protecting the pump.

4

Air dry and out

The air-drying mechanism strips rinse liquid and dries the board before out-feed, so downstream processes receive a clean, dry, audit-ready assembly.

The Ion You Cannot See Is the Return You Cannot Afford

6 min read · Flux residue and field reliability

+

A customer once showed us a warranty-returned power board that passed every test in his factory. Three months in a coastal warehouse, and a dendrite grew between two pads under a connector. One ionic film. One dead unit. One very expensive lesson: what you cannot see after reflow is exactly what your customer sees in the field.

If this sounds familiar

Your boards pass ICT and function test, yet returns trickle in months later. Your engineers blame the solder, the board shop, the humidity — anything but the cleaning step you quietly skipped because the board looked clean.

A method you can reuse

  • Map every flux residue source: paste, wave flux, rework touch-up.
  • Decide which assemblies need cleaning by service environment, not by appearance.
  • Standardize the wash: chemistry, brush contact, rinse, dry — same recipe every board.
  • Verify with a periodic ionic contamination or visual-plus test, not with a quick glance.

Where the S-168 fits

The S-168 turns that method into hardware: four independently controlled brush stages, filtered detergent circulation and air drying in one inline pass, so every board gets the same auditable clean.

Keeping the gain

Customers who standardize cleaning report fewer field returns quarter after quarter, and come back to us when they add capacity or new product families — the process scales with them.

Manual Wiping Is Not a Process — It Is a Lottery

5 min read · Replacing hand cleaning

+

Watch two operators hand-clean the same board and you will see two different boards. Different pressure, different solvent load, different patience at 4 PM on a Friday. Manual wiping is not a cleaning process. It is a lottery where the prize is a board that might be clean.

If this sounds familiar

Your quality team argues with production about whether boards are really clean. Your audit trail for cleaning is a signature on a sheet. And your best operator just quit, taking the process with her.

A method you can reuse

  • Time-study the manual station: seconds per board, boards per shift, solvent consumed.
  • Count the hidden costs: lint, solvent exposure, rejected boards, ergonomic strain.
  • Define a machine recipe: brush speed, detergent concentration, dry time.
  • Move the person from scrubbing to loading, inspecting and improving.

Where the S-168 fits

Stepless frequency-controlled brushes and PLC interlocks make the recipe the machine remembers, not the operator. The same board comes out the same way at 9 AM and at 9 PM.

Keeping the gain

The freed-up operator usually becomes your in-line quality gate, and the maintenance rhythm — brush swap four times a year, filter clean weekly — is simple enough to survive staff turnover.

Zero E-Waste Starts With a Clean Board, Not a Bigger Bin

Four Brush Stages, One Recipe: Tuning a Wash for Mixed Boards

6 min read · Process engineering

+

A dense SMT control board and a rosin-heavy power supply board should not get the same scrub. Yet most cleaning setups force one setting on every board. Here is how to think in stages instead.

If this sounds familiar

Your line runs consumer boards in the morning and industrial boards after lunch. Your single-speed cleaner is either too gentle for the power boards or too aggressive for the fine-pitch ones, and changeovers are guesswork.

A method you can reuse

  • Classify your boards into 2 or 3 cleaning classes by flux type and component sensitivity.
  • Assign each class a stage profile: which brushes engage, at what speed, with what chemistry.
  • Validate each class on the worst-case board, not the easiest one.
  • Write the profiles down and lock them to product codes.

Where the S-168 fits

Four cleaning stages with independent stepless frequency control let each class get its own brush intensity in a single pass. Antistatic brown-hair and alumina brushes cover both delicate and stubborn jobs.

Keeping the gain

New products are added by classification, not by re-engineering the wash. Send each new board type for a free proofing run and we return a recommended stage profile with the results.

Why Your Conformal Coating Peels: Start at the Wash, Not the Spray

5 min read · Coating adhesion

+

When coating bubbles, peels or fisheyes, everyone stares at the coating machine. But coating is brutally honest: it shows you exactly how clean the board under it really was.

If this sounds familiar

Your coating yield is unstable. The coating supplier blames your boards, the board shop blames the coating, and you are paying for strip-and-recoat labor on a line that was supposed to add protection, not problems.

A method you can reuse

  • Test adhesion on boards washed in-line versus boards only spot-cleaned.
  • Remove flux residues before coating, always — coating seals contamination in.
  • Dry completely: trapped moisture under coating is a corrosion time capsule.
  • Audit the wash step first whenever coating defects appear.

Where the S-168 fits

The S-168 washes and air-dries boards inline before coating, so the coating machine receives a surface it can actually bond to — and your strip-and-recoat loop disappears.

Keeping the gain

Stable coating yield is a metric your customer sees every month. Plants that fix the wash step typically keep the same recipe for years and simply scale it to new lines.

What Automotive and Medical Auditors Actually Check in Your Cleaning Room

6 min read · Audit readiness

+

Auditors do not ask whether you clean boards. They ask how you know they are clean, who verified it, and what happens when the process drifts. A sink and a brush cannot answer those questions.

If this sounds familiar

A big automotive or medical prospect is visiting. Your SMT line looks world-class until the audit reaches the cleaning corner: open solvent containers, handwritten logs, and a process that lives in one operator's memory.

A method you can reuse

  • Write a cleaning specification per product family: chemistry, stages, speeds, dryness criteria.
  • Make the process machine-enforced so the record is the machine, not a clipboard.
  • Define daily and weekly checks: brush condition, filter state, detergent level, gas concentration.
  • Keep maintenance logs that match the manual's schedule.

Where the S-168 fits

PLC control with protection interlocks, a defined maintenance rhythm in the manual, and visible transport give you exactly the artifacts an auditor wants: a controlled, repeatable, inspectable process.

Keeping the gain

Audit readiness compounds: once cleaning is machine-controlled, adding a new customer qualification is a documentation exercise, not a process rebuild.

Brush, Batch Tank or Inline: Choosing a Cleaning Architecture That Scales

7 min read · Equipment selection

+

There are three honest ways to wash a PCBA: manual brushing, a batch tank, or an inline machine. The right answer depends on your volume, your mix and where the line is going — not on which brochure arrived first.

If this sounds familiar

You are growing. Manual cleaning worked at 200 boards a day. At 2,000 it is a queue of boards, a queue of operators, and a queue of quality escapes, and you are not sure which machine architecture to bet on.

A method you can reuse

  • Manual: acceptable only for prototypes and rework benches.
  • Batch: good for low-volume high-mix, but boards wait and WIP grows between processes.
  • Inline brush: continuous flow, one-pass wash and dry, connects to conveyors and magazine unloaders.
  • Decide on total cost per clean board, including labor, chemistry, scrap and floor space.

Where the S-168 fits

The S-168 is the inline brush architecture: board in, four-stage wash, dry, board out, with visualization along the whole path. It sits behind your wave or at line end and clears boards as fast as they arrive.

Keeping the gain

Inline cleaning grows with the line: add a second unit on a second line rather than redesigning the process, and keep spare brushes and filters from the same supply channel.

FAQ: S-168 PCBA brush cleaning machine

What does the S-168 PCBA brush cleaning machine do?

It automatically completes board in-feed, brush cleaning, drying, air drying and out-feed. It is mainly used for cleaning surface-mount assemblies, short-pin through-hole components and mixed-technology PCB boards after soldering.

Is it inline or batch?

Inline. The S-168 is designed with a reasonable overall layout and can be connected with related equipment, so boards flow through without manual carrying between processes.

What control system does it use?

A PLC programmable controller (Mitsubishi, Japan) is the control core, with simple operation, multiple protection measures and high reliability.

How many cleaning stages are there?

Four cleaning stages, each using stepless electronic frequency conversion technology and independently controlled, suitable for various types of PCB cleaning.

What brushes does it use? Are they safe for components?

The dry-wash brush uses imported antistatic brown hair and the cover brush uses antistatic alumina, so scrubbing removes flux and rosin without charging or damaging components.

How much maintenance does it need?

The manual defines a light routine: clear rosin from brushes daily, top up detergent every 4 hours, clean filters weekly, replace brushes about four times a year, and check sensors monthly.

Yes. Southern Machinery offers free proofing: send sample boards with real flux residue and receive photos, video and a recommended stage recipe.
