Why calibrate

Feeder problems arrive on the line wearing a different name

A feeder that is out of alignment does not announce itself. It presents as a placement fault, so the process engineer changes a recipe, the operator changes a nozzle, and the real cause stays in the maintenance room until someone puts the feeder on a bench and looks at it directly.

01

Pick-up offset that looks like a machine fault

A worn or repaired feeder can present the component slightly off. On the machine that reads as a placement shift or an alignment problem, and the investigation starts in the wrong place.

02

Throw and jam rates that move with the shift, not the part

When a feeder feeds inconsistently, the line reacts by slowing down or stopping. The cost lands as downtime and rework, and both are attributed to the product rather than to the feeder.

03

Second-hand feeders that were never checked on arrival

Purchased or traded feeders arrive with unknown history. Without an incoming check, the first production board is the acceptance test, and the failures it produces are paid for twice.

04

Maintenance that is repeated instead of recorded

When a feeder is returned to service on a verbal handover, the next technician repeats the same check from scratch. A bench station with a written procedure turns that into a record.

05

Cross-checking that depends on whoever is on shift

Judging a feeder by eye, against a tape hole, under shop lighting, is a different test on every shift. A camera, a cross reference and a fixed procedure make the same judgement repeatable.

06

A machine problem that is actually a feeder problem

Separating the two quickly is worth more than either fix. A jig does not repair feeders; it makes the diagnosis shorter.

What it checks

Four things a maintenance technician can actually see

The jig is an observation and adjustment station. It does not repair a feeder and it does not test placement quality. What it does is put the feeder in a repeatable state so four specific things can be seen and adjusted rather than guessed.

01

Pick-up position

The position the nozzle takes the component from, viewed through the camera against a cross reference or calibration ruler.

02

Feeder pitch and feeding movement

Whether the feeder advances one pitch consistently, and whether the lever or thimble movement behaves the same way on every cycle.

03

Tape guide condition

Wear and damage on the tape guide cover, which is a common cause of unstable feeding long before it becomes a placement fault.

04

Alignment against the feeder base

Whether the feeder body itself sits square on the fixture, which separates a bent feeder from a misadjusted feeder.

How it works

From feeder on the bench to feeder back on the line

The manual describes the procedure as a fixed sequence. This is that sequence, in the order the technician performs it.

1

Confirm the feeder

Check the feeder series, the tape width and the pitch against the fixture before mounting anything.

2

Mount on the jig base

Seat the feeder on the feeder foundation and clamp it so it cannot shift while the camera is focused.

3

Focus and zero the reference

Set the camera focus and align the cross reference on the monitor to the calibration ruler or master reference.

4

Advance and observe

Step the feeder forward, watch the pick-up position on the monitor, and use single or continuous lever simulation to observe repeated cycles.

5

Adjust X, Y or H

Correct the supported adjustment points, then re-check rather than adjusting more than once and hoping.

6

Re-check before release

Run the feeder again through the same observation and confirm the position holds across repeated cycles.

Platform fit

Three things decide whether it works in your maintenance room

A calibration jig is only useful if it accepts the feeder you actually own, holds it in the position the machine expects, and gives your technician a reference they can read the same way twice. Each of those is a configuration question, answered before a price is quoted.

01

The feeder series it accepts

The jig base and its sub-bases determine which feeder families can be mounted. The published sheet names a set of feeder control zones; the exact accepted list is confirmed against your feeder part numbers.

02

The fixture that positions it

A repeatable bench check needs the feeder to sit in the same place every time. The feeder foundation and the locating pins are what turn a judgement into a measurement.

03

The reference the technician reads

The monitor cross reference and the calibration ruler are the reference. Whichever reference is supplied has to be the one your written procedure names, or the procedure is worthless.

04

Power and air at the bench

The product sheet states a working voltage and an air pressure requirement. Both belong on the quote, because a bench without the right supply is a bench that does not get used.

05

Where it sits in your feeder population

A jig earns its place next to the feeder repair bench, in incoming inspection, or in the spare-feeder store. Which one depends on how many feeders you run and how often they come back.

06

What it does not cover

It does not verify placement accuracy on the machine, it does not replace the machine calibration routine, and it does not cover feeder types outside the confirmed list. Those are separate jobs.

Field notes

Eight notes on feeder accuracy, maintenance rooms and the cost of a placement fault

Written for process engineers, maintenance leads and production managers who have to make the decision, not just read about it. Each note opens with the problem you will recognise, sets out a method you can reuse, names the tool that carries it, explains how to keep it running, and ends with the next step.

Note 01The placement fault that is really a feeder fault

When the same component drifts on one lane and not another, the recipe is rarely the answer.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

A line runs three shifts. On the night shift a particular lane starts throwing components, and the morning shift finds the placements slightly off on the same lane. The instinct is to change the recipe, then the nozzle, then the feeder bank. What is actually happening is that one feeder has a pick-up position that has drifted, and the drift only shows when the operator is compensating for it by hand.

The reusable method is a three-step separation test. First, swap the feeder to a different lane position and run the same board. If the fault follows the feeder, it is a feeder fault, full stop. Second, swap the feeder with an identical one from the same width group. If the fault stays with the position, it is not the feeder. Third, put the suspect feeder on a bench and look at the pick-up position directly. Two of these three steps take under ten minutes and none of them require a machine engineer.

The tool that carries the third step is a bench station with a camera and a fixed reference. Without one, the test ends at step two and the conclusion is a guess.

To keep the test alive, write it into the fault report. Every placement fault gets the three-step result recorded, not just the conclusion. After twenty reports, the pattern of which lanes fail will tell you something no single investigation can.

Next step: pick the feeder with the worst throw history this month and run the three-step test on it this week. Send us the feeder part number and a photo of the tape position at the point of failure, and we will tell you whether a jig configuration is worth quoting for it.

Note 02What actually happens to a feeder after a repair

A returned feeder is not a restored feeder until somebody has looked at it.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

A feeder comes off the machine after a jam, a crash or a scheduled strip. It goes to the repair bench, something is replaced or cleaned, and it goes back into the feeder bank. What almost never happens is a verification that the pick-up position is back where it was. The feeder was disturbed to be repaired, and the disturbance is exactly what changes the position.

The reusable method is a release gate. No feeder returns to production without three observations recorded: the pick-up position on the monitor, the feeding movement across at least ten consecutive pitches, and the tape guide condition. Three lines on a repair ticket, signed and dated. It takes longer to argue about than to do.

The tool is the same bench station, used as a release point rather than a repair point. That distinction matters: the jig is not there to fix the feeder, it is there to prove the feeder is back within expectation before it consumes line time.

To keep the gate real, keep the records. A feeder history that shows three checks per repair will show you which repair steps cause drift, and those are the steps worth tightening.

Next step: count the feeders your team released to the line last month without a recorded check. If the number is not zero, that is the business case for the bench, written in your own data.

Note 03Second-hand feeders are an incoming inspection problem, not a production problem

The cheapest feeder in the market is one that fails on your line instead of on the supplier's bench.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

A batch of second-hand feeders arrives. Some are clean, some have been opened, some have unknown history and mismatched wear. Installed into the machine, the good ones run and the bad ones present as placement faults that now belong to you. The cost lands in troubleshooting hours, in component consumption, and in the argument about who supplied them.

The reusable method is a sampling inspection with a written acceptance rule. Sample a defined number from the batch, run the same four observations on each, and write down what fails. A batch with a high failure rate is a purchasing problem, and the evidence is much stronger in your hands than in an argument after the fact.

The tool is the same jig, plus a simple log sheet. The jig does the observation; the sheet is what turns a series of observations into a purchasing argument you can actually use.

To keep the argument alive, keep the failed samples. One feeder in a drawer, tagged with its failure, settles more supplier discussions than a spreadsheet.

Next step: next time a second-hand feeder batch is quoted, ask for the sample inspection result before the purchase order. We can supply the acceptance rule sheet as a starting point.

Note 04How to judge whether feeder calibration is even your problem

Some lines have a placement quality problem that no feeder jig will touch.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

A production manager buys a jig because placement quality is poor. Three months later the quality is the same, because the drift is in the machine, the stencil, the paste or the reflow profile, and the feeder was never the variable. This is the most common way a feeder maintenance purchase turns into an unused purchase.

The reusable method is a baseline before the purchase. Take one feeder, run it through the jig, and record the pick-up position as a number your team agrees on. Then check whether the placement fault on the line matches the drift you measured. If the feeder is within expectation and the placement is still wrong, the jig has already told you it is not the answer, and it did so before you spent anything.

The tool is the jig used as a measuring instrument once, before it becomes a station. A single baseline is enough to make the purchase decision defensible.

To keep the decision honest, revisit the baseline quarterly against your placement first-pass yield. If yield moves and the feeder baseline does not, the jig is doing its job and the problem is elsewhere. If yield does not move and neither does the baseline, stop looking at feeders.

Next step: record your current pick-up position for five feeders of the same series before you buy anything, and send us the numbers. We will tell you whether the variation you have is normal wear or something worth quoting a jig against.

Note 05One bench, one operator: making the check fast enough that people actually do it

A verification step that takes twenty minutes does not survive a busy shift.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

The most accurate procedure in the world is skipped when the line is behind and the shift is ending. Every maintenance system in every factory has this problem, and it is why a good procedure decays into a bad habit within a quarter.

The reusable method is to design the check to be short, not to ask people to be more diligent. Measure the current check time properly, including setup and re-checking. Then set a target that is shorter than the manual time by enough that the saving is obvious. Anything that makes the operator's work easier survives; anything that only makes the report nicer does not.

The tool is a station that puts everything in one place: the fixture, the camera, the reference and the procedure. The value is not the magnification, it is that the technician does not have to search for a fixture or re-focus a camera every time.

To keep it short, keep the reference in a fixed place and never let it be substituted. A second master reference that is slightly different is worse than no reference, because two people will get two different answers from the same feeder.

Next step: time your current check on three feeders this week with a stopwatch, including setup. That measurement is the baseline for whether a station is worth anything to you.

Note 06What a feeder jig does not do, and why that matters when you buy one

The claims you should refuse to hear from any supplier, including us.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

A feeder calibration jig does not repair feeders, does not verify placement accuracy on the machine, does not replace the machine's own calibration routine, and does not cover feeder types outside the confirmed list. It also does not, by itself, reduce your throw rate by any percentage you have seen in a brochure.

The reusable method is to ask every supplier the same four questions before the quote: which feeder part numbers are accepted, what accuracy is stated and where is it measured, what is in the box, and what is excluded. A supplier who answers all four precisely is worth more than one who quotes a percentage improvement and cannot name a document.

The tool is the specification table with a source column. If a value has no document attached to it, it is a marketing claim, and it should be marked as unconfirmed rather than repeated in your own customer material.

To keep the discipline, write the exclusions into your own internal standard. A maintenance standard that lists what the jig does not check is the one your technicians will actually follow.

Next step: send us your four questions about your own feeder population. We will answer them against the documents we hold, and mark anything we cannot confirm as unconfirmed rather than filling the gap.

Note 07The maintenance room as a production asset

The room that gets the least attention is usually the one that decides your yield.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

In most factories the maintenance room is a bench, a toolbox and a person with a good memory. Feeders arrive, get fixed, and leave. Nothing is measured, so nothing improves, and the same fault is diagnosed again next month by a different person with no reference to the last time.

The reusable method is to make the maintenance room measurable. Three numbers per feeder, recorded every time: pick-up position, consecutive pitches observed, and tape guide condition. Six months of that is a dataset that tells you which feeders wear fastest, which repairs cause drift, and what your real replacement cycle is. None of that is available from a machine log.

The tool is the bench station plus a log sheet, deployed at three points: repair release, incoming inspection and spare-feeder storage. The station is the instrument; the log is what turns it into a management system.

To keep it running, review the log monthly with the maintenance lead, not annually with the plant manager. Ten minutes of review a month is what separates a maintenance room that improves from one that repeats.

Next step: start with the feeder that has been replaced most often this year and build the record for that one family first. A dataset on one feeder series is worth more than an empty standard covering forty.

Note 08Building the business case a budget committee will actually approve

The arithmetic that survives the first round of questions.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

Most feeder maintenance purchases are justified with a story about quality, and quality arguments lose to arithmetic in budget reviews. The story is usually true; it is just not the currency the committee spends in.

The reusable method is to build the case from maintenance labour only, because it is the number you can defend. Count the feeder checks per month, measure the time each one takes today, estimate the time on a bench station, apply your loaded technician rate, and compare the annual labour against the investment. That is a conservative case, because it deliberately leaves out downtime, throw rate and yield.

The tool is the payback model on this page, with your own figures in it. It prints a negative result honestly rather than flattering you, which is exactly why you can show it to a committee.

To keep the case alive, re-run the model with real figures after three months. If the measured check time is not shorter than you assumed, the model tells you so, and you can fix the process rather than defending the number.

Next step: time five feeder checks this month with a stopwatch and enter the real figures into the model above. If the payback is not defensible, you have saved yourself an approval meeting.

FAQ

Questions we get before the first quotation

Is it compatible with my machine and feeder?
Compatibility is confirmed against your machine model, feeder series, feeder width and part number before we quote. The jig base and its sub-bases determine what can be mounted, so a feeder part number is the single most useful thing you can send us. We will tell you when the answer is that a different fixture is needed rather than selling the standard one.
Which brand names appear on this page, and does that mean a partnership?
FUJI, Panasonic, Samsung, JUKI and I-pulse appear here only to describe the machines and feeders a jig may be asked to fit. Naming a brand to define an adaptation requirement is not a statement of authorisation, OEM relationship or certification, and we make no such claim.
How accurate is it?
We do not publish an accuracy figure for this jig, because no accuracy value appears in the Southern Machinery documents this page is built from. Third-party manuals state figures for their own machines; those are not a claim about ours. If you need a stated tolerance for acceptance, ask for it in writing before the order and we will confirm what can be committed.
What is in the box?
The operation manual lists a machine unit, LCD monitor, CCD camera, calibration ruler and user manual. The current standard package is confirmed before quotation, including the monitor size, which is one of the open points on this page.
Can you train our technicians?
Yes. Remote guidance for the technicians who will run the bench can be included with the order, and on-site support can be discussed for larger feeder populations. Tell us the technician count and their experience with the machine brand.
What does it cost, and what is the lead time?
Price and lead time depend on the configuration, the destination power requirement, quantity and the current workload. Send your feeder details and we will confirm both against a current quotation. We do not publish historical prices or lead times.
Do you supply the feeders as well?
Yes. The jig is one item in a wider feeder supply, spare parts and maintenance offer, and it can be quoted alongside feeder supply, feeder repair workflow support and full SMT/THT line integration.
Will this reduce our throw rate?
We do not claim a percentage. A feeder out of alignment contributes to throw, and correcting it removes one cause, but throw also comes from the tape, the component, the nozzle, the machine and the environment. Measure your own throw rate before and after on the same feeder family, and let that number decide.
