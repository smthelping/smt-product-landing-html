In one paragraph

What AI radial splicing tape is

AI radial splicing tape is a pre-cut, adhesive splice that joins two pieces of taped radial components (capacitors, inductors, LEDs and similar parts on a paper carrier) so a radial auto-insertion machine keeps feeding when one reel or ammo box runs out. Each splice is punched with holes that line up with the tape's feed holes, so the feeder's sprocket advances straight through the joint. Southern Machinery supplies it in 3-hole and 5-hole formats, in masking paper or kraft paper, with a hand splice cutter for 12.7 mm and 15.0 mm radial tape.

The real cost

A radial inserter never stops because of a hard part. It stops because a reel ran out.

Every radial line has the same weak moment: the last component on the tape. If the next reel is joined by hand with office tape, or not joined at all, the machine waits while someone re-threads the feeder. A proper splice turns that stop into a 40-second job done while the machine keeps working.

01

Re-threading costs minutes, every time

Without a splice the operator stops the station, threads the new tape through the feeder and indexes it to the first component. Multiply that by every reel on every feeder, every shift.

02

Office tape does not survive the sprocket

Generic tape covers the feed holes, so the sprocket pin has nothing to drive. The joint slips, tears or jams at exactly the point where nobody is watching.

03

A slipped joint means a mis-pitched part

If the splice shifts the pitch by even part of a hole, the next components arrive off-centre at the cutter and insertion head. That shows up as bent leads and missing parts.

04

Adhesive left behind ends up in the machine

Low-grade glue oozes from the joint, picks up dust and coats guides and sprockets. Now the consumable is creating maintenance work.

05

Ragged cuts make every splice a guess

Cutting tape with scissors leaves the end anywhere between two holes. The splice cannot register on holes that are not there.

06

Consumables bought ad hoc run out at night

Splice tape is cheap, so nobody plans it. Then the night shift finds an empty box and reaches for whatever tape is in the drawer.

How a splice is made

Four moves at the feeder, no tools beyond the cutter

The method below is the one printed on the source sheets for this tape. It is the same for 3-hole and 5-hole splices; the longer splice simply bridges more feed holes.

  1. 1

    Cut on the feed hole

    Cut both tape ends through the centre of a feed hole, square to the tape. The splice cutter does this in one press, at the right pitch.

  2. 2

    Peel the liner

    Lift the backing paper off the splice. The die-cut liner means your fingers never touch the adhesive face.

  3. 3

    Seat the locating dots

    Press the raised locating dots on the splice into the feed holes of both tape ends, so the two ends sit at the original pitch, then press down.

  4. 4

    Fold and press

    Fold the splice along its centre line over the other face of the tape and press. The joint is now closed on both faces and ready to feed.

Splice at the reel or the feeder input, never inside a running machine. Follow your line's lock-out practice before reaching into any feeder path.

High-tack adhesive

Feature

High-strength, high-viscosity adhesive on a masking paper or kraft paper base.

Advantage

The joint holds against the pull of the feeder and the bend around guides, instead of creeping apart over the next metres of tape.

Benefit

Fewer jams at the splice point, so the station keeps inserting instead of waiting for an operator.

Die-cut both faces

Feature

Front and back of every splice are precision die-cut; hole size is enlarged for easy handling.

Advantage

Holes in the splice match the feed holes in the tape, so the sprocket pin drives straight through the joint and the machine can identify it.

Benefit

No mis-pitched component after a splice, which means no bent leads at the cutter and no missing parts on the board.

Locating dots

Feature

Raised locating dots that seat into the feed holes of both tape ends before the splice is pressed down.

Advantage

The two ends are registered at the original pitch by the splice itself, not by the operator's eye.

Benefit

Any trained operator makes the same splice on the first try, on any shift.

Two backings

Feature

Masking paper with a no-residue, durable adhesive, or kraft paper with a coated, smooth surface.

Advantage

Masking paper releases cleanly; kraft paper's coated face slides through guides with low friction.

Benefit

You choose the backing your machine runs best on, and keep glue off guides and sprockets.

No-touch liner

Feature

The splice is handled by its liner, so fingers never contact the adhesive face during use.

Advantage

No skin oil or dust on the bonding surface, which is what usually weakens a hand-made joint.

Benefit

Splice strength is the same on the thousandth splice as on the first.

Look at what actually stopped the line

Ask a production manager what stops a radial line and you will hear about bent leads, a worn cutter, a board that warped in the oven.

Now look at the downtime log. The most common entry is not a failure at all. It is "reel change." A feeder ran empty, and the machine waited.

The stop that hides in plain sight

A reel change is not dramatic, so nobody fights it. It is three or four minutes of an operator threading tape, indexing the first part, closing the cover. Then it happens again on the next feeder. And again on the next shift.

None of those minutes are big enough to escalate. Together they are often the biggest single block of lost insertion time you have.

Move the work off the machine

You cannot remove the reel change. Reels end. What you can do is change where the work happens.

Joined in time, the new reel is already part of the old tape when the last component leaves. The machine never sees an empty feeder. The operator still does a job, but it is a 40-second job at the reel, not a stop at the station.

That is the whole idea: take a task that sits on the machine's critical path and move it beside the path.

A splice built for the sprocket

This is what AI radial splicing tape is for. Each splice is die-cut with holes that line up with the tape's feed holes, so the feeder drives straight through the joint. Locating dots seat the two ends at the original pitch. A high-tack adhesive on masking or kraft paper holds through the guides.

Southern Machinery supplies it in 3-hole and 5-hole formats, with a hand cutter for 12.7 mm and 15.0 mm radial tape so every cut lands on a feed hole.

Why this gets cheaper every month

The first roll changes one habit. After that the pattern repeats on its own: every reel that ends is spliced, every splice comes from the same box, every box is reordered on the same day of the month.

The cost is predictable, the downtime it removes is predictable, and the operator who makes a hundred splices a week gets faster at it than anyone who threads tape twice a day.

Nobody chose 3-hole. It was just in the drawer.

Walk around a dozen factories and ask why they use the splice they use. The honest answer is usually: that is what the last buyer ordered.

Habit is fine until a heavy part starts pulling joints apart halfway down the feeder.

What the hole count really controls

A splice carries load. The feeder pulls the tape forward one pitch at a time; the guides bend it; the weight of the components hangs from it. All of that passes through the adhesive area of the splice.

More holes bridged means a longer splice. A longer splice means more bonded area on each side of the cut. That is the whole difference between 3-hole and 5-hole.

A one-minute decision rule

Small, light parts on a short, straight tape path: 3-hole. You get the most splices per roll and a joint that is easy to make.

Heavier parts, larger electrolytics, inductors, or a tape that bends around several guides: 5-hole. You trade a little tape for a lot of margin.

Anything unusual, a different hole pattern or a very long path: ask for 6-hole or a made-to-order pattern, and confirm it with a sample first.

The codes that cover most lines

Southern Machinery stocks four standard splices: SCT-031000 and SCT-030500 for 3-hole in masking and kraft paper, and SCT-051000 and SCT-050500 for 5-hole. Every one is die-cut on both faces with locating dots, so the choice is about load, not about quality.

Decide once, then stop deciding

Once a feeder has its splice, write it on the feeder setup sheet. The operator never has to judge it again, the reorder never has to be thought about again, and a new hire makes the same splice as a ten-year veteran.

The backing is not decoration

Most buyers treat masking and kraft as two colours of the same thing. Pick the cheaper one, move on.

The tape path does not see it that way. The backing is the surface that slides over every guide between the reel and the insertion head.

Two papers, two behaviours

Masking paper carries a high-viscosity masking adhesive that holds firmly and releases cleanly, with no residue left behind.

Kraft paper is also high-viscosity, but its surface is coated and smooth. On a machine with tight or dragging guides, that smooth face slides with less friction.

Let the machine choose

If your guides are clean and the path is short, masking paper is usually all you need, and on our standard codes you get 1,000 splices per roll.

If you see splices catching, lifting at the edge or leaving marks in the guide, try kraft. Same feeder, same parts, one week. Count the jams. The number decides.

Both are on the shelf

Southern Machinery's AI radial splicing tape comes in both backings for 3-hole and 5-hole formats. The die-cut holes, the locating dots and the no-touch liner are identical, so changing backing changes nothing about how the operator works.

Make the result stick

When the trial is done, record the winner against that feeder. The decision now survives shift changes, staff turnover and the next price negotiation.

The glue gets blamed for the scissors

When a splice fails, the first suspect is always the adhesive. Stronger tape, please.

Look closer at the failed joint. Very often the tape end was cut somewhere between two feed holes. The splice never had a chance.

Registration is the whole job

A splice works because its holes line up with the tape's feed holes. That only happens if both tape ends are cut through the centre of a feed hole, square to the tape.

Cut 2 mm off and the pitch across the joint is now wrong by 2 mm. The sprocket pulls, the next component arrives off-centre, and the insertion head finds a lead where it expected a gap.

Fix the cut, and the glue is enough

Think of a splice as two operations. First, prepare the ends so they sit at the original pitch. Second, bond them. Most of the reliability comes from the first step.

A cutter built for one pitch

Southern Machinery's radial tape splice cutter is a hand tool made for exactly that first step. It comes in versions marked for 12.7 mm and 15.0 mm tape, so the cut lands on the feed hole in one press. Paired with a die-cut splice that has locating dots, the joint is registered by the tools, not by the operator's eye.

Fewer re-checks, every shift

When the cut is always right, nobody has to measure the joint afterwards. The splice becomes routine, routine becomes fast, and fast splicing is what keeps reels changing while the machine runs.

If it is not written, it is improvised

Ask three operators how they splice a radial reel and you will see three methods. One uses the cutter, one uses scissors, one presses the splice with a thumbnail and hopes.

All three work most of the time. The night shift is where "most of the time" stops being good enough.

Why reel changes drift

Reel changes are frequent, quick and boring. Nobody audits them. So the method slowly becomes whatever is fastest for each person, and the failure rate becomes whatever that method produces.

Blame the tape, and you will buy the wrong fix

A jam at a splice point looks like a tape problem. Most of the time it is not. Changing brand without finding the cause just moves the problem to next month.

The five usual suspects

1. The cut missed the feed hole, so the pitch across the joint is wrong.

2. The locating dots were not seated before pressing, so the ends shifted.

3. The adhesive face was touched or dusty, so the bond is weak.

4. The splice was folded off-centre, so one edge lifts in the guide.

5. The splice is too short for the load: a 3-hole joint on a heavy part.

One check for each

Measure pitch across the joint against the tape on either side. Look for dots sitting proud of the holes. Check whether operators are peeling by the liner. Look for a lifted edge after the first guide. Compare part weight against the hole count you chose.

Start with safe state: stop and isolate the feeder under your site procedure before you inspect anything in the tape path. If the cause is not clear, escalate to your process engineer rather than adjusting the feeder.

Tools that remove the first four

A cutter made for your pitch removes suspect one. A die-cut splice with locating dots removes suspect two. A no-touch liner removes suspect three. A centre fold line removes most of suspect four. What is left is the hole count, and that is a decision you make once per feeder.

Write down what you found

Log each splice failure with the suspect number. After a month the pattern is obvious, and the fix is usually training or hole count, not a new supplier.

A splice has to pass three tests in ten seconds

Once the new reel is joined, the splice travels from the reel into the feeder, around the guides, past the sprocket and up to the cutter and insertion head. Every one of those points is a test.

Where joints usually fail

At the sprocket, the pin needs a feed hole exactly one pitch from the last one. At the guides, the joint has to bend without an edge lifting. At the cutter, the next component must arrive centred, because the cutter and the head work on the assumption that every part is where the tape says it is.

A joint that is a little off in pitch passes the first test and fails the third.

Pitch is the only number that matters

Hold one idea: across the splice, the feed-hole pitch must be identical to the rest of the tape. Get that right and the machine cannot tell a splice is there. Get it wrong and the machine tells you, usually as a bent lead or a missing part.

Built so the machine cannot tell

That is the design goal of Southern Machinery's AI radial splicing tape: holes die-cut to match the feed holes, locating dots that hold the two ends at pitch, and a cutter for 12.7 mm or 15.0 mm tape that puts every cut on a hole. On an S3000 or S3010A radial inserter, a good splice simply feeds like tape.

The quiet benefit

When splices are invisible to the machine, reel changes stop being events. Operators splice ahead, feeders never run dry, and the insertion rate you paid for is the rate you get across the shift, not just between reel changes.

Cheap consumables cause expensive gaps

FAQ

Questions buyers ask before the first roll

What is AI radial splicing tape used for?
It joins the end of one reel or ammo box of taped radial components to the start of the next, so a radial (vertical) auto-insertion machine or a radial tape feeder keeps feeding without being re-threaded. The splice has holes that match the tape's feed holes, so the feeder drives through the joint.
Should I use 3-hole or 5-hole splices?
Start with 3-hole for small, light radial parts and short tape paths. Move to 5-hole for heavier parts such as larger electrolytic capacitors and inductors, or where the tape bends around several guides, because the longer splice bridges more feed holes and carries the load over more adhesive area. 6-hole is available on request.
Masking paper or kraft paper: what is the difference?
Masking paper uses a high-viscosity masking adhesive that holds well and leaves no residue. Kraft paper is also high-viscosity but has a coated, smooth surface that slides with less friction through tape guides. If you are unsure, trial both on the same feeder for a week.
Which machines does it fit?
Radial auto-insertion machines that run paper-carrier radial tape, including Southern Machinery's S3000 and S3010A, and radial tape feeders on SMT placement machines. Because tape suppliers differ, we confirm fit from a photo or sample of your tape before you order.
Can I use it on axial (horizontal) tape?
No. Axial tape has a different construction with no feed holes on a paper carrier. Ask us for the horizontal-insertion splice tape instead.
What does the splice cutter do, and which size do I need?
It cuts the radial tape square through the centre of a feed hole so the splice registers at the correct pitch. It comes in versions marked for 12.7 mm and 15.0 mm tape. Most radial taping uses a 12.7 mm feed-hole pitch; some larger parts are taped at 15.0 mm. Check your tape drawing, or send us a photo with a ruler and we will tell you.
How is a splice made?
Cut both tape ends through the centre of a feed hole, peel the liner, press the splice's locating dots into the feed holes of both ends, then fold the splice along its centre line over the other face and press. Do it at the reel or feeder input, never inside a running machine.
How long can I store it?
Keep rolls sealed, out of direct sun and away from heat. For Southern Machinery's SMD splice tape the published guidance is roughly six to twelve months at about 10 to 30 °C; ask us for the guidance that applies to the AI tape batch you receive.

Splices come on rolls (1,000 per roll for masking paper and 500 per roll for kraft paper on our standard codes), packed by carton. Orders typically ship about 2 days after order confirmation. Tell us your monthly use and we plan the quantity with you.
