01 — Why printing decides the line

The printer is the cheapest place to fix a defect, and the last place you look

Yield problems are usually chased at AOI or at rework. By then the paste volume is already wrong, the components are already placed into it, and the only lever left is a soldering iron. Printing is the one station where a few thousand dollars of tooling and a two-minute changeover change the outcome of the whole shift.

01

Paste volume is set once and copied everywhere

Squeegee pressure, speed and separation set the deposit on every pad of every board in the run. Get them right and the mounter has a flat, tacky, correctly-sized target. Get them wrong and you have produced the same defect several hundred times before anyone notices.

02

Rework is the most expensive repair you can buy

A touched-up joint costs you the operator time, the flux, the risk of a lifted pad, and a traceability hole — you now cannot say the board was built to process. Fixing the deposit costs you a stencil and a setup.

03

Oversize and low-volume boards have no automatic answer

A 1200 mm LED board or a 20-piece NPI run does not justify a fully automatic in-line printer. That is exactly the gap the SP-1200 and SP-300 fill, and it is the reason we still build semi-automatic machines.

02 — The printing cycle

Nine steps, and eight of them are adjustable

The cycle below is the sequence a stencil printer actually runs. Every step is a parameter you can change; the specification table later in this page tells you the range each machine allows.

01

Board in, tooling located

The board is clamped against the tooling pins or held by the vacuum nozzle, so its position is repeatable from board to board. On the SP series this is done by hand; on the A9 it is done by the transport and clamping system.

02

Fiducial read and table aligned

The A9 reads fiducials with a CCD camera — square, round, triangle, cross or pad-hole — and corrects X, Y and theta. The SP series does the same correction mechanically, by moving the stencil frame relative to the printing table.

03

Stencil lowered to the board

The stencil is brought down onto the board. Contact pressure has to be enough to seal the apertures and not enough to bow the stencil.

04

Squeegee travels across the apertures

The blade pushes paste ahead of it and rolls it into the apertures. Angle, speed and pressure decide whether the aperture fills cleanly or smears.

05

Paste rolls and fills the apertures

At the right speed the paste rolls rather than slides. Too fast and the aperture is under-filled at the trailing edge; too slow and the paste dries on the stencil during long runs.

06

Squeegee lifts

The blade is raised. The order of this lift against the next step is one of the most useful settings on a modern printer, because it decides how much paste stays in the aperture.

07

Stencil separates from the board

Separation speed is quoted as 0.1–20 mm/s on the A9. A slow, controlled separation is what keeps the deposit shape; a fast one pulls the paste back out of the aperture.

08

Stencil cleaned underneath

Dry, wet and vacuum cleaning are programmable on the A9. The SP series is cleaned by the operator. Skipping this step is how a stencil clogs across a shift.

09

Board out, deposit inspected

The board leaves for the mounter. 2D solder paste inspection is standard configuration on the A9, and can be extended to in-line 3D SPI where the line calls for it.

Steps 1–9 are the general stencil-printing sequence. The exact parameter values for your board come from your own paste, stencil thickness and aperture design — we do not publish a recipe that would be wrong for your product.

ARTICLE 01Your printer is not slow — your changeover is

Most printing capacity is lost between products, not during them. Three numbers tell you whether your constraint is the machine or the setup.

The article body is published in English so that the engineering detail stays exact. Titles and summaries are translated.

The squeegee crosses a 400 mm stencil in about two and a half seconds. In that window, the paste either rolls into every aperture and releases cleanly, or it does not — and nothing later in the line will notice until the board has been placed, reflowed and inspected.

Most printing problems are not printing problems. They are changeover problems. A line that prints 40 boards an hour and stops for 25 minutes between products is not slow because the printer is slow; it is slow because the printer spends the day being set up. The same is true of alignment: an operator who spends 40 seconds per board aligning by eye has built a throughput ceiling into the process that no squeegee speed can lift.

Measure three numbers before you buy anything. First, changeover time from last board of product A to first good board of product B. Second, alignment time per board. Third, how many boards per shift are reprinted because the deposit was wrong. If changeover plus alignment is more than a fifth of the shift, your constraint is setup, not printing.

That measurement is what decides the machine. If setup dominates, you want an automatic printer with stored recipes and fiducial recognition — the A9 changes product in under 5 minutes and aligns with a CCD camera rather than a hand. If alignment time is small and volume is low, a semi-automatic machine like the SP-1200 removes the capital cost without touching the constraint, because the constraint was never the print stroke.

Re-measure after installation, using the same three numbers. Printers drift: a squeegee blade wears, a stencil clogs, a support pin gets moved and never put back. A monthly check of changeover time and reprint count catches that drift while it is still a number on a sheet rather than a shipment of scrap.

Write down your three numbers — changeover, alignment, reprint rate — and send them to us with your board size. They describe your printing problem more precisely than any specification table, and they are what we will actually use to recommend a machine.

ARTICLE 02The 0.35 mm number nobody asks about

The smallest gap between adjacent paste apertures decides whether your next board can be printed at all. It takes ten minutes to check.

The article body is published in English so that the engineering detail stays exact. Titles and summaries are translated.

There is a number in the SP series specification that almost nobody asks about, and it decides whether your next product can be printed on the machine at all: the minimum distance between adjacent pads.

The specification sheet quotes 0.35 mm. On a board with generous spacing that number is irrelevant and you will never think about it. On a dense board — a fine-pitch QFN, a 0402 array, a board where the designer has packed components to the edge of the process — 0.35 mm is the difference between a print that releases cleanly and one where the paste bridges between two pads and stays bridged through reflow.

The check takes ten minutes and should happen before you specify a machine. Take your densest board. Measure the smallest gap between two adjacent paste apertures — not between components, between apertures, because that is what the paste sees. Compare it with the machine's minimum distance figure. Then do the same for the board you plan to build in two years, because that one is usually denser.

If your densest gap is inside the machine's limit, the answer is not a different squeegee. It is a different combination of stencil thickness, aperture design and separation speed, and on the A9 those are adjustable in ways the SP series does not offer: separation speed from 0.1 to 20 mm/s, squeegee speed from 6 to 200 mm/s, pressure from 0 to 15 kg, and a choice of 60°, 55° or 45° blade angle. The semi-automatic machines run a fixed print stroke with fewer knobs.

The number also moves. Stencil thickness changes it. Paste particle size changes it. A stencil that printed cleanly for a year can start bridging after a supplier changes the paste, and the first instinct is to blame the printer. Keep the aperture-gap measurement with the stencil, not with the machine, so you can tell the two apart.

If you are not sure whether your densest board is inside the limit, send us the aperture drawing and the board. We will tell you which machine holds it and which print parameters we would start from — and if the answer is that neither machine holds it as designed, we will say that too.

ARTICLE 03The stencil is the cheapest yield lever you own

A few hundred dollars of tooling against a few thousand a month of rework. Keep a record and you can rule the stencil in or out in a minute.

The article body is published in English so that the engineering detail stays exact. Titles and summaries are translated.

A stencil costs a few hundred dollars. A rework operator costs a few thousand a month. Between those two numbers sits most of the yield you are trying to recover, and the stencil is the one you can change this week.

Printing defects have a habit of being attributed to the machine. Paste bridging, insufficient deposit, smeared pads, clogged apertures — the printer is standing right there, so the printer gets the blame. In practice a large share of these trace back to the stencil: worn tension, a bent aperture, a thickness chosen for the last product rather than this one, or cleaning that has stopped being effective.

Build a stencil record and keep it with the stencil. Record the frame size, the thickness, the aperture design revision, the tension when new, the date it entered service, and the last date it was inspected. Then add one field: the last time it was cleaned. That single sheet turns 'the printer is misbehaving' into 'this stencil has been in service for fourteen months and its tension has dropped'. The A9 specification lists a stencil inspection system as an available option for exactly this reason — it checks the stencil before it enters the machine.

Pair the record with a cleaning discipline that actually happens. The A9 offers programmable dry, wet and vacuum cleaning, which means the stencil is cleaned on a schedule rather than when someone remembers. On the SP series cleaning is manual, so put it in the work instruction with a frequency attached and count it as part of the cycle rather than as an interruption to it.

Review the record when a defect appears, not when a defect becomes a pattern. The value of a stencil log is that it lets you rule the stencil out in a minute — and the minute you save is the one where you would otherwise have adjusted the squeegee pressure and created a second problem on top of the first.

Start the log with the stencils you are running today. If you want a starting point, ask us for the stencil frame sizes and thicknesses we quote for the A9 and the SP series, and put those at the top of the sheet.

ARTICLE 04Semi-automatic or fully automatic: a case you can defend to your CFO

Argue the decision in the currency your approver already tracks — operator hours removed, divided into the investment.

The article body is published in English so that the engineering detail stays exact. Titles and summaries are translated.

Somebody in your company has to sign the purchase order, and that person does not print boards. They will ask a simple question — why this machine rather than the cheaper one — and the answer needs to survive a budget review.

Most printing proposals fail at that point because they are argued in the wrong currency. Accuracy in micrometres, cycle time in seconds, camera resolution — none of it maps onto anything the approver measures. Meanwhile the cheaper semi-automatic machine looks like the safe answer, because the cost of the automatic machine is visible and the cost of the constraint it removes is not.

Translate the decision into three lines the approver already tracks. Line one: how many operator hours per month go into printing, alignment and reprint today. Line two: how many of those hours the machine removes. Line three: the investment divided by the monthly value of the hours removed. That is a payback period in months, expressed in the same units as every other capital request in the building.

The two machines answer that calculation differently, which is the point. A semi-automatic printer removes the print stroke from the operator's hands but not the loading and alignment, so it saves a fraction of the hours at a fraction of the cost. A fully automatic printer removes loading, alignment, cleaning and inspection as well, and changes product in under 5 minutes — it saves far more hours, and it costs more. Which one wins depends on your volume, and the calculation will tell you.

Recalculate every year. Payback is not a property of the machine; it is a property of the machine in your factory at your volume. A line that doubles its volume makes the automatic machine look better, and a line that moves to low-volume high-mix work makes the semi-automatic machine look better. The number moves, and the right time to notice is the annual review rather than the next breakdown.

There is a calculator further up this page that runs exactly this arithmetic on your own figures. Put your numbers in, then send them to us with your board size and we will tell you honestly whether the machine you are considering is the one that wins the calculation.

ARTICLE 05Speed, pressure, angle: three numbers that decide the deposit

Set them in the wrong order and you get a machine that prints well on Monday and badly on Thursday with no record of what changed.

The article body is published in English so that the engineering detail stays exact. Titles and summaries are translated.

Three settings decide the deposit on every pad: how fast the squeegee travels, how hard it presses, and at what angle the blade meets the stencil. Everything else on the machine exists to hold those three constant.

Teams usually adjust them in the wrong order and for the wrong reason. Pressure goes up because a pad looks light. Speed goes up because the line needs more boards. Angle gets changed by whoever reassembled the head last, and nobody records it. The result is a machine that prints acceptably on Monday and mysteriously badly on Thursday, with no record of what changed.

Fix the order. Set the angle first and leave it alone — it is a mechanical setup, not a running parameter, and the A9 offers 60°, 55° and 45° as distinct configurations. Set the speed from the paste manufacturer's data rather than from the line's appetite, and treat any change as a process change. Set pressure last and as low as the board allows, because excess pressure is what wears blades, bows stencils and drives paste under the stencil.

Then write down what you set, and require anyone who changes it to write that down too. The A9 quotes squeegee speed from 6 to 200 mm/s, pressure from 0 to 15 kg and separation from 0.1 to 20 mm/s — a wide enough window that two operators can set the same machine completely differently and both believe they are right. The semi-automatic machines give the operator fewer knobs, which is a real advantage when the workforce turns over.

Review the three settings against the deposit, not against the calendar. A paste lot change, a stencil change or a board revision is a reason to check them. A Monday morning is not. Parameter drift is slow and boring, and the only defence is a record of what the settings were when the process was good.

Take a photograph of the control panel when the process is running well and keep it with the stencil. It sounds crude, and it is the fastest way to answer 'what did we change?' — which is the question you will actually be asking at the time.

ARTICLE 06When to stop hiring operators and start buying a printer

Adding a fourth operator to a manual printing station builds a permanent monthly cost into a line that could have been capital.

The article body is published in English so that the engineering detail stays exact. Titles and summaries are translated.

You can add an operator to a printing station and get more boards out of it. You can do that three times. The fourth time, you have built a manual line with a printer attached to it, and the cost of the constraint is now permanent payroll.

Printing scales with people in a way that placement does not, because the loading, the alignment and the cleaning are all manual on a semi-automatic machine. Each additional operator adds a fixed amount of throughput and a permanent monthly cost. The line looks flexible and is, in fact, a fixed commitment with no residual value — you cannot resell the training or depreciate the habit.

Set a trigger before you need it. A workable one: when printing and reprint together account for more than two operator positions per shift, or when changeover between products exceeds 15 minutes, the manual approach has stopped being cheaper and started being structural. Those are the two numbers to watch, because they are the two that a fully automatic printer attacks directly.

At that point the comparison changes shape. An automatic printer is capital, depreciates, has residual value and does not appear in next year's payroll. It changes product in under 5 minutes, aligns with a camera rather than an eye, cleans on a schedule and inspects in 2D as standard. The A9 specification quotes cycle time under 7 seconds excluding printing and cleaning — the constraint it removes is not the print stroke but everything around it.

Re-check the trigger every year, in both directions. A line that drops from high-volume to high-mix may genuinely be better served by a semi-automatic machine, and it would be a mistake to keep buying automation because it was the right answer two years ago. The trigger is a measurement, not a policy.

If you are close to the trigger and not sure which side of it you are on, send us your operator count per shift and your changeover time. That is enough to give you a defensible answer, and it takes less time than the meeting where you would otherwise argue about it.

ARTICLE 07Cleaning is where your yield disappears quietly

A stencil that is not cleaned does not stop printing. It prints slightly worse until an aperture blocks and a pad goes down empty.

The article body is published in English so that the engineering detail stays exact. Titles and summaries are translated.

Cleaning is the step everyone agrees is important, nobody measures, and every printer has been blamed for at least once. It is also the step where the most yield disappears quietly, because its failure mode is gradual rather than dramatic.

A stencil that is not cleaned properly does not stop printing. It prints slightly worse, then slightly worse again, until an aperture is fully blocked and a pad goes down with no paste on it at all. By then the shift has produced a few hundred boards in the degraded window, and the question is not how to fix the printer but how many boards to quarantine.

Put cleaning on a schedule and give it a completion test. The schedule is a frequency tied to boards printed or to elapsed time, whichever comes first. The completion test is visual: hold the stencil to a light source and confirm you can see through every aperture. Both take less time than the investigation that follows a blocked aperture.

The machines differ here in a way that matters. The A9 specification lists a programmable cleaning system with dry, wet and vacuum modes, so the interval is a setting rather than a habit, and the machine does it between boards without being asked. The SP series is cleaned by the operator, which is entirely adequate at low volume and becomes the first thing to slip when volume rises. Neither is wrong; they are priced differently because they demand different amounts of attention.

Log the cleaning with the stencil, alongside its tension and its aperture revision. When a bridging defect appears, the log tells you in seconds whether cleaning has been happening on schedule — and if it has, you can move on to paste, pressure and separation instead of re-arguing a step you already control.

If you want to know which cleaning configuration suits your stencil type and volume, tell us the stencil material and how many boards you print per shift. That determines whether programmable dry, wet and vacuum cleaning earns its place on your machine or is money you do not need to spend.

ARTICLE 08Nine questions to ask before you sign for a screen printer

Seven of them are specification questions. Two of them decide whether the machine still prints well in year three.

The article body is published in English so that the engineering detail stays exact. Titles and summaries are translated.

Buying a screen printer is a decision you will live with for a decade, made in a week, usually against a deadline. Nine questions, asked before you sign, remove most of the ways that goes wrong.

One: what is the smallest gap between adjacent apertures on the densest board you will run in the next three years? Two: what is your changeover time today, measured from last good board of product A to first good board of product B? Three: how many boards per shift get reprinted because of the deposit? Four: who will set the print parameters, and what record will they leave? Five: what stencil frame size do you need, and does the machine you are considering accept it?

Six: does the machine take your board at all — length, width and thickness? Seven: what utilities does it need, and does your floor have them where the machine will stand? Eight: what does a squeegee blade, a blade holder and a stencil frame cost, and how long do they take to arrive? Nine: who trains your operators, and what happens on the first day the process drifts after the trainer has gone?

Ask these against a specific machine and the answers sort themselves into two piles. Questions one to seven are specification questions, and the documents on this page answer most of them. Questions eight and nine are support questions, and they are the ones that decide whether the machine is still producing good boards in year three. A supplier who answers the first pile and dodges the second is telling you something useful.

Write the answers down in one document and keep it. When a defect appears in eighteen months, that document is the baseline: it records what you bought, what it was supposed to do, and what you expected to pay for consumables. Without it, every future problem is a fresh argument with no reference point.

Bring the nine answers to us and we will work through them against the A9, the SP-1200 and the SP-300 — including the questions where the honest answer is that a different machine or a different supplier suits you better. That conversation costs you an email and is worth more than the specification tables it replaces.

14 — Questions we get asked

Before you ask us, check here

What is the difference between the A9 and the SP-1200?
The A9 is a fully automatic in-line vision printer: it loads, aligns, prints, cleans and inspects without an operator, and it changes product in under 5 minutes. The SP-1200 is a semi-automatic printer: an operator loads and aligns each board, and the machine handles the print stroke. The A9 is the right answer for continuous production; the SP-1200 is the right answer for long boards and low-volume work.
Can the SP-1200 really print a 1200 mm board?
That is what its specification sheet says, and 1200 mm is the machine's headline feature. But our three source documents quote different PCB sizes for the same family — 300 × 1200 mm on the SP-1200 sheet, 350 × 1200 mm on the SP-300 sheet and in the user manual. Ask us to confirm the size against the current drawing for your configuration before you order.
Do I need a fully automatic printer for a small line?
Usually not. A fully automatic printer earns its cost when printing is a bottleneck — when changeover time, alignment time or cleaning time is eating shifts. For NPI, low volume, or boards that will not fit an in-line machine, a semi-automatic printer does the job at a fraction of the capital cost.
What printing accuracy should I expect?
The A9 specification sheet quotes ± 0.015 mm printing accuracy at CPK > 2.0, and ± 0.007 mm repeat position accuracy, and states it can print 03015 components. The SP series is quoted at ± 0.02 mm to ± 0.05 mm depending on which document you read. Accuracy figures are only meaningful next to a CPK value — ask for it.
Does the printer come with the stencil?
The machine comes with its frame clamps and tooling; the stencil itself is made to your board and is quoted separately. Tell us your board size, aperture design and stencil thickness and we will quote the frame with the machine.
What paste should I use?
We do not recommend a paste — that decision belongs with your process engineer and your paste supplier, because it depends on your alloy, your profile and your reliability requirements. What we do is make sure the printer can hold the parameters your paste needs: the A9 spans 6–200 mm/s squeegee speed, 0–15 kg pressure and 0.1–20 mm/s separation.
How long does installation take?
The A9 installation manual documents the sequence: position, level, set transport height to 900 ± 40 mm, connect power and air, then set the first print program. On-site time depends on whether your boards and stencils are ready when the machine arrives. Have the stencil and the paste on site and it goes quickly.
Can you supply a complete SMT line, not just the printer?
Yes. We supply board loaders, printers, mounters, reflow ovens, AOI, SPI, stencil cleaning and storage, and the handling between them. Tell us your board, your volume and your existing equipment and we will propose a line rather than a machine.
