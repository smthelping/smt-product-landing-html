01 - Why custom tooling

A standard nozzle is built for the average part, and your problem is never the average part

Vacuum picks up flat, light, centred components. The parts that stop a line are none of those three things.

01

Suction stops working at the edge of the envelope

A part with an uneven top face cannot form the seal suction needs. A part that is too heavy slips under acceleration. A part with a fragile face is marked by a rigid tip. Those three failures account for most of the odd-form work still sitting on a manual bench.

02

The tool has to match the component, not the machine

A gripper is designed around the external profile of one part family. That is why the same head places a 0402 chip and then needs a different tool for a 9.5 mm bobbin. The machine is rarely the constraint; the interface to the part is.

03

The cost of not solving it never appears on the BOM

Manual odd-form placement is not a line item. It is an operator, a bench, a rework step and a first-pass yield that never quite reaches the target. The calculator below is built so that you can put your own numbers into it.

02 - Process

From a component sample to a validated tool, in nine steps

Every step below produces something you can look at. Nothing is quoted from a written description alone.

01

Receive the component

Send the part itself, ideally four to six inches of tape or a tube, or a drawing with the critical dimensions called out.

02

Measure what matters

Outside profile, the face the tool has to bear on, mass, centre of gravity and any lead or pin pattern.

03

Choose the handling method

Vacuum, friction jaws, a hybrid of the two, or a slot. The method follows the part, not the machine.

04

Model the jaw

CAD geometry with the chamfers and radii that let the part enter the jaws without being pushed off centre.

05

Check the structure

Thicker sections where the jaw sees the bending load during acceleration, so the head does not have to compensate for a flexible tool in software.

06

Machine and fit

Body and jaw are cut to the interface your head expects, so the tool seats and repeats at the same height every cycle.

07

Test on your component

Pick and place cycles on your own part, at production speed, with the pickup rate recorded and shipped with the tool.

08

Validate the material

Tungsten steel, ceramic, diamond steel or a soft tip, chosen against wear, static and how fragile the component is.

09

Ship with the numbers

The tool travels with its measurements and its test result, so your process engineer is not starting from zero.

Steps 2, 4 and 6 decide whether the tool works. A gripper that is geometrically right but structurally flexible will still lose parts at speed.

ARTICLE 01The Odd-Form Part Your Mounter Refuses to Place Is a Tooling Problem, Not a Machine Problem

When a placement head rejects a component, the reflex is to blame the machine or the component. Almost always it is the interface between them, and that interface is a part you can order.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

A mounter that refuses a component is not usually broken, and the component is not usually defective. Something in the interface between the two has run out of range, and the operator's response - take it to a bench - is the correct response to the wrong problem. The part never gets solved; it gets routed around.

The bench is where this becomes expensive. It has an operator, a workstation, a rework allowance and a first-pass yield figure that quietly absorbs the difference. None of that appears as a line called odd-form placement, so it survives every cost review. Meanwhile the machine that could have placed the part sits with an idle head and a nozzle it was never given.

The diagnostic question is not whether the machine can place the part but what the machine is trying to hold it by. If the answer is a flat top face, then the test is whether the part has a flat top face, whether it is light enough for the vacuum you have, and whether its face tolerates contact. Fail any one of those and the tooling specification is wrong, not the machine.

That reframes the purchase. Instead of a capital case for a new placement platform, it becomes a tooling case: one interface, cut to the profile of one part family, that converts a manual operation into a machine operation without touching the machine. Friction jaws, a slot, a hybrid of suction and jaws - the choice follows the geometry of the part, and the geometry is measurable.

The return keeps arriving because the tool is reusable. The same jaw runs the same part family across every board that contains it, and when the family is retired the tool is retired with it. What you are buying is not a fix for one board; it is the removal of a whole class of parts from the manual bench, and the bench shrinks accordingly.

Pick the single part that costs you the most manual time this month. Photograph it next to a ruler, measure its outside profile, weigh it, and send those three things with the machine model. That is enough to say whether a tool is possible and roughly what it would take. Everything else is a refinement of the same conversation.

ARTICLE 02Why Your Best Operator Is Still Feeding Connectors by Hand

The bench exists because the parts on it are the ones nobody designed a tool for. That is a procurement gap, and it is closable.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

Walk onto most EMS floors and there is a bench with connectors on it, and a person putting them on boards. Ask why and the answer is always the same: the machine cannot pick them. Ask who decided that, and the trail usually goes cold.

The trail goes cold because the decision was never made. Nobody evaluated the connector and rejected it; the connector arrived in a product the factory won a quote for, and the default handling method for a part with no tooling is a person. The cost is real and continuous, but it was never approved, so it is never reviewed.

The method that fixes this is a tooling audit rather than a machine audit. List every part that currently reaches a bench. For each one, record the seconds it takes, the number of boards per month that use it, and what stops the machine from picking it. Sort the list by total monthly seconds. The top three lines are your tooling programme, and they are usually not the parts anyone complains about.

Once the list exists, a tooling supplier can quote against it directly, because every line already carries the part and the volume. That is the difference between an open-ended request and a specification: one produces a price range, the other produces a geometry and a lead time. The bench does not disappear, but its contents stop being an accident.

The compounding effect is that the audit becomes a routine. Every new product introduction runs through the same list, and the question 'can this be placed?' gets asked before the first board is built rather than after the first complaint. Tooling becomes part of the process plan instead of a remedy for a problem.

Run the audit on one line only, and keep it to a single page. Total the manual seconds per month at the bottom. If the number surprises you, you now have both the business case and the part list in the same document, and you can send it as it stands.

ARTICLE 03How to Tell Whether an Odd-Form Component Can Be Placed Automatically

Six questions about the part itself that predict, before anyone quotes anything, whether a tool can pick it reliably.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

There is a version of this conversation that wastes months: a part goes back and forth between a factory and a supplier, samples are posted, and eventually someone says the part is not automatable. It is almost always decidable in ten minutes, before anyone quotes anything.

The waste comes from asking the wrong question first. 'Can this part be placed automatically?' sounds like a question about machines, so it gets sent to the machine supplier, who answers about machines. The real question is about the part's geometry and mass, and it can be answered at the bench by whoever is holding it.

Six things decide it. Does the part have a face the tool can bear on - flat for suction, two roughly parallel flanks for jaws? Is it light enough for the vacuum available, or heavy enough that jaws are needed? Will its surface mark under contact? Does it have leads or pins that need to enter holes, which means axial force? Is its centre of gravity near the middle, so it will not swing under acceleration? And does it arrive in a package a feeder can present? Four yeses and a tool is straightforward; the questions that come back no tell you what the tool has to solve.

The answers turn into a tooling specification almost mechanically. A flat face and low mass point at a vacuum nozzle. Parallel flanks and a heavy body point at friction jaws. A fragile face points at a soft tip. Leads and holes point at a force-controlled head with a rigid jaw. Package shape decides whether you also need a feeder, which is a separate conversation with the same supplier.

What this buys you over time is a decision you can make in-house. The six questions do not need a supplier on the call, so new parts get classified during process planning, and the ones that need tooling are identified while there is still time to order it. The parts that reach the bench are then the ones you deliberately chose to put there.

Take the part that has been sitting on the bench longest and answer the six questions in writing. If you end up with four or more in the 'yes' column, send the answers and a photograph to a tooling supplier and ask for a geometry. If you do not, you have saved yourself the sample postage.

ARTICLE 04Six Measurements to Take Before You Ask for a Custom Nozzle Quote

The measurements that decide the design, and the order to take them in. Arriving with these turns a two-week exchange into a single message.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

The single most common reason a custom nozzle quote takes two weeks is that it is missing one dimension, and the dimension is usually on the face nobody photographs - the one the tool has to bear on.

That dimension matters more than the outline because it decides the handling method. Get it wrong and the tool is designed for the wrong contact surface, which means the prototype fails at the pickup-rate test and the whole cycle restarts. The cost is not the tool; it is the two weeks.

Six measurements cover it. Outside profile in the two axes the jaws will close across. The height of the face the tool bears on. Mass, to the nearest gram. The position of the centre of gravity along the long axis, even roughly. The lead or pin pattern, if the part has one, including the spacing. And the package: tape width, tube, or tray. Taken in that order, each one narrows the design space for the next.

With those six numbers, a tool designer can propose a geometry without a sample in hand, which is the difference between a quote in days and a quote in weeks. The sample is still worth sending - a part in the post beats a dimensioned drawing for confirming the intangibles - but it stops being the thing the quote waits for.

The habit pays off at the second tool. Once the six measurements exist for a part family, a new variant in that family usually differs in two of them, and the drawing can be derived rather than designed. Repeat orders get faster because the measurement work was done once and written down.

Open a spreadsheet with six columns and one row per part on the manual bench. Fill in what you can measure today and leave the rest blank; the blanks are what you need a caliper or a scale for. When the row is full, the quote request writes itself.

ARTICLE 05What a Nozzle Actually Costs Over a Year, Not at the Counter

The purchase price is the smallest number in the calculation. Wear, pickup rate, downtime and the labour behind the bench are the ones that move.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

A nozzle is one of the cheapest things on a placement line and one of the few that can stop it. That asymmetry is why the counter price is the least useful number in the decision.

The costs that move are the ones around the tool. Wear changes the tip geometry, which changes the pickup rate, which produces misses that the operator has to clear - and clearing a miss costs more than the nozzle by an order of magnitude. A tool that oxidises and loses its seal behaves the same way. None of this is visible at the counter, and all of it is visible in the shift report.

The calculation that holds up has four terms. Tool cost per year, from price divided by expected life. Misses per thousand placements, times the cost of clearing one. Scheduled cleaning and inspection minutes, times the labour rate. And the occasional line stop when a tool fails mid-run, times what a stopped line costs per minute. Three of the four are numbers you already have.

Seen that way, the material decision stops being about price. Tungsten steel costs more and wears longer, which wins when the miss cost is high. Ceramic resists static and breaks, which wins when the part is static-sensitive and you can hold spares. Diamond steel takes both advantages and charges for them. Soft tips protect a delicate face and wear out fastest, so they belong on the parts that need them and nowhere else.

The return compounds because the four terms are all reduced by the same thing: a tool that matches the part. Higher pickup rate cuts misses, correct material cuts replacement, correct geometry cuts cleaning, and a spare on the shelf converts a failure into a changeover. Every one of those shows up in the same shift report that showed you the problem.

Take last month's shift report and count the misses on one part. Multiply by your cost of clearing one, then add the hours spent cleaning the tool. Compare that annual figure with the price of the tool, and you have the argument - built entirely from your own data, which is the only kind a finance team accepts.

ARTICLE 06Designing a Gripper Jaw Around a Component You Have Never Seen Before

What a drawing can and cannot tell a tool designer, and why a sample in the post is worth more than a dimensioned PDF.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

A drawing tells a tool designer the shape of a part and almost nothing about how it behaves. The gap between those two things is where most failed prototypes come from.

The gap is not carelessness. A dimensioned PDF describes the nominal geometry of a perfect part. The parts on a line have flash, moulding draft, a surface finish, a slightly off-centre mass, and a coating that may or may not be slippery. A jaw designed to the nominal shape fits the drawing and fights the part. The failure is invisible until the pickup-rate test.

The method that closes the gap is to treat the sample as primary evidence and the drawing as a cross-check. Measure the part in front of you. Note where the surface is smooth and where it catches. Try to slide it with your fingers, which tells you roughly how much friction a jaw will have to work with. Weigh it, and check whether it hangs level when suspended - that is the centre of gravity, and it decides whether the part will swing during acceleration.

Those observations translate directly into jaw geometry. A part that slides easily needs more jaw force or a rougher contact face. A part that hangs off-level needs the grip point moved toward the heavy end. A part with flash on one edge needs clearance there and contact somewhere else. This is why a sample in the post beats a PDF, and why the same supplier will ask for one even after reading your drawing.

Once a family is characterised, the knowledge is reusable. The second variant in the family arrives with the same surface behaviour and a different outline, so the jaw is adapted rather than invented. That is the difference between tooling as a project and tooling as a library.

Send both. Put four to six inches of tape in an envelope with the drawing, and mark on the drawing which face the machine should bear on. The two together answer questions neither answers alone, and the quote comes back with a geometry instead of a question.

ARTICLE 07Keeping a Custom Nozzle Alive: What to Inspect and When

A short inspection routine, an interval you can defend, and the two failure modes that a visual check will not catch.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

A custom nozzle fails in two ways. One is visible from across the room. The other one is not, and it is the one that quietly costs yield for weeks before anyone notices.

The visible failure is breakage - a cracked ceramic tip, a jaw that no longer closes square. The invisible one is dimensional drift: the tip has worn, the height has changed by a few hundredths of a millimetre, and the placement still looks fine in a sample. It shows up as an intermittent miss that gets blamed on the component, then on the feeder, then on the operator. The tool is the last thing anyone suspects and the first thing that should have been measured.

The routine that catches both is short. Check the tip under magnification for chips and build-up. Measure the seating height against the head datum, not against last week's reading. Close the jaws and check they meet squarely, then open them and check the gap. Look at the O-ring and the spring for compression set. Four checks, and the second one is the only one that needs a number.

Set the interval from the miss rate rather than from the calendar. If the pickup-rate test on a new tool gave you a baseline, run the same test at a fixed number of cycles and compare. When the number moves, the interval shortens. That converts maintenance from a schedule into a measurement, which is also what makes it defensible to a production manager.

The compounding benefit is a spare. A second tool on the shelf turns a tool failure from a line stop into a changeover, and the changeover is where you can afford to inspect the failed tool properly rather than swapping it under pressure. Budget the spare at the time of the first order, when the drawing already exists and the price is at its lowest.

Pick the tool that has been in service longest, measure its seating height today, and write the number on the tool's card along with the date. That single reading is the baseline every future comparison needs, and it takes four minutes.

ARTICLE 08Building the Business Case for Custom Tooling Without Guessing at the Numbers

How to assemble the labour, yield and downtime figures your finance team will accept, using numbers from your own line rather than a vendor's.

Full articles are in English. The summary above carries the argument in your language.

Every tooling proposal eventually meets a finance team, and the ones that survive are the ones built from the factory's own numbers rather than a supplier's projection.

The proposals that fail share a shape. They lead with a percentage - twenty per cent faster, ten per cent fewer defects - with no derivation. The finance team cannot check the number, so they discount it, and the discussion becomes a negotiation about credibility instead of about the investment. A supplier's projection cannot win that argument, however accurate it is, because it is not the factory's evidence.

The structure that works has three lines and a fourth. Labour released: operators on the operation, multiplied by the hours the tool gives back, at your wage rate. Yield: defects avoided per thousand placements, at your measured cost of rework - and rework costs more at each later stage, so use the stage where the defect was actually caught. Downtime: line stops avoided, at your cost per stopped minute. The fourth line is tooling investment, and it is the only one a supplier supplies.

What makes this credible is that three of the four numbers are already in your systems. The wage rate is on the payroll. The rework cost is in the quality data. The stopped-minute cost is usually known to the production manager to the nearest dollar. Assembling them takes an afternoon, and once assembled the same three lines support every tooling request after this one.

The return continues past the first approval because the model becomes a template. New parts run through the same three lines, and the comparison between tooling requests becomes a comparison between payback periods rather than between opinions. That is the point at which tooling stops being a purchase and becomes a programme with a budget.

Start with the labour line alone, because it is the easiest and usually the largest. Operators, hours returned, wage rate. If that single line does not justify the tool, the other three will not rescue it, and you have saved yourself the meeting. If it does, you already have the first third of the case written.

15 - Questions

The questions that come up before an order

What do you need from me to quote a custom nozzle?
The component itself, ideally four to six inches of tape or a tube, or a drawing with the outside profile and the face the tool bears on dimensioned. Photographs help but cannot replace a measurement.
Which machine brands can you build for?
Our nozzle line covers Yamaha, Fuji, JUKI, Samsung, Panasonic, Hitachi, I-pulse, Siemens and ASM, Universal, Mirae, Sanyo and Sony. If your head is not on that list, send the interface drawing and we will say whether we can cut it.
Can you place a part that is too heavy for vacuum?
Yes, with friction jaws. An odd-form head in this class applies up to 100 N of placement force and carries components up to 300 g, so the jaw takes the load and the vacuum seal stops being critical.
How long does a custom tool take?
Our own sheets disagree: the nozzle sheet publishes one week, and the design note says two to four weeks with about a month for a fully customised solution. Treat two to four weeks as the planning figure and ask us to confirm against the drawing for your part.
What material should the tool be made from?
Tungsten steel for wear, ceramic for static-sensitive parts, diamond steel when you need both and can accept the cost, and a rubber or silicone tip when the part face marks easily. Ceramic is fragile, so budget for spares if you choose it.
Will a custom tool void my machine warranty?
That depends on your machine supplier, not on us, and we will not guess. The tools are cut to the standard interface your head expects and need no machine modification, which is the usual condition, but confirm it with your supplier.
How do I know the tool works before it ships?
It is run on your own component at production speed, and the pickup rate from that test ships with the tool. You are not accepting an untested part.
