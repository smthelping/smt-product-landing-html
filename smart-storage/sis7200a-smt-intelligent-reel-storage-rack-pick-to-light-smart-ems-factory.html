How it works

Six steps from goods-in to the feeder

The same flow runs on the 7-inch SIS7200A rack, the 13-inch racks and the mobile trolleys. Steps 1 to 4 come straight from the published operation process; steps 5 and 6 from the delivery flow.

1

Scan the reel

A PDA or fixed scanner reads the reel label. New reels get a unique Reel ID label at goods-in, carrying part number, quantity, DayCode and LotCode.

Barcode printing and labelling are part of the software; an X-ray counter can supply the quantity.
2

Choose any empty slot

Scan any free slot, or let the system recommend one. Reels no longer need a fixed home, so the rack fills evenly and no slot sits reserved and empty.

Slot width can be re-adjusted for 7, 13 and 15 inch reels of different thickness.
3

The slot lights, the reel goes in

The chosen slot LED comes on. The operator puts the reel in, the light goes off and the slot address is saved against the Reel ID.

On sensor racks the slot detects the reel itself; on scan racks the operator confirms with a scan.
4

Next free slot is suggested

The adjacent empty slot lights for the next reel, so put-away runs as one continuous walk instead of a search.

Merge and split of storage locations is done by PDA scan.
5

Pick by light

The ERP or MES releases a work order. The system compares it against stock, builds the kit and lights every reel needed, each order in its own colour, oldest date code first.

Seven LED colours per slot; the tower light shows which rack to walk to.
6

Verify at the line

Each picked reel is scanned out and scanned again at the feeder station, so the material and the station are checked twice before the line starts.

Station tables can be imported from Excel; shortages raise an automatic notice to the warehouse.

Kitting5 min read

The Two-Hour Kit That Never Shows Up on the Line Report

Your placement machines are fast. The hour before they start is not. Most of that hour is someone walking a reel store looking for labels.

The line is waiting, and nobody logs why

Watch a high-mix SMT line at changeover. The machines are ready, the program is loaded, and the operators are waiting for a trolley of reels. The OEE report records setup time, but it rarely says that eighty minutes of that setup was a person in the warehouse reading part numbers off reel labels one shelf at a time.

Searching, not picking, is the real cost

In a fixed-location store, every part number has a home. Homes fill unevenly, overflow reels end up somewhere else, and partial reels come back to whatever space is free. The picker spends most of the job confirming where a reel is, not taking it. Add three work orders released together and the same aisle is walked three times. Southern Machinery's own solution document puts traditional kitting at about two hours per order.

Make the reel announce itself

Flip the job around. Instead of the person knowing where every reel lives, the rack knows, and tells the person by lighting the slot. The work order is compared against stock automatically, the kit list is built, and every reel in it lights. Several orders can run at once, each in its own colour. The picker's only decision is to take the lit reel.

What the SIS7200A does on a kitting run

The SIS7200A holds 1,400 seven-inch reels on a 2130 x 400 mm footprint, every slot with its own LED. Released orders light their reels in separate colours, oldest date code first, and the tower light shows which rack to walk to. Each reel is scanned out and scanned again at the feeder station. The published result for this setup is kitting from two hours to fifteen minutes, roughly eight times faster.

Measure it on your own floor

Before you buy anything, time three ordinary kits from release to the reels arriving at the line, and count how many minutes were spent finding rather than carrying. That single figure tells you more about payback than any brochure. Then repeat the measurement on the first week with lit slots, and keep the comparison as your baseline for the next line.

Inventory accuracy5 min read

Why Your Reel Count Never Matches the ERP

Stock is right in the system and wrong on the shelf, or the other way round. The gap opens at every hand-over that nobody scans.

Two days of counting, still not trusted

Many SMT plants close the reel store for a full stocktake twice a year and still find that the ERP and the shelf disagree the following week. Planners start padding safety stock, buyers reorder parts that were already in the building, and the line stops for reels that the system says are available.

Where the numbers drift

The drift does not come from the count. It comes from the moves between counts: a reel put back on the wrong shelf, a partial reel returned without its new quantity, a reel borrowed for an urgent line and never booked out. Each move is small, each relies on a person remembering to record it, and the errors pile up quietly.

Tie every reel to one slot address

If every reel has a unique ID and every slot has an address, each put-away and each pick becomes a single scan that links the two. Stock is then not something you count, it is something the rack already knows. A stocktake becomes a walk that compares what the system expects with what each slot holds.

How the intelligent rack keeps the record honest

On the SIS7200A, the slot lights for put-away and the Reel ID is bound to the slot the moment the reel goes in. Picks are lit, taken and scanned out. On sensor-type racks, a reel removed without an order, or put in a slot that was not lit, keeps the slot flashing until it is corrected. Southern Machinery's published case shows a full stocktake falling from two days to two hours.

Track the gap, not just the count

Pick twenty part numbers at random each week and compare the system quantity with the shelf. Record the mismatch rate. It is the most honest measure of whether your process is working, and it is the number to watch after any change you make to storage.

Error proofing4 min read

Right Feeder, Wrong Reel: How Mis-Picks Actually Happen

Most wrong-part defects are not made at the machine. They start in the warehouse, two hand-overs earlier.

A good board run with the wrong capacitor

A first-article inspection catches a 10 nF capacitor where a 100 nF belongs. The reel was correct on the kit list, correct on the trolley and wrong on the feeder. By the time it is found, a panel or a full lot needs rework.

Look-alike labels and tired eyes

Reels of the same package, the same supplier and nearly the same part number sit side by side in a fixed-location store. Under time pressure a picker matches the first six characters and moves on. Checking at the line relies on the same person, or the next one, reading the same label again.

Remove the choice instead of checking it harder

The safest pick is the one where there is nothing to choose. When only one slot is lit, the picker cannot take the neighbour by mistake. When the reel is scanned out against the order and scanned again against the feeder station, two independent checks stand between the warehouse and the board.

The checks built into the SIS7200A flow

Material, slot, work order and feeder station are bound automatically. The lit reel is scanned out of the rack; the station table travels with the kit to the feeder area for a second scan before production. Sensor racks add an alarm for any reel removed from an unlit slot. Southern Machinery describes the result as automatic verification across every delivery link.

Count the near-misses

Ask line operators to log every reel they reject at the feeder for being wrong. Those near-misses show where your current process leaks long before a defect reaches the customer. After the change, the same log tells you whether the leak is closed.

Date code control4 min read

First In, First Out for SMD Reels Without a Spreadsheet

FIFO is easy on a whiteboard and hard on a shelf with ten reels of the same part. Let the rack pick the oldest one.

Ten identical reels, one of them old

A part number arrives every month from the same supplier. By the end of the quarter there are ten reels of it in the store, and the one that came in first is behind the other nine. It will be found when it is already past the date your quality team wanted it used by.

FIFO depends on people reading dates

In most stores, FIFO depends on the picker reading a DayCode or LotCode on each reel and choosing the oldest. When parts are spread across a wide area, or when the reel is in an overflow shelf, that comparison simply does not happen. The same is true for partial reels, which should be used before a full reel is opened.

Let the system choose the reel, not the person

If each reel's date code and receipt time are captured at goods-in, the system can decide which reel to issue. The picker sees only one lit slot for that part number. FIFO becomes a property of the store rather than a rule people have to remember.

How the rack enforces it

The SIS7200A software calculates DayCode, LotCode and storage time for every reel and lights the oldest reel for each line item. It can also give partial reels priority, keeping tail stock down. Southern Machinery's solution document describes this as fully automatic screening without human intervention.

Check the age of what you issue

Once a month, list the reels issued to production and the age of each at issue. If the average age falls and the spread narrows, FIFO is working. If not, look for reels being stored outside the rack.

Floor space4 min read

Fitting 1,400 Reels Into Less Than One Square Metre

A reel store rarely runs out of shelves. It runs out of the right shelves in the right place.

The store is full, but half the shelves are empty

Warehouse managers often ask for more racking when the real problem is that fixed locations are reserved for parts that are not in stock this month, while busy parts overflow into aisles and on top of cabinets.

Fixed homes waste space twice

Fixed locations leave gaps whenever a part runs low, and they force overflow somewhere else whenever a part runs high. Both cost floor area. Both also cost time, because overflow reels are the ones nobody can find.

Any reel, any slot

When the system remembers where each reel is, reels no longer need a fixed home. Any free slot can take any reel that fits it, so the rack fills evenly. The space you need is driven by the number of reels you hold, not by the number of part numbers you might hold.

The numbers on the SIS7200A

Integration5 min read

Connecting a Reel Rack to SAP, Oracle or Your Own MES

Most rack projects stall on the interface, not the hardware. Here is how the data actually moves.

IT asks one question first

The warehouse team likes the lights. Then IT asks how the rack talks to the ERP, who owns the stock record, and what happens when the network drops. A good answer to those questions decides whether the project goes ahead.

Where the trouble usually is

Problems come from unclear ownership. If both the rack software and the ERP think they hold the stock record, they drift apart. If the interface is a proprietary protocol, every change needs the supplier. If the rack cannot report events back, the ERP is always a step behind.

Keep the rack as an executor

The clean design keeps the warehouse system as the owner of stock and treats the rack as a device that executes light commands and reports what happened. Commands and reports use open formats, so your own developers can work with them.

What the Southern Machinery interface provides

According to the Storage System API V3.1 manual, each rack behaves as a small web server using HTTP with JSON. The warehouse system sends commands such as light on, light off, standby, reboot and shutdown, with a slot index from 0 to 1399 and one of seven colours. The rack reports put-away and pick events back to a web service on your server, with optional token authentication. The warehouse software itself exchanges XML, CSV or Excel files, runs on Oracle, MS SQL or MySQL, and has been linked to SAP, Oracle and HopFlow systems in delivered projects.

Tail stock4 min read

Partial Reels: The Inventory That Quietly Eats Your Margin

Every changeover sends partial reels back to the store. Most plants have no idea how many they hold.

The shelf full of almost-empty reels

Open any SMT store and you will find a shelf or a box of partial reels. Some have a few hundred parts left, some a few thousand. Nobody is sure, so new reels are opened while the partials sit.

Why partials get ignored

Returning a partial reel properly means recounting it, relabelling it and putting it back where the system thinks it is. Under changeover pressure, that is the step that gets skipped. The reel goes back without a count, the system still shows the original quantity, and the next kit draws a full reel because nobody trusts the partial.

Count once, issue first

Count the partial reel when it comes back, record the real quantity, and let the system issue partials before full reels whenever they cover the order. That keeps the number of open reels small and the tail stock low.

How the rack software handles it

The SIS7200A software calculates quantities from the work order and adjusts the mix of full and partial reels to keep tail stock at an optimal level. An X-ray reel counter can be linked so the returned quantity is measured, not guessed. Southern Machinery's solution document states that this reduces the daily inventory workload.

Weigh the partial reel pile

For one month, record how many partial reels come back each day and how many are issued again. If the second number is far below the first, partials are being stored but not used.

Planning6 min read

Planning a Smart Reel Store: Racks, Trolleys and Layout

A reel store is a small project with a long life. Most of the decisions are made in the first drawing.

Where do you start?

Once a plant decides to move to lit racks, the questions arrive at once: how many racks, which reel sizes, fixed or mobile, wired or wireless, and what happens to the store during the change. Getting the first drawing right saves months later.

The common planning mistakes

Plants often size the store for today's reel count instead of next year's, forget 13 and 15 inch reels, place racks without thinking about the picking route, or leave the network until the racks arrive. Each mistake is cheap on paper and expensive on the floor.

Plan in four layers

Decide in this order: reel count by size, room layout and picking route, network and integration, then line-side movement. Each layer constrains the next, so working in order prevents rework.

Matching models to each layer

For 7-inch reels, the SIS7200A holds 1,400 per rack. For 13-inch reels, the 13-inch racks hold from 250 to 400 depending on version. For line-side kitting, the SIS5120A or SIR5000C trolleys carry 390 to 600 seven-inch reels; the SIR5000C runs on a 12 V battery over Wi-Fi. Fixed racks use TCP/IP with wired or wireless links, or RS-485 on the SIR7000B. The proposal drawings show an example room of 7400 x 4096 mm combining 7-inch double-sided and 13-inch single-sided racks. Southern Machinery offers free installation and training and about one month for a customised solution.

Review the store every six months

After go-live, check rack occupancy, kitting time and stock accuracy twice a year. If occupancy passes about 85%, add a rack before overflow starts again.

FAQ

Questions buyers ask before ordering

How many reels does one SIS7200A rack hold?

1,400 seven-inch reels thinner than 17 mm: 7 layers of 200 slots, used from both faces, on 2130 x 400 x 2000 mm. Slot width can be re-adjusted for thicker 7-inch reels or for 13 and 15 inch reels, which lowers the count.

Does it work without an ERP or MES?

Yes. The web-based warehouse software runs on its own with BOM and work-order import from Excel, CSV or XML. When you are ready, the same software links to SAP, Oracle, HopFlow or your own MES.

How does our MES talk to the rack?

Over HTTP with JSON. Each rack behaves like a small web server: your system sends light-on, light-off, standby, reboot or shutdown commands with a slot index and colour, and the rack reports put-away and pick events back to your server. Token authentication is optional.

What is the difference between sensor and scan racks?

A sensor (inductive) rack detects a reel arriving in or leaving a slot and alarms on any unexpected change. A scan rack relies on the operator scanning the slot to confirm. Both light the slot the same way.

Can it handle MSD and date-code control?

DayCode, LotCode and storage time are calculated per reel, so the oldest reel is lit first and reels near their limit can be flagged. Humidity-controlled storage for moisture-sensitive parts is a separate cabinet we can quote alongside.
