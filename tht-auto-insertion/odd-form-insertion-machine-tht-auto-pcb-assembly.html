The real problem

Odd-form parts are the last manual island on your THT line.

Insertion machines handle axial and radial parts. Reflow and wave soldering handle the joints. But relays, transformers, connectors, big capacitors and coils still land on a bench with a tray, a fixture and a pair of hands. Every one of those parts is a different package, a different lead condition and a different shift-dependent output.

Pain 01

Manual insertion is slow and shift-dependent.

Skilled operators carry the whole odd-form workload, and output moves with fatigue, shift patterns and staff turnover.

What changes: automated insertion with customer-specific feeder and gripper tooling delivers a repeatable cycle you can schedule against.
Pain 02

Heavy parts float before soldering.

When leads are not secured, large components tilt or lift during wave soldering and turn into rework, touch-up labour and hidden quality risk.

What changes: the optional clinching unit bends leads under the board. Catalog guidance: lead diameter under 1.0 mm and exposed lead of 2 mm or more, subject to final design review.
Pain 03

Packaging is inconsistent across the BOM.

The same board may need tape, tube, tray and bulk parts. Manual handling is the only thing that currently bridges those formats.

What changes: radial tape, vertical taping, horizontal tape, tube, bowl and tray feeders are matched item by item against your BOM.
Pain 04

Changeover eats engineering hours.

High-mix EMS work means new assemblies every week, and every new odd-form part restarts the setup conversation.

What changes: Windows-based operation, vision programming and quick-plug clamp replacement shorten the path from new job to first good board.
Pain 05

Bent pins damage the PCB holes.

Forcing a misaligned component into a plated hole creates scrap and defects that inspection may only catch after soldering.

What changes: a top mark CCD and four bottom component CCDs align board and part visually before contact, and check the insertion result.
Pain 06

Labour cost is the least stable number you own.

Wage inflation, overtime and recruitment make odd-form cost hard to forecast, so quoting becomes conservative and margins shrink.

What changes: the ROI model compares your current manual standard against automated insertion using your own BOM, shift and labour data.
The process

Six steps from magazine to clinched board.

One operator loads the line. The machine repeats the same sequence for every board, every shift, every order.

01

Board in

Chain or belt conveyor accepts boards 70 × 70 up to 450 × 400 mm, left-to-right or right-to-left.

02

Clamp and align

Servo top plate or cylinder clamping fixes the board; the top mark CCD reads the fiducials.

03

Feed

The matched feeder presents the component; bottom CCDs verify body and lead position.

04

Insert

Up to four heads insert at any angle from 0 to 360 degrees, at up to 98 N insertion force.

05

Clinch

The S7900IV-W clinching unit bends the leads under the board to hold the part for soldering.

06

Board out

The board leaves ready for wave or selective soldering, with the odd-form parts already located.

The machine

Flexible odd-form automation, not a one-part special.

The S7900 is a platform. One machine covers multiple components, feeder types and insertion requirements - and each capability below is written as feature, technical meaning and what it does for your line.

01Six feeder families

Radial tape, vertical taping 90 degrees, horizontal tape, tube, bowl and tray feeders.

Advantage: packaging format stops dictating whether a part can be automated.

Benefit: fewer parts stay on the manual bench, so headcount planning gets simpler.

02Four insertion heads

S7900IV and S7900IV-W both list four insertion heads in the specification.

Advantage: several components are placed in one pass over the board instead of one at a time.

Benefit: higher output per square metre of floor space, without buying a second machine.

03Vision-guided insertion

One top mark CCD plus four bottom component CCDs.

Advantage: the PCB and the component are aligned visually before any contact happens.

Benefit: less hole damage and fewer bent leads, which shows up as lower scrap and less touch-up.

04Lead clinching option

Standard configuration on S7900IV-W; the catalog notes automatic lead bending to prevent floating during welding.

Advantage: components are mechanically held to the board before the soldering process starts.

Benefit: fewer tilted or lifted parts, fewer rework hours, more stable wave soldering quality.

05Any direction, nozzle or clamp

Insertion direction 0-360 degrees, component pickup by nozzle or clamp.

Advantage: asymmetric bodies and non-standard lead patterns do not need a custom orientation fixture.

Benefit: one platform absorbs the parts that used to require special tooling decisions.

06Fast job setup

Windows operating system, vision programming, quick-plug clamp replacement.

Advantage: new assemblies are taught on screen instead of rebuilt mechanically.

Benefit: high-mix changeover is measured in minutes, which protects your delivery promise.

Note 01 · Labour

Why Your Odd-Form Bench Still Runs on Human Hands

The last manual island on a modern THT line is not a technology gap. It is a decision nobody has forced yet.

The moment

Walk the line at 2 a.m. and count who is still working. The placement machines are done, the reflow oven is cycling, the AOI is scanning. And at the end of the line a tray of relays is waiting for hands.

The real problem

Odd-form parts stay manual because every part looks like an exception. A relay is not a resistor. A transformer has a body, a footprint and a lead pattern that no standard feeder was designed for. So each new assembly adds a new exception, and exceptions accumulate into a permanent bench.

The cost is not only the wage. It is the variance: two operators on two shifts produce two different insertion rates, two different defect profiles and two different explanations when a board fails downstream.

The method

Stop treating odd-form as a category and start treating it as a data problem. List every non-standard through-hole part on your highest-volume assemblies, then record four things for each: package type, body size, lead geometry and how it currently arrives. That list is the actual scope of the automation project - and it is almost always shorter than the fear suggests.

The machine

An odd form insertion machine for THT auto PCB assembly exists to absorb exactly that list. The S7900 pairs radial tape, vertical taping, horizontal tape, tube, bowl and tray feeders with four insertion heads, so the packaging format of each line item stops deciding whether it can be automated.

The repeat cycle

The improvement only holds if the list stays alive. Review it at every new product introduction, add the part, match the feeder, and the bench shrinks instead of growing back.

Your next step

Export the through-hole BOM lines from your three highest-volume boards, photograph the packaging of each odd-form part, and send them for a feasibility review. That is a one-hour task with a same-week answer.

Note 02 · Cost

The Real Cost of Manual Relay and Connector Insertion

Your quotation assumes a rate that only exists on the best shift with the best operator on the easiest part.

The moment

Someone asks why the margin on a mature product has quietly disappeared, even though material prices have not moved.

The real problem

Manual insertion cost is usually booked as a single labour rate. In reality it carries four hidden lines: the base wage, the rework triggered by tilted or lifted parts, the scrap from damaged plated holes, and the opportunity cost of a skilled operator doing unskilled motion all day.

None of those lines appear on a standard cost sheet, which is why the decision to automate is so often postponed.

The method

Build the comparison from your own numbers, not a supplier's. Take one component family, measure the true cycle time over a full shift, count the operators involved, log the rework minutes and the scrap pieces for two weeks, then divide by good boards shipped. That single figure is the number worth comparing against an automated insertion cycle.

The machine

This is where published reference data becomes useful. One Southern Machinery example compares manual relay insertion at around 1000 pieces per hour with an S7900IV at around 4700 pieces per hour on the same family, and states a labour equivalence of about five workers. Treat those as a benchmark to beat with your own data, not as a promise.

The repeat cycle

Re-run the same measurement ninety days after installation. Machines drift less than people, but product mix changes, so the comparison has to be refreshed to stay honest.

Note 03 · Method

A Six-Step Method to Automate Almost Any Through-Hole Part

Most failed automation projects were not engineering failures. They were scoping failures.

The moment

A project dies in the sample stage because the machine "cannot do this part" - and nobody wrote down which parts, exactly, it was supposed to do.

The real problem

Teams quote an insertion machine against a general ambition, not a defined part list. Without a part list there is no acceptance criterion, so the project can neither succeed clearly nor fail clearly.

The method

Run these six steps before you ask for a price:

  • One - Inventory. List every non-standard through-hole part and its annual volume.
  • Two - Package. Record the packaging format of each part as received, with a photo.
  • Three - Geometry. Capture body size, height, lead diameter, lead length and required orientation.
  • Four - Board data. Share Gerber or CAD files, panel layout, PCB thickness and board weight.
  • Five - Process. State whether clinching is required, and what happens after insertion: wave, selective or hand solder.
  • Six - Acceptance. Agree the target cycle time, accuracy and defect definition in writing.

The machine

The S7900 platform is designed to be specified against that list: feeder per package, nozzle or clamp pickup per body shape, head count per volume, clinching per downstream process. Every choice maps back to a line in your inventory sheet.

The repeat cycle

Turn the six steps into a template. The second automation project then starts from a form instead of a blank page.

Note 04 · Machine

Putting the Method on the Line: Inside the S7900 Platform

A specification table is only useful when you can translate each row into a decision on your own floor.

The moment

Two models, a long parameter list, and a purchasing decision that has to be defended for the next five years.

The real problem

Buyers compare headline numbers - heads, speed, accuracy - and ignore the rows that actually decide whether the machine fits: component height, board weight, conveyor type and whether clinching is included at all.

The method

Read the table in four passes. First the envelope: PCB size from 70 × 70 up to 450 × 400 mm, thickness 0.6-2.0 mm, board weight up to 5 kg. Second the payload: component body up to 50 mm diameter and height up to 25 mm on the W model, 30 mm on the standard model. Third the mechanics: chain conveyor on S7900IV, belt conveyor on S7900IV-W, and clinching as standard only on the W model. The machine

Those four passes answer the practical questions. If your boards are heavy and your parts are large, the W model earns its larger footprint. If you need pure insertion speed without lead bending, the standard model is the faster cycle at 0.7 seconds per component.

The repeat cycle

Keep the four-pass reading habit for every machine you evaluate afterwards - spares, feeders, loaders. The pattern holds across the whole line.

Note 05 · Quality

The Defects Manual Insertion Leaves Behind

The failure usually shows up after soldering, which is the most expensive place to find it.

The moment

A board fails at final test, and the root cause traces back to a component that was never sitting flat.

The real problem

Manual insertion has three characteristic defects. Leads get bent on the way in, damaging the plated barrel. Heavy parts lift or tilt before soldering because nothing holds them. And orientation errors slip through because the check is human and intermittent.

All three are latent. They pass visual inspection, travel through the oven, and only declare themselves at test - after the value has already been added.

The method

Move the check upstream. Verify alignment before contact instead of after soldering, hold the part mechanically until the joint is formed, and confirm orientation on every cycle rather than at intervals.

The machine

The S7900 implements that method directly: a top mark CCD aligns the board, four bottom component CCDs verify the part, and the S7900IV-W clinching unit bends the leads under the board so the component cannot move during wave soldering. An insertion direction of 0 to 360 degrees removes the orientation guesswork that asymmetric bodies otherwise create.

The repeat cycle

Track first-pass yield on odd-form assemblies as its own metric. If it stops improving, the part list has outgrown the current tooling, and that is the signal to add a feeder.

Your next step

Ask your test engineer which defect codes trace back to odd-form components in the last quarter. That ranking tells you where to start.

Note 06 · Flexibility

Automating Odd-Form Without Losing High-Mix Flexibility

The fear is real: automation that only pays on one product is not automation, it is a fixture.

The moment

A high-mix factory rejects an insertion machine because the products change every three days.

The real problem

That objection comes from experience with hard-tooled automation, where changeover meant mechanical rebuilding. Modern flexible insertion is software-led: the part is taught on screen, the gripper is swapped as a plug-in item, and the feeder stays where it is.

The method

Separate the two costs of changeover. Tooling change is physical and bounded. Program change is digital and near-instant. A high-mix machine is one where the physical part is small, standardised and quick.

The machine

S7900 runs on Windows with vision programming and quick-plug clamp replacement. Feeders are configured per component family rather than per board, so switching assemblies re-uses the same hardware set. Conveyor direction can be set left-to-right or right-to-left, which matters when a line is rebalanced.

The repeat cycle

Standardise the tooling library and treat it as an asset. Every new product that re-uses an existing gripper is a changeover that costs almost nothing.

Your next step

Count how many distinct grippers and feeders your top twenty assemblies would actually need. The number is usually far smaller than the product count.

Note 07 · Integration

Where the Odd-Form Machine Sits in a THT Line

Sequence matters more than specification. The best machine in the wrong position is a bottleneck.

The moment

The machine arrives, runs beautifully in isolation, and then starves or blocks the rest of the line.

The real problem

Odd-form insertion has a different cycle profile from axial and radial insertion. Axial parts place fast with simple motion. Odd-form parts place slower, with vision checks and often lead clinching. Put them in the wrong order and the slower step paces the line.

The method

Design the line around the slowest step. Board handling first: loader, then insertion machines, then soldering, then unloader. Keep board transfer height consistent, and decide conveyor direction before ordering anything.

The machine

S7900 accepts boards from 70 × 70 up to 450 × 400 mm at a conveyor height of 750 or 900 mm depending on model, matching common THT equipment. It supports both conveyor directions, so it can sit before or after a radial machine without redesigning the transfer.

The repeat cycle

Draw the line layout once and keep it as the reference document. Every future purchase gets checked against it, which prevents small mismatches from accumulating.

Your next step

Sketch your current THT flow with cycle times written on each station. Send the sketch with your component list for a line-layout review.

Note 08 · Project

From Inquiry to Ramp-Up: What the Project Actually Looks Like

Most of the risk in an automation project lives in the four weeks before the machine is ordered.

The moment

A budget is approved, a supplier is chosen, and someone asks the only question that matters: what happens next?

The real problem

Buyers focus on the machine and underestimate the preparation. Feeders, grippers and nozzles are designed against real samples, and a missing sample at the right moment can delay a delivery by weeks.

The method

Work backwards from the target ramp-up date and assemble the technical package early: PCB data, BOM, component drawings, packaging photographs, target throughput, current defect points and the downstream soldering process.

The machine

A typical S7900 project runs through feasibility on samples, tooling design, machine build, factory acceptance, shipping, installation with operator training, and a supported ramp-up period. The repeat cycle

After ramp-up, keep a short monthly review for the first quarter. Early issues are cheap; the same issues in month six are expensive.

Questions

Common questions from EMS engineers.

Answers follow the Southern Machinery technical file for the odd form insertion machine, with uncertain items flagged for confirmation during quotation.

Which components can an odd form insertion machine place?
It is designed for odd-form and through-hole parts such as relays, transformers, connectors, large capacitors, coils and fuses. Final feasibility depends on package, lead geometry, board layout and sample testing.
Can it handle different component packaging?
Yes. Radial tape, vertical taping at 90 degrees, horizontal tape, tube, bowl and tray feeders are all available. Feeder choice is configured after reviewing your BOM and component packaging.
Does the machine stop components floating before wave soldering?
The S7900IV-W includes a lead clinching unit as standard configuration. The technical file notes automatic lead bending to help prevent floating during welding. Final clinching design depends on lead diameter, exposed lead length and PCB layout.
What is the typical insertion speed?
The specification lists 0.7 seconds per component for S7900IV and 2 seconds with bent feet for S7900IV-W. Real output depends on component type, feeder, clinching requirement and board routing.
How accurate is the placement?
S7900IV is specified at ±0.06 mm and S7900IV-W at ±0.05 mm, both with a CPK of at least 1.0 in the source table.
What data should we send for evaluation?
Send PCB Gerber or CAD data, BOM, component drawings, photographs of packaging, target throughput, your current manual process and any wave soldering or clinching constraints. Samples are recommended before final confirmation.
Can it connect to a full THT line?
Yes. The machine supports left-to-right and right-to-left conveyor direction and can be paired with PCB loaders, unloaders and downstream soldering equipment. The exact layout needs line-design confirmation.
Is the return on investment guaranteed?
No generic ROI figure should be treated as a guarantee. Published examples are reference points only. Southern Machinery validates ROI case by case using your labour cost, shifts, volume, component mix and final configuration.
